Dead Men on Thrones.

Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Night of St. Bartholomew.

Admiral Gaspard de Coligny was already wounded when the men came for him. Two days earlier, on August 22nd, fifteen seventy-two, a shot fired from a window near the Louvre had struck him in the street, shattering a finger and tearing through his arm. And he had spent the days since recovering in his lodgings under royal protection while the court he'd only recently reconciled with debated what to do about the fact that someone had just tried to kill the most powerful Protestant in France. In the early hours of August 24th, the debate ended. A group of armed men led by Charles de Louviers, sieur d'Angoumois, broke into Coligny's rooms, ran him through, and threw his body from a window into the courtyard below, where it was mutilated and dragged through the streets by a crowd that had gathered, by then, expecting exactly this. The feast day the killing fell on gave the massacre its name. Nothing about what happened next was contained by the calendar.

The killing was not the war's beginning. France had been tearing itself apart over religion for a decade already, in a conflict historians bracket from the Massacre of Vassy in fifteen sixty-two, when Catholic troops killed a congregation of Huguenot worshippers, to the Edict of Nantes in fifteen ninety-eight, when a king who had once been a Protestant himself finally bought his exhausted kingdom something like peace. Thirty-six years, eight separate wars, a string of treaties that never held for long. The gathering in Paris that had put Coligny and much of Protestant France's nobility within reach of the crown's soldiers was itself meant to be the latest and most ambitious of those treaties: a royal wedding, six days earlier, marrying the Catholic king's sister to Henry, the Protestant king of Navarre, built specifically to bind the two warring faiths into one family. It did not survive the week.

The distance from that single killing to what followed cannot be measured in inches or intentions, because historians still disagree about how much distance there actually was. What seems clearest is this: Catherine de Medici, the queen mother, and her son King Charles IX had spent two days weighing what to do about a man whose influence over the young king, and whose appetite for pulling France into open war with Spain on the Netherlands' behalf, frightened them both. Sometime on the night of August 23rd, in a council meeting whose exact deliberations no surviving record fully captures, they settled on killing Coligny and a small circle of Huguenot leaders gathered in Paris for a royal wedding meant to seal peace between Catholic and Protestant France, not the city's entire Protestant population. That much most historians now accept. What they don't agree on is how much further Charles and his mother meant to go, or whether the wider massacre that engulfed Paris within hours of Coligny's death was ever something the crown decided rather than something the crown, having lit the match, simply failed to put out. One recent, deeply researched account argues for a more direct royal hand in what followed than the older picture of a narrow plot spinning out of control. Others hold the older line. The sources that would settle it, mostly written afterward by people with every reason to assign or deflect blame, were never going to make the question easy.

By daybreak on August 24th, Catholic militia companies were moving through Paris with lists of Protestant households, and killing that had begun as a targeted strike against a handful of named men became, within hours, indiscriminate. Contemporary estimates for Paris alone run to roughly two or three thousand dead over the week that followed, men, women, and children pulled from houses that had been marked, killed in the streets, thrown into the Seine in numbers large enough that bodies were still being fished out of the river days later. The violence did not stay in Paris. Over the following weeks it spread to provincial cities, Lyon, Orleans, Rouen, Bordeaux, Toulouse among them, each with its own local score of Catholic and Protestant grievance that the news from Paris seemed to license settling all at once. Modern historians, working from incomplete parish and burial records across an entire kingdom, put the full national death toll somewhere between five and thirty thousand. That is not a rhetorical hedge. It is the actual range the evidence supports, and no single number inside it has managed to displace the others as the settled figure.

King Charles IX's own responsibility for that spreading violence is, like his mother's, genuinely contested rather than simply unflattering to pin down. The clearest evidence shows a young king pressured for hours into authorizing the killing of Coligny's circle, not a monarch who woke that morning already commanding a citywide purge. Days later, the crown told the Parlement of Paris that the killings had preempted a Huguenot plot against the throne, a justification most historians read as cover assembled after the fact rather than a plan executed on schedule. Whether Charles bears more direct personal responsibility for what his militia did in his name over the following week is a live argument this book will not settle by picking the more dramatic version. What is certain is that a king who began the week trying to broker peace between his kingdom's two faiths ended it presiding over one of the worst outbreaks of religious violence sixteenth century Europe would see.

News of the massacre reached Rome within days, and Pope Gregory XIII's response left nothing to interpretation. He ordered a Te Deum, a hymn of thanksgiving, sung at the church of San Marco and declared a jubilee to mark the occasion. He had a commemorative medal struck, engraved with an angel bearing a cross and sword over the bodies of the fallen, inscribed simply Ugonottorum strages: slaughter of the Huguenots. And he commissioned Giorgio Vasari to commemorate the event in fresco, on the walls of the Sala Regia, the Vatican's grand ceremonial hall at the top of the staircase leading to the Sistine Chapel. Vasari painted three scenes rather than one: Coligny wounded in the street, Coligny's death and the massacre that followed it, and Charles IX himself explaining the killings before the assembled Parlement of Paris, the crown's cover story rendered permanently in plaster and pigment a few dozen feet from where the pope who commissioned it once said Mass.

The frescoes are still there. They have never been removed, painted over, or quietly retired to a storeroom the way an inconvenient monument sometimes is. They survive in the same room where they were unveiled in fifteen seventy-three, under the same ceiling, passed through by anyone who takes the Apostolic Palace's guided route rather than the standard tour that carries most visitors straight from the museums to the Sistine Chapel's crowded floor. Nobody has to explain them away, because almost nobody sees them at all.
