Dead Men on Thrones.

Chapter Twenty-Five: The Bankers and the Bonfire.

Before dawn on Friday, October 13th, thirteen oh seven, royal officials across the kingdom of France broke the seals on identical letters they had been ordered to open at exactly the same hour and not one moment before. King Philip IV had sent them out weeks in advance, sealed and silent, to seneschals and bailiffs in every region he controlled, with instructions that made the operation itself part of the point: strike everywhere at once, before any single Templar house could warn the next. In Paris, the king's own men broke into the Temple, the order's fortified headquarters on the right bank of the Seine, and arrested Jacques de Molay, the Grand Master, along with roughly sixty of his own knights. Across the rest of the country that same morning brought several hundred more arrests, enough that the day's full tally across France lands somewhere around six hundred, a number that survives in enough regional detail, Paris, La Rochelle, the south, to be taken seriously in a way the figure most people actually associate with that day is not. Fifteen thousand, or twenty thousand, gets repeated constantly in popular retellings. It is not a count of who was arrested on October 13th. It is, at best, a rough and heavily disputed guess at how many men had ever belonged to the Templars across the whole of Christendom at the order's peak, a figure serious historians place closer to seven thousand and treat with real caution even then. The single coordinated morning that actually broke the order was a fraction of that: a few hundred men, taken in their own beds, in a kingdom whose king had already decided what their confessions were going to say before a single one of them had spoken.

The date has outlived almost everything else about that morning in popular memory, mostly for a reason that has nothing to do with thirteen oh seven at all. Friday the thirteenth as a specifically unlucky day is not a medieval superstition. Fear of the number thirteen and fear of Friday both existed separately for centuries before Philip's raid, but nobody wrote them down together as one dreaded date until a nineteen oh seven American novel gave the combination its name. And the modern habit of blaming that superstition on the Templars is younger still, mostly a product of twentieth century conspiracy paperbacks and the thrillers that followed them. No chronicler in thirteen oh seven, or for six hundred years afterward, drew any connection between the day of the week and what had happened to the Templars. The connection was manufactured long after the fact and attached to a real atrocity because a real atrocity makes a better story than an invented one, which is its own small demonstration of how easily people prefer a myth with a date on it to history without a tidy hook.

Two centuries earlier, the order that fell that morning had begun as something considerably smaller and, on its own terms, considerably more sympathetic. Around eleven nineteen, the sources are vague enough that some historians put it a year or two either side, a French knight named Hugues de Payens and a handful of companions, Godfrey de Saint-Omer among them, approached King Baldwin II of Jerusalem and the city's patriarch with an offer: they would take vows, live as monks. And dedicate themselves to protecting the Christian pilgrims then traveling the dangerous road from the port of Jaffa up to Jerusalem, a route regularly worked by bandits since the First Crusade had opened it to European traffic without making it remotely safe. Baldwin gave them quarters on the Temple Mount, in what had been the Al-Aqsa Mosque, and the name that stuck to them, the Poor Fellow-Soldiers of Christ and of the Temple of Solomon, came directly from where they slept. For a decade the order existed on the strength of that mission alone, without formal recognition from Rome. That changed in January eleven twenty-nine, when a council convened at Troyes by Bernard of Clairvaux, already the most influential churchman in Western Christendom, gave the Templars an official Rule, modeled on the Benedictine monastic pattern Bernard's own Cistercian order followed, that bound them to poverty, chastity, and obedience in the same breath as combat. Bernard did not stop at endorsing them procedurally. He wrote a treatise defending the entire premise of a warrior-monk, a man who could kill in battle and still be doing God's work rather than sinning against it, and that argument did as much to legitimize the order in the eyes of Europe's nobility as the council's paperwork did.

What the order actually became, over the following century and a half, had less to do with swords than with money. Templar houses stretched from Portugal to the Baltic to the crusader states themselves, and a pilgrim or a merchant could deposit funds at one of them, receive a sealed document recording the sum. And withdraw an equivalent amount at another Templar house hundreds or thousands of miles away, a system that functioned, in practice, like a letter of credit centuries before that term existed, run at a scale nobody else in Latin Christendom was managing. Kings noticed. The French crown under Philip II ran its royal treasury through the Paris Temple. Henry III of England kept his own treasury there in London and used the order for financial transactions well beyond simple storage. Popes borrowed from them too; Alexander III leaned on Templar credit more than once. By the time Philip IV came to the throne, the order he would eventually destroy was one of the wealthiest, most trusted financial institutions in Europe, and Philip himself was one of its debtors, a king whose wars and whose own extravagant court had left him owing the Temple money he had no easy way to repay. This book has already shown what Philip IV was capable of doing to a pope who stood in his way. Clement V, the Frenchman he'd helped install and who would go on to move the papacy itself into Avignon rather than risk Rome's independence from the French crown, was not a man who was going to stand in the way of what came next either.

The charges brought against the arrested Templars that October, and refined over the years of interrogation that followed, were specific, lurid, and almost entirely without corroboration outside the confessions the charges themselves produced. Denying Christ during initiation. Spitting or trampling on the cross. Obscene, sometimes explicitly sexual kissing rituals between the man being initiated and the officer receiving him. Sodomy, encouraged or at least tolerated within the order. Worship of an idol, described in wildly inconsistent terms by different confessors, sometimes a severed head, sometimes a cat, sometimes a three-faced figure given the name Baphomet. That name did not originate with the Templars at all. It is a garbled medieval French rendering of Mahomet, used by earlier crusade-era writers as a corrupted way of referring to the prophet Muhammad, and it surfaces in the Templar trial only inside coerced testimony, never in any surviving Templar record, oath, or ritual text from before the arrests. The pattern across the whole interrogation is even more telling than any single charge: wherever inquisitors applied torture freely, confessions came quickly and in volume; wherever they didn't, on Cyprus, and to a lesser degree in England, confessions were rare or entirely absent. Modern historians who have studied the trial in full, Malcolm Barber's work remains the standard account, treat that correlation as close to decisive. Men were not confessing to a hidden truth. They were confessing to whatever stopped the torture, in whatever shape their interrogators had already decided the truth needed to take.

The chapter just before this one described real heretics. The Cathars held a real and coherently argued theology that split the world between a good, spiritual God and an evil one responsible for matter itself, a nine-centuries-later echo of the very split this book's own excommunicated shipowner had first argued for and lost. Whatever else can be said about the crusade and the inquisition that destroyed them, the Cathars actually believed what they were killed for believing. The Templars believed nothing resembling what they were forced to confess. There was no idol, no organized blasphemy, no secret rite passed down from house to house. There was a rich, well-connected order that happened to hold a debt its own king wanted erased, and a legal machinery built over the previous century, the same inquisitorial apparatus first refined against the Cathars, now turned against men whose actual sin, if the word applies at all, was solvency.

Jacques de Molay spent nearly seven years as a prisoner after that October morning, tried, retried, made to confess and then to retract the confession, before the case against him finally reached its end. On March 18th, thirteen fourteen, a small number of sources argue for March 11th instead. And the surviving record does not fully settle it, he was led out to the Ile aux Juifs, a small island in the Seine near the western tip of the Ile de la Cite, close enough to Notre Dame to be seen from its towers but not, as the popular version of the story usually has it, burned in the cathedral's own square. He had recanted his confession by then, publicly, at the cost of guaranteeing his own execution rather than a quieter life sentence. What he actually said as the flames reached him is not fully recoverable. The most contemporary surviving account, a poem by Geoffroi de Paris, a clerk with connections to Philip's own court, has him appealing to God to judge the truly guilty and avenge the wronged, a general cry for justice rather than a curse aimed at any specific man. The version most people know today, Molay naming Philip and Clement directly and prophesying both would answer for it within the year, does not appear in any source from thirteen fourteen. It was assembled and popularized centuries later, most influentially by a French novel series written in the 1950s, and it has since displaced the real, vaguer record so thoroughly that most retellings don't know there was ever a plainer version to displace.

The timing that followed needed no curse to be strange enough on its own. Clement V died on April 20th, thirteen fourteen, a little over a month after Molay's execution, at the end of a long decline from illness that had been dragging on for years rather than any sudden strike. Philip IV lasted longer, into November, when a stroke took him while he was out hunting. He lingered for weeks afterward rather than dying on the spot, and was gone by the twenty-ninth. Both men were dead within the same calendar year Molay had been burned in, which is a real and striking fact that needs no supernatural explanation to be worth noticing, and no supernatural explanation to resist either. Clement had been ill for years. A forty-six-year-old king can suffer a stroke without divine intervention arranging it. The Capetian dynasty Philip left behind went on to a stranger fate than either death alone: three of his sons and then a grandson died in rapid succession over the following fourteen years, ending, by thirteen twenty-eight, a direct royal line that had held the French throne for three centuries. That collapse is real, documented history. The idea that a dying man's curse from an island in the Seine reached forward to cause it belongs to the same later imagination that gave Molay his more dramatic final words, a legend built, brick by brick, on top of a true story that was disturbing enough without it.
