Dead Men on Thrones.

Chapter Twenty-Nine: The Printing Press Changes Everything.

Sometime in the 1440s, in a rented workshop in Mainz, a goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg solved a problem that had nothing obviously to do with God. He was not trying to spread an idea. He was trying to cast metal letters precisely enough, in an alloy soft enough to pour and hard enough to survive thousands of impressions, that they could be arranged into words, locked into a frame, inked, and pressed into paper using a screw mechanism borrowed from wine and olive presses. Exactly when he started is genuinely unclear, one account puts it as early as fourteen thirty-eight, another says fourteen forty to fourteen forty-five, and the records that would settle it were never kept with that question in mind. What survives more clearly is the result: a Bible, completed around fourteen fifty-five, of which fewer than fifty copies still exist anywhere in the world. Nobody in that workshop was thinking about Jan Hus, dead forty years already, or about the German monk who would not be born for another decade. Gutenberg had solved a mechanical problem. The problem he had actually solved, without fully grasping it himself, was that for the entire history of the written word until that workshop, every single copy of every single book had required one human being to sit and copy it out by hand, one letter at a time, which meant that whoever controlled the hands controlled which ideas survived.

The speed of what happened next is the part that is easy to undersell by only citing one famous book. By fifteen hundred, barely fifty years after that first Bible, printing presses were operating in more than two hundred cities across Europe. Venice alone had something like a hundred and fifty of them running at once. This was not a slow diffusion of a curiosity. It was the fastest spread of a new production technology the continent had ever seen, and almost none of it, at first, had anything to do with theology. Presses printed grammar books, legal codes, almanacs, indulgence forms, and a great deal of material nobody today would call important. The religious consequences arrived later, as a side effect of capacity that already existed for other reasons, which is usually how the most dangerous uses of a new technology arrive.

This book's previous chapter told the story of a Bohemian priest named Jan Hus, burned at Constance in fourteen fifteen for an argument not far from the one Marcion had been excommunicated for making thirteen centuries earlier: that ordinary believers deserved more direct access to their own faith than the institution wanted to give them. The Council that condemned Hus was not satisfied with killing a living man. In fourteen twenty-eight, on orders from Rome, a bishop had John Wycliffe's bones dug out of consecrated ground at Lutterworth, thirteen years after Hus's execution and forty four years after Wycliffe's own natural death, burned, and the ashes scattered into the River Swift. Hus was not an isolated case either. Eighty three years later and four hundred miles south, a Dominican friar named Girolamo Savonarola had done something Hus never managed: he ran Florence itself for nearly four years, preaching apocalyptic reform to crowds large enough to burn the city's vanities in bonfires in its own central square. It was not enough. On May 23rd, fourteen ninety-eight, Savonarola and two companion friars were hanged and burned in that same square, and their ashes were scattered in the Arno so that nothing recognizable would be left to venerate. A priest with no political power and a friar who had briefly held an entire city both ended the same way, because both existed inside the same hard constraint: a man can be found, in one place, and a fire can be lit under him exactly once.

The institution that did this had learned something more durable than fire long before Hus or Savonarola were born, and it had learned it from the very man this book keeps returning to. Marcion of Sinope's own writings do not survive. Not one line of his edited gospel or his Antitheses exists today in his own hand or in a copy anyone made deliberately to preserve his argument. What survives is Tertullian and Irenaeus quoting him at length in order to refute him, which means the only reason anyone today can describe what Marcion actually believed is that his enemies thought his words were dangerous enough to be worth repeating and dismantling in public. For the thirteen centuries between his excommunication and Gutenberg's workshop, that was close to the only way an idea outlived the person who first argued it: by surviving inside the writing of someone who controlled a scriptorium and had a reason to copy it, even hostile reasons. An institution that decides what gets recopied, over generations, controls memory itself almost as completely as it controls the stake.

A German monk named Martin Luther broke that constraint by accident. On October 31st, fifteen seventeen, a date that rests more on later tradition than on any contemporary eyewitness record, Luther is said to have posted ninety five theses in Latin at Wittenberg, written as an invitation to an academic debate among theologians, not as a manifesto for the public. Someone printed them anyway, without his permission, and someone else translated them into German. According to the fullest surviving account of the early printings, three separate editions appeared before the year was out, in Leipzig, Nuremberg, and Basel, roughly three hundred copies apiece, most of which have since been lost precisely because nobody at the time thought they were printing something historic. Within about two weeks, the theses had spread through Germany. Within roughly two months, by most accounts, they had reached the rest of Europe. Luther himself seems to have been startled by this. In a letter the following March, he wrote plainly that he had never wished or planned to publicize the theses at all. The printing press had taken an internal academic provocation out of his hands within days and turned it into something no single bishop, abbot, or even pope could call back in.

What happened to Luther's output over the next several years has no real precedent in Hus's century or Savonarola's. Within five years of Wittenberg, by the historian Andrew Pettegree's reckoning, Luther had become Europe's most published author, full stop, not merely the most published writer on religion. Estimates of his exact share of the German book market vary depending on what is being counted and over what years, one commonly cited figure puts his writing at roughly a third of all books sold in Germany between fifteen eighteen and fifteen twenty-five, a more conservative count puts his share of total German printed output nearer one in five across a slightly longer stretch. Either number describes something that had never happened to a condemned heretic before: his words were not in one place to be found and burned. They were in Leipzig and Basel and a dozen other print shops simultaneously, each shop indifferent to whatever happened to the others, each capable of producing hundreds more copies in the time it would have taken a single scribe to finish one.

One hundred and six years after Constance sentenced Hus, the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V summoned Luther to a different imperial assembly, in the German city of Worms, to answer for a version of the same underlying argument. Worms in fifteen twenty-one had every legal instrument Constance had carried in fourteen fifteen: an emperor's authority, the threat of an imperial ban, even a promise of safe conduct that imperial lawyers could, in theory, have argued away exactly as Sigismund's lawyers once argued Hus's away. What that same machinery no longer had, and what this book's next chapter picks up in full, was the ability to make the argument itself disappear once the verdict came down. Luther could be declared an outlaw. His books could not be un-printed, and there were already too many of them, in too many places, for a single confiscation order to matter.

The institution's answer, when it finally arrived, was not fire. It was a list. In fifteen fifty-nine, under Pope Paul IV, after an earlier attempt two years before had been quietly withdrawn, Rome issued the Index Librorum Prohibitorum, condemning the entire body of work of some five hundred fifty authors by name, one at a time, rather than attempting to track down and burn every physical copy of what they had written. A church that had spent centuries controlling which manuscripts got recopied had been forced, by a goldsmith's workshop in Mainz, to admit that burning was no longer a sufficient technology. What replaced it was paperwork: a catalogue of everything the institution could no longer stop from existing, only from being read with its permission.
