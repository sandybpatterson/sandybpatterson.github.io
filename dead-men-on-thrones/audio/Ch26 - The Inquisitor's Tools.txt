Dead Men on Thrones.

Chapter Twenty-Six: The Inquisitor's Tools.

On February 12th, fourteen eighty-one, the city of Seville burned six men and six women at the stake, the first mass execution carried out by an institution that had existed, on paper, for barely two years. The tribunal had been operating out of a fortress on the edge of the city since the previous September, and this first auto-da-fé set the pattern that would repeat, with growing efficiency, for the next three and a half centuries: a public procession, a reading of the charges, a handing over of the condemned to men who were not, technically, the ones doing the killing. Twelve people were dead by the end of the day. Nobody involved thought that number was unusual enough to record with any particular horror. It was simply the machine's first day of work.

The machine itself was younger than that morning in Seville by only a couple of years. On November 1st, fourteen seventy-eight, Pope Sixtus IV issued the bull Exigit Sinceras Devotionis Affectus, granting King Ferdinand II of Aragon and Queen Isabella I of Castile the authority to appoint inquisitors of their own choosing within their own kingdoms. This was not, whatever the name suggested, a revival of the thirteenth century papal Inquisition this book has already described hunting down the surviving Cathars of the Languedoc. That earlier institution answered to Rome, staffed by Dominican friars operating under papal instruction. This one answered to Ferdinand and Isabella. The pope had granted permission; the crown held the leash. Sixtus IV would come to regret how loosely he'd held it. By fourteen eighty-two, alarmed at reports reaching him from Spain, he tried to reassert control over inquisitors he himself had authorized, writing that many of them were moved less by zeal for the faith than by "avarice, hatred, and other motives," seizing property and settling grudges under cover of the institution's authority. The attempt failed. The crown had no intention of handing back a tool this useful, and Sixtus IV spent the last two years of his papacy watching an institution he had created operate almost entirely outside his reach.

The man who built that institution into something durable was not present at its founding. Tomás de Torquemada, a Dominican friar who had served as Isabella's personal confessor since fourteen seventy-four, was not among the first two inquisitors sent to Seville in fourteen eighty. He rose to the top of the structure only in August fourteen eighty-three, when he was named Inquisitor General for Castile and León, a post extended two months later, in October, to cover Aragon, Catalonia, Valencia, and Majorca as well. From that position he held for the next fifteen years, until his death on September 16th, fourteen ninety-eight, Torquemada took a single fortress tribunal and turned it into a kingdom-wide bureaucracy with standardized procedures, written instructions for local inquisitors, and a target that expanded, in practice, well beyond the conversos it had originally been built to investigate.

What made the Spanish Inquisition distinctive was not merely that it tortured people. Torture in ecclesiastical and secular courts alike was a routine feature of medieval and early modern justice, and this book's previous chapter has already shown what coerced confessions were worth as evidence. What deserves its own attention here is how carefully the method was engineered around a specific theological constraint, and how that constraint shaped the exact shape of the suffering it was meant to limit.

Canon law held, going back to a ruling at the Council of Tours in eleven sixty-three, that the Church itself could not shed blood. Churchmen who spilled it, even in a just cause, were understood to have crossed a line their own office forbade them from crossing. This is the principle behind a legal fiction Inquisition trials leaned on constantly: a condemned heretic was never executed by the Church. He was "relaxed to the secular arm," formally handed to civil authorities with the specific written request that they moderate the sentence so that there be no shedding of blood and no danger of death. Every person present at that handover understood exactly what request was actually being made. Burning caused no cut, spilled no blood a churchman's hands could be blamed for, and satisfied the letter of a rule the outcome made a mockery of.

A separate and older principle shaped what happened before that point, during interrogation itself. Pope Innocent IV's twelve fifty-two bull Ad extirpanda had authorized secular officials to torture heresy suspects, but explicitly barred them from causing death or permanent loss of limb. Three centuries later, the Spanish Inquisition's own procedural manuals still operated inside that same boundary, and its three standard methods read almost like an engineering solution to the constraint. The garrucha bound a prisoner's wrists behind his back, hoisted him by rope, and dropped him partway, a technique that dislocated shoulders without cutting skin. The potro stretched a bound body across a frame by turning rollers at either end, straining joints toward their limit without severing anything. The toca forced cloth into the mouth and poured water over it in a controlled simulation of drowning, terror and asphyxiation calibrated to stop short of the death the rule forbade. None of the three drew blood in any way a notary's report would need to mention. All three were agony deliberately engineered to fit inside a rule that had never, in Tours or in Rome, imagined it would be used this way. The limits were not always respected in practice. Sessions were nominally capped in length, a physician and notary were supposed to attend, and the law forbade torturing the same suspect twice over the same charge, a restriction inquisitors evaded routinely by simply declaring a second session a continuation of the first rather than a new one.

The Inquisition's machinery reached its widest public reckoning in a single compressed year. On January 2nd, fourteen ninety-two, Granada, the last Muslim kingdom on the Iberian Peninsula, surrendered to Ferdinand and Isabella, completing a reconquest of Spain that had run for over seven centuries. Less than three months later, on March 31st, the crown signed the Alhambra Decree, ordering every Jew in Spain to convert to Christianity or leave the kingdom by the end of July. On August 3rd, Christopher Columbus sailed from the port of Palos, funded in part by wealth the crown had only recently finished consolidating. Three events, one triumphant, one expulsive, one about to remake the map of the world, landed inside a single Spanish year almost by coincidence and have been read as a single achievement ever since.

How many people the Alhambra Decree actually displaced is genuinely unsettled among historians, not a matter of popular exaggeration alone. The archival historian Henry Kamen, working from surviving tax and emigration records, has argued for a much lower number than older accounts assumed, somewhere near forty to fifty thousand emigrants out of a pre-expulsion Jewish population he estimates at roughly eighty thousand, with most of the rest choosing conversion over exile. Older estimates, still commonly repeated, run considerably higher, into the hundreds of thousands. No single number commands consensus. What isn't contested is the choice the decree actually posed: baptism, exile, or, for those who tried to do neither openly and kept practicing in secret, exactly the kind of suspected crime the Inquisition existed to investigate.

The death toll attributed to Torquemada personally suffers from a similar gap between a widely repeated figure and what the evidence actually supports. The number most often cited, roughly two thousand burned during his tenure, traces back to the nineteenth century Jewish historian Heinrich Graetz, who estimated that total across Torquemada's fourteen years in office. It should not be confused with the modern archival estimate most historians now cite for the institution as a whole, which is Kamen's figure of three to five thousand executions across the Inquisition's entire three hundred and fifty six year existence, not Torquemada's fifteen years alone. Treated as Graetz's own period estimate rather than Kamen's institutional one, two thousand sits within a plausible range for the years when the Inquisition's killing ran hottest. Treated as settled fact, it borrows a precision the surviving records from the 1480s and 1490s simply don't support.

This book's excommunicated shipowner had wanted, thirteen centuries earlier, to cut the Jewish God and the Jewish scriptures out of Christian theology entirely, and lost that argument to a church that kept both. Spain in fourteen ninety-two was not refighting that theological quarrel. It shared an appetite with it, not a cause. Marcion wanted a cleaner theology; Ferdinand and Isabella wanted a cleaner kingdom, and pursued it by removing Jewish people from Christian Spain rather than removing Jewish scripture from Christian belief, first by decree, then by the slower machinery of an institution built to catch anyone who had converted without meaning it. Neither instinct required the other to exist.

The institution built to enforce that second project outlived the century that created it by more than three hundred years, and it did not go quietly even at the end. It was abolished once in eighteen oh eight, under the French occupation, and reinstated the moment that occupation ended. It was abolished again in eighteen thirteen, by the liberal Cortes convened at Cadiz, and reinstated again the following year when the old order returned. Only on July 15th, eighteen thirty-four, under a decree signed by the regent Maria Cristina de Borbon during the minority of the child queen Isabella II, did the abolition finally hold. Three hundred and fifty six years had passed since Sixtus IV's bull. It had taken two failed attempts and a third try under a government secure enough to make it stick before an institution built to outlast argument actually stopped.
