HOW TO USE THIS DOCUMENT

This book does not carry footnotes in its main text. That is a deliberate
choice — the argument is made in prose, and a reader should be able to follow
it without stepping over a numbered marker every third sentence. But every
specific claim in these chapters rests on something, and this is where that
something is written down.

Entries are grouped per chapter under three headings:

  PRIMARY SOURCES     — the ancient texts the chapter actually draws on,
                        including the hostile ones. Where a text survives only
                        inside a later writer's rebuttal, that is stated.
  MODERN SCHOLARSHIP  — the named researchers and books behind the chapter's
                        historical claims.
  WHERE THIS IS       — the contested points. Every place the chapter takes a
  CONTESTED             position that serious scholars disagree about, with
                        who disagrees and on what grounds.

That third heading is the one that matters most. This book's whole argument is
that institutions flatten messy history into clean stories that serve them.
An appendix that hid its own uncertainty would be doing the same thing.

A NOTE ON DATES: ancient dates in this material are frequently approximate
and frequently disputed. Where a date is genuinely argued over, it is marked
"c." or the dispute is named outright. Where this document gives a firm date,
that date is firm.

Covers Chapters 1–12 (Part One complete, Part Two underway).
Entries for Chapters 13–64 will be added as those chapters are drafted.

CHAPTER ONE — BEFORE THE BOOK
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Ignatius of Antioch. The seven letters — Ephesians, Magnesians, Trallians,
Romans, Philadelphians, Smyrnaeans, and Polycarp — written while under guard
en route to execution, conventionally c. 107.
    The "middle recension" of seven letters is the text scholars generally
    accept as authentic; a longer thirteen-letter recension is a later
    expansion. The Philadelphians passage about "the archives" — where
    Ignatius answers a demand for written proof by saying his archive is
    Jesus Christ — is the anecdote the chapter is built on.

Justin Martyr. First Apology, c. 155–157. Chapter 67 describes an ordinary
Sunday gathering: "the memoirs of the apostles" read aloud alongside the
prophets, for as long as time allows.
    The single most important sentence in this chapter. It shows certain
    texts acquiring scriptural function in practice, decades before anyone
    wrote a list.

The Muratorian Fragment. A damaged Latin manuscript naming most of what
became the New Testament. Discovered by Ludovico Antonio Muratori and
published in 1740.

Tatian. Diatessaron, c. 170s. The four gospels woven into a single continuous
harmony, which became the standard gospel text for Syriac-speaking Christians
for generations.

Gospel of Thomas; Gospel of Peter; Gospel of the Hebrews. The non-canonical
gospels the chapter names as circulating alongside the four. Thomas survives
complete in Coptic from the Nag Hammadi find of 1945; Hebrews survives only
in fragments quoted by later writers.

Paul, Letter to the Romans, chapter 16 — Phoebe named as deacon, Prisca as
co-worker, Junia as "outstanding among the apostles."
    The counter-narrative texture in this chapter's closing section comes
    straight out of Paul's own greeting list.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Bauer, Walter. Rechtgläubigkeit und Ketzerei im ältesten Christentum (1934).
English translation: Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity (1971).
    The strong thesis that in many regions what later got labeled heresy was
    simply the local Christianity, with Roman-flavored orthodoxy arriving
    afterward and rewriting the record.

Ehrman, Bart D. Lost Christianities: The Battles for Scripture and the Faiths
We Never Knew (2003).
    The accessible general treatment of the plural-Christianities picture
    this chapter describes. Named in the project brief as a tonal model.

Metzger, Bruce M. The Canon of the New Testament: Its Origin, Development,
and Significance (1987).
    Still the standard reference work on how the list got made.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The date of Ignatius's letters. The traditional c. 107 is not settled. A
substantial body of scholarship places them in the 130s or 140s, and a
minority argues later still. The chapter says the exact year "is argued over
by people who have devoted their careers to arguing over it," which is
accurate and is the right amount of weight to put on it.

The date of the Muratorian Fragment. The conventional c. 180 dating is
directly challenged by Albert C. Sundberg, "Canon Muratori: A Fourth-Century
List," Harvard Theological Review 66 (1973), and at book length by Geoffrey
Mark Hahneman, The Muratorian Fragment and the Development of the Canon
(1992), both arguing for a fourth-century Eastern origin. If they are right,
the "first canon list" is roughly two centuries later than the textbooks say.
The debate has never fully closed. The chapter flags this explicitly and
should keep doing so.

Bauer's thesis. Most historians today think Bauer overstated his case — the
picture varied enormously by region, and plenty of communities held something
recognizably continuous with later orthodoxy from very early. The chapter
takes the correct position: the strong version is disputed, the underlying
fact it rests on is not.

CHAPTER TWO — THE MAN WHO FORCED THE QUESTION
================================================================================

A STANDING WARNING FOR THIS CHAPTER

Almost nothing known about Marcion comes from Marcion. No manuscript of his
gospel survives. No copy of the Antitheses survives. Every substantive claim
about what he taught reaches us through writers who were trying to destroy
his movement, most of them writing decades to centuries after his death. This
is not a reason to disbelieve everything they say — hostile sources quote
their opponents at length precisely in order to refute them, which preserves
material that would otherwise be lost entirely — but it is a reason to hold
every biographical detail loosely.

PRIMARY SOURCES

Tertullian. Adversus Marcionem (Against Marcion), five books, c. 207–208.
    The single fullest source on Marcion's theology and his edited gospel,
    written roughly half a century after his death by someone determined to
    refute him verse by verse. The "wasps building nests from a wasp" line
    is Tertullian's.

Irenaeus. Adversus Haereses (Against Heresies), c. 180. Book 3 carries the
Polycarp encounter — "I recognize the firstborn of Satan."
    Irenaeus claims to have the story from people who were present. He is
    also the closest thing to a contemporary witness in the chain, which is
    why the chapter uses it while flagging what it is.

Epiphanius of Salamis. Panarion, c. 375–378. The catalogue of heresies
carrying the story that Marcion was expelled from his father's church at
Sinope for seducing a virgin.
    More than two hundred years after the fact, with no earlier source
    mentioning it. The chapter treats it as probably a shame story attached
    after the fact, and says so.

Eznik of Kolb. Against the Sects (De Deo), 5th century, Armenian.
    Evidence that Marcionite communities were still active and still worth
    refuting roughly three hundred years after the excommunication.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Harnack, Adolf von. Marcion: Das Evangelium vom fremden Gott. First edition
c. 1920; second, expanded edition 1924. English: Marcion: The Gospel of the
Alien God.
    The origin of the strong Marcion-as-hinge thesis this book largely tells:
    that Marcion invented the idea of a closed New Testament and forced the
    wider church to answer in kind.
    Sources give the first edition as either 1920 or 1921 and it isn't worth
    settling here; "c. 1920" is accurate either way. If Chapter 45 ever needs
    the exact year for a specific argument, check it then.

Lieu, Judith M. Marcion and the Making of a Heretic: God and Scripture in the
Second Century. Cambridge University Press, 2015.
    The most important modern treatment, and the direct counterweight to
    Harnack. Argues canon formation was slower and multi-causal, and that the
    singular-arch-heretic story served the people telling it.

Klinghardt, Matthias. Das älteste Evangelium und die Entstehung der
kanonischen Evangelien (2015). English edition: The Oldest Gospel and the
Formation of the Canonical Gospels (2021).
    Argues Marcion's gospel was not an edit of Luke but an earlier text that
    Luke — and Matthew, and Mark — drew on. Recurs in Chapter 3.

Vinzent, Markus. Marcion and the Dating of the Synoptic Gospels (2014).
    Goes further still: the canonical gospels as a set written partly in
    response to Marcion rather than before him.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

This is the most contested chapter in Part One, and the book handles it
correctly by refusing to resolve it. Three live positions:

  1. Harnack's — Marcion is the hinge. Most of the chapter's narrative.
  2. Lieu's — Marcion is the earliest visible symptom of a pressure that was
     building anyway (Gnostic movements broadly, Montanism, competing
     apostolic claims, and the technical shift from scroll to codex making
     large bound collections physically possible).
  3. Klinghardt / Vinzent — the direction of dependency between Marcion's
     gospel and Luke may run the other way entirely.

The chapter lands on "both things can be true at once, and probably are,"
and leaves it unresolved. That is the honest position and should not be
tidied up in revision.

The 144 excommunication date is firm. The 200,000 sesterces and its return
come from Tertullian and are plausible but single-sourced.

CHAPTER THREE — THE LOST DOCUMENT
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Papias of Hierapolis. Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord, five volumes,
first decades of the second century. Lost. Survives only in quotations
preserved by later writers, principally Eusebius, Historia Ecclesiastica
3.39, which carries the Elder's remark that "Matthew compiled the sayings in
the Hebrew dialect, and each one interpreted them as best he could."

Gospel of Thomas. 114 sayings, almost no narrative frame, no passion, no
resurrection. Recovered complete in Coptic at Nag Hammadi in 1945.
    The chapter's evidence that the sayings-gospel genre was a real form and
    not a scholarly invention required to make Q plausible.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Weisse, Christian Hermann. Die evangelische Geschichte kritisch und
philosophisch bearbeitet (1838).
    Early formulation of what became the two-source hypothesis.

Streeter, B. H. The Four Gospels: A Study of Origins (1924).
    The classic English statement of the case for Q.

Farrer, Austin. "On Dispensing with Q," in Studies in the Gospels (1955).
Developed by Michael Goulder and, most accessibly, by Mark Goodacre, The Case
Against Q: Studies in Markan Priority and the Synoptic Problem (2002).
    The serious minority position: Mark first, Matthew used Mark, Luke used
    both. No lost document required.

Klinghardt, Matthias — as above, Chapter 2. His argument was developed in
part specifically to solve the Synoptic Problem without Q.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

Q's entire existence. This is worth stating as bluntly as the chapter does:
no fragment of Q has ever been found, and no ancient writer quotes it by name
or credits it as a source. It is an inference from the agreements between two
other documents, not a discovery. The chapter says this plainly and does not
retreat from it.

The verbal-agreement statistics. The chapter cites twenty-seven of twenty-
eight Greek words matching in the "serving two masters" passage, and all
twenty-four matching in the "ask and receive" passage. These are standard
figures in the Q literature. If a revision ever changes the wording of that
sentence, re-verify the counts rather than trusting the paraphrase.

Papias's testimony. Whether the Elder was describing anything like Q, or
something else entirely, or simply garbling the origins of canonical Matthew,
is genuinely unresolved. The chapter says "nobody can say for certain,"
which is the right call.

CHAPTER FOUR — FOUR ACCOUNTS, ONE STORY
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Celsus. The True Word (Alethes Logos), c. 177–180. Lost. Survives only as
quoted inside its own rebuttal.

Origen. Contra Celsum, c. 248.
    Origen quotes Celsus at such length in order to answer him that the lost
    attack can be substantially reconstructed. Origen's defense — that four
    identical accounts would be the real red flag — is the argument the
    chapter puts on the table and declines to settle.

Josephus. Antiquities of the Jews, Book 18. The census under Quirinius,
independently dating it to 6 AD.

Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, both 4th century. The earliest and best
manuscripts of Mark, both ending at 16:8.

Eusebius and Jerome both report that nearly every Greek manuscript known to
them ended Mark at verse 8.

Irenaeus appears to know some version of the longer ending by the 170s, which
is why the chapter says it "did circulate reasonably early" rather than
calling it purely late.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The census problem. Matthew places the birth under Herod the Great, who died
in 4 BC. Luke ties it to the Quirinius census of 6 AD. The chapter states
that no proposed reconciliation has reached anything like consensus, which is
accurate. Proposals include an earlier unrecorded census, a mistranslation of
Luke's Greek, and a deliberate literary anachronism. None has carried the field.

The ending of Mark. Textual critics overwhelmingly hold that the original
ended at 16:8. This is about as close to settled as New Testament textual
criticism gets, but the longer ending's early circulation is real and the
chapter is right not to imply it was invented centuries later.

The two genealogies. The "one of them is secretly Mary's" harmonization is
nowhere stated in either text. The chapter notes this and notes that it would
still be beside the point in a culture tracing legal descent through the
father. Both observations are sound.

FOR THE READER WHO WANTS THE FULL CASE

Ehrman, Bart D. Jesus, Interrupted (2009) lays out the gospel discrepancies
at book length for a general audience. Raymond E. Brown, The Birth of the
Messiah (1977, rev. 1993) and The Death of the Messiah (1994) are the
standard scholarly treatments from a committed Catholic scholar — useful
precisely because they are not written to embarrass the text.

CHAPTER FIVE — THE COSMIC GOSPEL
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

The Gospel of John, throughout. The prologue (1:1–14); the ego eimi sayings;
the temple confrontation at 8:58; the farewell discourse (chapters 14–17),
with the vine and branches at chapter 15; Thomas at 20:24–29.

Exodus 3:14 — "I Am Who I Am." The Septuagint, the Greek translation in wide
use in the first century, renders it with the same phrase John repeatedly
puts in Jesus's mouth.

Philo of Alexandria. The Logos as mediator between an unreachable God and
the material world, fusing the Stoic rational principle with Hebrew
scripture's creative "word of the Lord." Writing in Greek in roughly the same
decades as Jesus's ministry.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Martyn, J. Louis. History and Theology in the Fourth Gospel (1968).
    The influential reconstruction tying John's aposynagogos passages to the
    Birkat ha-Minim, a curse against heretics added to synagogue liturgy in
    the late first century, and reading John's community as recent refugees
    from a formal split.

Reinhartz, Adele. Cast Out of the Covenant: Jews and Anti-Judaism in the
Gospel of John (2018).
    The leading pushback. Reads the expulsion language as the gospel's own
    rhetoric, defining insider and outsider, rather than a report of one
    datable legislated event.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

John's relationship to Philo. Whether the author read Philo, absorbed the
idea secondhand, or arrived at something similar independently out of Jewish
wisdom tradition is unsettled. The chapter's own observation is a good one
and worth keeping: Philo's Logos recurs throughout his work, while John's
appears once, gloriously, and never again.

The Martyn thesis. Now substantially contested — both on whether the
Birkat ha-Minim was even in use early enough, and on whether the expulsion
language is reportage at all. What survives the dispute is the broader
picture: this community understood itself as painfully and recently separated
from the Jewish world it grew out of. The chapter is careful to say exactly
that much and no more.

The dating of John, c. 90–110, is standard and not seriously disputed.

A NOTE FOR CHAPTER 56

The John 15 material — conditional intimacy wired to monitored productivity —
is picked up again in Chapter 56 on clinical rather than literary terms.
Sources for the psychological claims belong in that chapter's entry, not
this one. Do not import clinical framing back into Chapter 5; the chapter
deliberately keeps it literary and defers.

CHAPTER SIX — THE MAN WHO BUILT THE THEOLOGY
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

The seven undisputed letters: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians,
Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon.

The six disputed: Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, and the three
pastorals — 1 and 2 Timothy, Titus.

Acts of the Apostles. The three accounts of the Damascus road — chapters 9,
22, and 26 — which differ on who heard the voice, who saw the light, and who
fell. Also the sole source for Paul's Roman citizenship, which his own
letters never mention.

The Epistle of James, 2:14–26 — the only place in the Bible the phrase "faith
alone" appears, and it appears in order to be rejected.

Luther, Martin. Preface to the New Testament (1522) — the "epistle of straw"
line, which Luther removed from later editions.
    The blunter "Jimmy into the stove" remark comes from the Table Talk
    tradition, which is a collection of after-dinner recollections written
    down by students, not Luther's own published prose. Treat it as
    reported, not documented. The chapter's "by some later, more colorful
    accounts" wording is doing the right work.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Sanders, E. P. Paul and Palestinian Judaism: A Comparison of Patterns of
Religion (1977).
    The study that broke Luther's reading. Second Temple Judaism operated on
    what Sanders called covenantal nomism: inside the covenant by grace,
    keeping the law as response rather than price of admission.

Dunn, James D. G. "The New Perspective on Paul" (1983 Manson Memorial
Lecture). Coined the label for the movement Sanders started.

Wright, N. T. — the most widely read constructive development of the New
Perspective.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The New Perspective itself. Not universally accepted. The most serious
pushback comes from theologians who argue it understates how seriously Paul
does talk about sin and individual reconciliation. The chapter's formulation
is exactly right and should be preserved: even Sanders's critics now have to
argue with him rather than around him.

1 Corinthians 14:34–35. Three live positions: (a) a later interpolation Paul
never wrote — supported by the fact that the verses migrate to a different
location in part of the manuscript tradition, a recognized signature of a
marginal note absorbed into the text; (b) Paul quoting a Corinthian slogan in
order to refute it in the next verse; (c) authentic and meant as written. The
chapter presents (a) and (b) and notes there is no settled resolution. Correct.

Galatians 3:28 as a pre-Pauline baptismal formula. Widely held, not certain.
The chapter says "many scholars think," which is the right hedge.

The pastorals' authorship. A majority of critical scholars regard 1 and 2
Timothy and Titus as post-Pauline. This is closer to consensus than the
disputed status of Ephesians, Colossians, and 2 Thessalonians, which are more
genuinely split. Keep the distinction; the chapter currently groups all six
together as "disputed," which is defensible shorthand but slightly flattens
a real difference in scholarly confidence.

SEE ALSO: Appendix C, The Authentic Paul, for the full breakdown.

CHAPTER SEVEN — THE BRILLIANT HERETIC
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Eusebius. Historia Ecclesiastica, Book 6, which is the principal source for
Origen's life — his father Leonides's arrest under Septimius Severus in 202,
his mother hiding his clothes, the letter to his father in prison, the
catechetical school, the torture under Decius.
    Eusebius is broadly sympathetic to Origen. That matters for the
    castration story: this is not a hostile source inventing something
    damaging, it is an admiring source repeating something he had heard.

Origen. On First Principles (De Principiis) — the first systematic Christian
theology, and the source for apokatastasis. Contra Celsum — see Chapter 4.
The Hexapla — the six-column parallel Old Testament, surviving only in
fragments and later citations.

Jerome and Epiphanius both attempted counts of Origen's output. Jerome's
careful tally lands under two thousand titles; Epiphanius claims six
thousand. The chapter uses the discrepancy honestly rather than picking the
bigger number.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The self-castration. Seriously doubted by scholars since at least the late
nineteenth century, on the grounds the chapter gives: the entire intellectual
project was a machine for turning literal readings into symbolic ones. The
live alternative is that "eunuch" was used by Alexandrian teachers for chosen
celibacy, and a metaphor calcified into a reported event. The chapter's
treatment of this — including its observation that this is a different kind
of source problem from the hostile-witness problem elsewhere in the book — is
one of the strongest passages in Part One.

The 553 condemnation. The standard summary is that the Second Council of
Constantinople condemned Origen. Modern scholarship has raised real doubts
about whether the fifteen anathemas against Origenism were issued by the full
ecumenical council at all, or by a smaller local synod (generally placed in
543, under Justinian) whose findings were later folded into the bigger
council's reputation. The chapter flags this. Keep the flag.

Origen's death. Generally placed in 253 or 254, from injuries sustained under
the Decian persecution. The chapter gives both years, which is correct.

CHAPTER EIGHT — CLOSING THE BOOK
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Athanasius. Thirty-Ninth Festal Letter, 367. The earliest surviving document
listing exactly the twenty-seven books of the New Testament, in the order
still printed today.

Eusebius. Historia Ecclesiastica, c. 325 — the four-bin sort of the
candidates into accepted, disputed, spurious, and outright forgeries. Also
Book 7.25, preserving Dionysius of Alexandria's stylistic argument that the
author of Revelation cannot be the author of the Gospel of John; and Book
4.23, preserving Dionysius of Corinth on 1 Clement being read aloud in
worship generations after it was written.

The Shepherd of Hermas and the Epistle of Barnabas, both appended to Codex
Sinaiticus (4th century) with no visual signal marking a boundary.

1 and 2 Clement, given the same honored placement in Codex Alexandrinus
(5th century).

The Didache ("The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles"). Lost for roughly a
millennium; a single surviving copy found by Philotheos Bryennios in a
monastery library in Constantinople in 1873, bound into an eleventh-century
manuscript (Codex Hierosolymitanus), and published in 1883.

Jerome, on 2 Peter reading nothing like 1 Peter — and accepting it anyway.

Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397), reconfirmed at Carthage (419).

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Metzger, Bruce M. The Canon of the New Testament (1987) — as Chapter 1.

Gamble, Harry Y. Books and Readers in the Early Church: A History of Early
Christian Texts (1995).
    For the physical and technological side: scroll to codex, copying,
    circulation. The chapter's argument that geography and reach decided the
    canon depends on this material.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

"The first canon list." The chapter explicitly refuses this framing, noting
that Origen appears to assume a closed twenty-seven-book set in a casual
aside more than a century earlier. This is the point the project's own
pre-publish checklist singles out — that what Athanasius's letter was "first"
to do turned out to have real scholarly pushback that made the chapter better
once included. It did. Do not let a later revision quietly restore the
simpler claim.

The authority of Hippo and Carthage. Regional North African synods, not
ecumenical councils. Real influence in the West, no binding authority in the
East, where questions about Revelation and the shorter catholic epistles kept
circulating for generations. Popular retellings routinely inflate these into
"the council that decided the Bible." The chapter is precise about this and
the precision is load-bearing.

2 Peter's attestation. The claim that it ranks last among the twenty-seven by
a wide margin in early patristic citation is standard in the canon
literature. It is a counting claim, so if the sentence is ever rewritten,
re-verify rather than paraphrasing from memory.


================================================================================
CHAPTER NINE — THE EMPEROR'S CHURCH
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Lactantius. De Mortibus Persecutorum (On the Deaths of the Persecutors),
written within roughly a decade of the Milvian Bridge.
    The earliest account of the vision, and the modest one: a dream, the
    night before, instructing Constantine to mark his soldiers' shields.
    Lactantius also preserves the full Latin text of the Nicomedia letter
    that history calls the Edict of Milan, and the text of Galerius's 311
    Edict of Serdica.

Eusebius. Historia Ecclesiastica — written while Constantine was alive,
mentions divine assistance at the battle without describing a vision.

Eusebius. Vita Constantini (Life of Constantine) — written after Constantine's
death, and now the whole army sees a cross of light above the sun in daylight,
with words beside it, followed by a dream that night explaining it. Eusebius
says he had it from Constantine directly, under oath.
    Three tellings, two by the same author decades apart, each larger than
    the last. The chapter's handling of this escalation is the point of the
    section.

Zosimus. Historia Nova, c. 500. The hostile pagan source for Fausta being
shut in an overheated bathhouse.
    Written more than a century and a half after the events with an evident
    grudge against Constantine's memory, and unsupported by anything closer
    to the events. The chapter treats it with exactly the skepticism it
    applies to Epiphanius in Chapter 2 — consistent handling, and worth
    keeping consistent.

Council of Arles, 314 — convened by Constantine to adjudicate the Donatist
schism, with bishops from as far as Gaul and Britain.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

Sincerity versus calculation. The oldest argument in Constantine studies.
The chapter presents both readings and lands where most serious historians
now land — leaning sympathetic without abandoning the cynical reading — and
then makes the better point, which is that belief and utility were probably
never fully separable for him.

"The Edict of Milan." No document by that name has ever been found and none
was issued at Milan. What survives is Licinius's letter from Nicomedia two
months later. The chapter is precise about this. It matters because the
popular version of this story is wrong in a way that flatters Constantine.

Firm dates in this chapter: Milvian Bridge, 28 October 312. Milan meeting,
313. Galerius's edict, 311. Foundation of Constantinople, 330. Crispus and
Fausta's deaths, 326. Constantine's baptism and death, 337. Arles, 314.
These are not in serious dispute.

The deaths of Crispus and Fausta. No official explanation was ever issued and
none survives in any contemporary record. Every account of motive is later
speculation. The chapter's decision to state only what can be said without
speculation — two people in his own household dead within weeks, on his
orders, never explained — is the correct one.


================================================================================
CHAPTER TEN — ONE LETTER'S DIFFERENCE
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Socrates Scholasticus. Historia Ecclesiastica, Book 1, early-to-mid 5th
century. The source for Paphnutius's mutilation and for Constantine's
reported habit of kissing the ruined eye socket.
    Writing roughly a century after Nicaea. The chapter flags the specific
    detail as possibly more vivid than precisely accurate while treating the
    underlying scene — an emperor hosting men his predecessors' regime
    tortured — as not in dispute.

Eusebius of Caesarea and Athanasius. Both attended the council and both gave
attendance estimates: Eusebius "more than two hundred and fifty," Athanasius
"three hundred, more or less."
    Neither eyewitness used the number 318.

Hilary of Poitiers. On the number 318 as a sign of divine design, connecting
it to Abraham's 318 servants in Genesis 14 and to the Greek numeral spelling
of the number, which opens with letters that also open the name Jesus.
    The source for why 318 became the traditional figure despite neither
    eyewitness using it.

The Nicene Creed, 325. The text itself — one God, one Lord Jesus Christ,
begotten not made, of one substance with the Father.

Arius. The Thalia ("Banquet"), c. 323. Largely lost; known mainly through
hostile quotation, principally Athanasius's own citations of it in his
polemics against Arius.
    The same epistemic situation as Marcion in Chapter 2: the primary source
    survives mainly inside the writing of people trying to destroy it.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Gibbon, Edward. The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire
(1776–1789). The "single diphthong" line.
    Quoted for the line, not relied on for the history. Gibbon's own
    Enlightenment contempt for the whole controversy is itself a data point
    about how later, more secular readers received this material, and the
    chapter says so outright rather than letting the quote stand unexamined.

Hanson, R. P. C. The Search for the Christian Doctrine of God: The Arian
Controversy, 318–381 (1988). The standard academic treatment of the whole
period this chapter and the next several span.

Ayres, Lewis. Nicaea and Its Legacy: An Approach to Fourth-Century
Trinitarian Theology (2004). The major modern account arguing there was
never one settled "Nicene" theology, in 325 or in 381 — consistent with this
chapter's own point that the settlement did not hold.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The 318 number. Not a real headcount. The chapter states this plainly:
symbolic, probably assigned after the fact, and both eyewitnesses who
bothered to estimate gave smaller, vaguer figures. Modern historians
generally estimate 200 to 300.

The Paphnutius story. Sourced to a single writer roughly a century after the
event. Vivid and widely repeated, but not independently corroborated. The
chapter is honest that it cannot confirm the specific detail while treating
the general picture — confessors attending in an emperor's company — as
solid.

Arius's death. The chapter notes the surviving graphic account comes from a
hostile source and treats it with the same skepticism this book has applied
to Zosimus on Fausta (Chapter 9) and Epiphanius on Marcion (Chapter 2).
Consistent handling, worth keeping consistent.

"Three more emperors" in the closing line. Rhetorical shorthand, not a
precise count. Constantius II and Theodosius certainly belong on that list;
Julian and Jovian's reigns were brief and largely orthogonal to this specific
fight. If a revision ever tightens this sentence, do not add false precision
to a line that is doing scene-setting work, not enumeration.


================================================================================
CHAPTER ELEVEN — MAKING IT MANDATORY
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Ammianus Marcellinus. Res Gestae, Book 31. The major contemporary account of
the Battle of Adrianople, 9 August 378, by a historian writing within a few
years of the event. His narrative ends shortly after the battle.

The Edict of Thessalonica ("Cunctos populos"), 27 February 380. Codex
Theodosianus 16.1.2. The text itself — quoted directly, including the
"foolish madmen" (dementes vesanosque) line.

Codex Theodosianus 16.10, the 391–392 decrees, including the line "no one is
to go to the sanctuaries, walk through the temples" — the harder measures
against pagan worship the 380 edict itself does not contain.

The ancient church historians — Socrates Scholasticus, Sozomen, and Rufinus
— for the destruction of the Serapeum in 391, each giving slightly different
detail. The chapter follows the widely repeated core: Bishop Theophilus's
provocation, the pagan barricade, the demolition, and the soldier who struck
the cult statue without the sky falling.

Ambrose of Milan. Letter 51, to Theodosius, written to justify his own
conduct after the Massacre of Thessalonica.
    The primary source for the whole episode, and worth remembering as such
    — the later, more cinematic version with Ambrose physically blocking a
    cathedral doorway comes from the medieval Golden Legend, not this letter.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Williams, Stephen, and Gerard Friell. Theodosius: The Empire at Bay (1995).
    The standard academic biography for a generation.

Hebblewhite, Mark. Theodosius and the Limits of Empire (2020). The first
English-language biography in twenty-five years, reflecting more recent
scholarship.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

What the Edict of Thessalonica actually targeted. Popular retellings
routinely describe it as the moment paganism became illegal empire-wide.
Read on its own terms, its target is intra-Christian — Arians and other
non-Nicene factions — and it does not name paganism at all. The harder
anti-pagan measures came a decade later, in 391–392. The chapter is precise
about this distinction and it should not be softened in revision; it is the
same kind of correction this book has already made about "the first canon
list" (Ch. 8) and "the Edict of Milan" (Ch. 9).

The Massacre of Thessalonica. How much premeditation to assign Theodosius is
genuinely unsettled — whether the order was a considered decision or an
order given in rage and not recalled in time. The chapter states this as an
open question rather than picking a reading.

The Ambrose penance story. Letter 51 establishes that real, substantial
penance was extracted. The specific dramatic staging most people picture —
Ambrose bodily barring the cathedral door — is a later medieval embellishment
(the Golden Legend) with no support in the primary letter. The chapter flags
this the same way it flagged the Zosimus bathhouse detail in Chapter 9.

Adrianople's causal weight. Historians disagree on how directly the defeat
connects to the western empire's collapse a century later. The chapter notes
the disagreement rather than asserting a straight causal line.


================================================================================
CHAPTER TWELVE — THE CREED AS WEAPON
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Rufinus of Aquileia. Commentary on the Apostles' Creed (Commentarius in
Symbolum Apostolorum), c. 404. The source for the twelve-apostles legend
(each apostle contributing one article) and the earliest writer to mention
the "descended into hell" clause at all — noting himself that it was not in
Rome's own creed, only in his own church's at Aquileia.

Marcellus of Ancyra, letter to Julius of Rome, c. 337. The oldest surviving
written text of the Old Roman Creed. Does not contain the descent clause.

Hippolytus (attributed). Apostolic Tradition, early 3rd century. Preserves
the creed in its interrogatory baptismal form — three questions, three
answers, three immersions — rather than as a single recited block.
    Modern scholarship treats the text as likely a composite/edited
    compilation rather than a single author's unified work; the attribution
    to Hippolytus himself is not fully settled. The chapter uses "usually
    attributed to" language for this reason.

Irenaeus, Against Heresies, and Tertullian, Against Marcion — as Chapter 2.
Both also produced independent prose "rule of faith" summaries in roughly
the same decades as the Old Roman Creed's formation, aimed at the same
target, without evidence of coordination between the three centers.

Marcion's edited gospel — as Chapter 2. Reconstructed as beginning at Luke
3:1 combined with Luke 4:31 ("...descended into Capernaum"), omitting the
birth and infancy material entirely.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Kelly, J. N. D. Early Christian Creeds (3rd ed., 1972). The standard modern
study connecting the Old Roman Creed's formation to the anti-Marcionite
crisis of the 170s–180s.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The date and cause of the Old Roman Creed. c. 180 and an anti-Marcionite
purpose is the scholarly mainstream view following Kelly, not a settled
certainty — search results returned during fact-checking specifically
flagged "the most recent debate on the origin of the Apostles' Creed" as
an active scholarly conversation. The chapter attributes the dating to
Kelly by name rather than presenting it as uncontested consensus.

The "descended into hell" clause. Absent from the earliest text (Marcellus,
337), first mentioned by Rufinus at Aquileia specifically, and not
universal until roughly 650. The chapter is precise about this timeline
rather than treating the clause as original to the creed.

The twelve-apostles legend. Rufinus is the source and, on his own broader
scholarship's evidence, is not describing something that could be literally
true — the actual document postdates the apostolic generation by roughly
half a century. The chapter states this plainly.

Hippolytus's authorship of the Apostolic Tradition. Not fully settled among
scholars; the text may be a compilation from multiple periods. The chapter
hedges with "usually attributed to" rather than asserting it flatly.


================================================================================
CHAPTER THIRTEEN — THE GHOST IN THE MACHINE
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Victor of Vita. Historia Persecutionis Africanae Provinciae (History of the
Persecution of the African Province), late 5th century. Contemporary
eyewitness account of Vandal King Huneric's persecution of Catholic
Christians in North Africa, including the text of Huneric's 484 edict, which
explicitly repurposes earlier Roman anti-heretical statutes.

Gregory of Tours. Historia Francorum (History of the Franks), late 6th
century. The source for the traditional account of Clovis's baptism at
Reims, including the detail of roughly three thousand of his soldiers being
baptized alongside him. Written roughly a century after the event it
describes.
    Modern scholarship treats Gregory's account as shaped by his own
    theological and political purposes rather than a neutral transcript;
    the chapter frames the soldier count as "tradition says" rather than
    a verified figure.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Heather, Peter. The Goths (1996) and Empires and Barbarians (2009). Standard
modern treatment of Gothic history and the "Homoian" versus "Arian"
terminology question — Heather's argument that Arianism functioned partly
as a marker of Gothic identity once the Goths were settled among
Nicene-majority Roman populations.

Britannica and the Uppsala University Library's own published history of
the Codex Argenteus. Consulted for the manuscript's sixth-century Ravenna
origin, its likely connection to Theodoric's Ostrogothic court, and its
present location at Uppsala.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The exact date of Clovis's baptism. The traditional date is Christmas Day,
496, but historians have never fully settled it; some scholarship places it
as late as 506–508. The chapter states this range rather than asserting
496 as certain.

The precise theological label for what Ulfilas taught. "Arian" is the
familiar shorthand and the one most readers will recognize, but the more
historically precise term for the Gothic church's actual creed is
"Homoian" — a related but distinct anti-Nicene position that avoided
Arius's own more contested formulations. The chapter uses both terms
deliberately, flagging the distinction rather than treating "Arian" as
perfectly accurate shorthand.

The degree of intentionality behind Huneric's 484 conference in Carthage.
Victor of Vita, the only detailed surviving source, was himself a victim
of the persecution he describes and writing to make the Vandal regime look
as bad as possible; the chapter relies on his account for facts that are
independently corroborated (the edict's text, the exiles) while noting he
is not a neutral narrator.

================================================================================
CHAPTER FOURTEEN — INTO THE VACUUM
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Anonymus Valesianus (Excerpta Valesiana), part II, early 6th century. The
principal surviving narrative source for the deposition of Romulus
Augustulus, Odoacer's dispatch of the imperial regalia to Constantinople,
and the message that the West no longer required its own emperor.

Malchus of Philadelphia, fragments, late 5th century (preserved via
Photius). Byzantine-side source for Zeno's negotiations with Odoacer and
the insistence that Julius Nepos continue to be recognized as the
legitimate Western emperor.

Sidonius Apollinaris, Letters (Epistulae) and Poems, 460s-480s. Sidonius's
own correspondence is the primary source for his career, his defense of
Clermont against Euric, and his exile and later pardon.

Eugippius. Vita Sancti Severini (Life of Saint Severinus), written c. 511,
roughly three decades after Severinus's death, by a monk of his own
community. The sole detailed source for Severinus's activity in Noricum;
written to celebrate its subject as a saint, a genre bias the chapter
notes rather than treating the text as a neutral record.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Ward-Perkins, Bryan. The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization (2005).
Source for the archaeological argument from pottery quality, roof-tile
construction, and material culture generally showing a genuine, measurable
decline in ordinary living standards across the former Western provinces
following the collapse of centralized administration.

Heather, Peter. The Fall of the Roman Empire: A New History of Rome and
the Barbarians (2005). Consulted for the broader mechanics of Western
administrative collapse (cursus publicus, annona, provincial taxation)
across the 5th century, not just the single events of 476.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

Whether 476 is the meaningful "fall" date at all, versus 410 (the sack of
Rome), 455 (the Vandal sack), or 480 (Julius Nepos's death). The chapter
treats 476 as the date the office of Western emperor itself stopped
existing — a real administrative fact — while being explicit that most
modern historians regard Nepos, recognized by Constantinople and not
deposed by any Western authority, as the last legitimate holder of the
title, and that the material collapse Ward-Perkins documents was a
decades-long process before and after the date, not a single-year event.

The reliability of Eugippius's Life of Severinus as a historical source.
Written for hagiographic purposes roughly thirty years after its subject's
death, by an author with an explicit interest in presenting Severinus as a
miracle-working saint. The chapter draws on it for Severinus's concrete
civic activities (food distribution, negotiating captive releases), which
are corroborated by the text's own internal consistency and its otherwise
mundane, non-miraculous detail, while not treating its miracle claims as
historical fact.

The Ward-Perkins "catastrophist" reading itself. Ward-Perkins's account is
influential but not unchallenged; some historians favor a "transformation"
model emphasizing continuity and gradual adaptation over sudden civilizational
collapse. The chapter follows Ward-Perkins's archaeological evidence because
it is the most concrete and least disputable part of his case, without
claiming his broader interpretive framework is the only scholarly view.


================================================================================
CHAPTER FIFTEEN — THE MONK WHO MADE THE PAPACY
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Gregory the Great. Registrum Epistularum (Register of Letters), 590-604.
Roughly 854 letters survive, the primary source for Gregory's
administration of the Patrimony of Saint Peter (instructions to rectors
and defensors on tenant treatment and loans), the Lombard negotiations of
595 and the exchange with Emperor Maurice, and the dispatch of Augustine's
mission to England in 596.

Gregory the Great. Regula Pastoralis (Pastoral Rule / Pastoral Care),
early 590s. The bishop's-handbook text itself; its later reception is
covered under Modern Scholarship below.

Bede. Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum (Ecclesiastical History of
the English People), completed 731 — over a century after the events it
describes for this chapter. Source for the Anglii/angeli slave-market
story and its accompanying puns (Deira/de ira, Aelle/Alleluia).
    The chapter treats this specific anecdote as likely legendary
    embellishment rather than a reliable eyewitness account, while relying
    on Gregory's own Registrum for the verifiable fact of the 596 mission.

King Alfred. Preface to the Old English translation of the Regula
Pastoralis, c. 890s. Primary source for Alfred's own stated reasons for
choosing Gregory's text as one of the first works translated into Old
English as part of his program to rebuild English clerical literacy.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Markus, R. A. Gregory the Great and His World (1997). Standard modern
academic biography, source for the administrative and political detail
of Gregory's papacy (Patrimony management, Lombard diplomacy, relations
with Constantinople).

Modern musicological scholarship on Gregorian chant (see, e.g., overviews
in Grove Music Online and standard chant histories) establishing that the
mature Gregorian chant repertory developed gradually over the century
following Gregory's death, and that the "dictated by a dove" legend
postdates him by roughly two centuries. Consulted specifically to correct
the popular misattribution before including any claim about Gregory and
liturgical music.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The Archangel Michael/Castel Sant'Angelo vision. Not attested in any
source contemporary with Gregory; the chapter treats the 590 plague
procession itself as historical and the angelic vision specifically as
later legend, consistent with how the building came to be named.

The Bede slave-market anecdote (Non Angli sed Angeli). Written more than
a century after the fact by an author working from oral tradition and his
own narrative purposes. The chapter flags the triple-pun structure as a
signal of literary construction rather than verbatim record, while
treating the actual 596 mission (independently documented in Gregory's
own letters) as solid fact.

Gregory's authorship of Gregorian chant. A popular and long-standing
attribution that modern musicology does not support in anything like its
traditional form; the chapter credits him only with the verifiable
administrative work of organizing the schola cantorum.

The causal weight the chapter places on Gregory for later medieval
institutions (Charlemagne's coronation, the Crusades). This is this
book's own argument about necessary institutional preconditions, not a
claim that Gregory intended or foresaw either development — worded as
such in the chapter's closing paragraphs.


================================================================================
CHAPTER SIXTEEN — THE PROPHET AND THE POWER
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Theophanes the Confessor, Chronographia, early 9th century. Byzantine
source (writing well after the fact) for Heraclius's restoration of the
True Cross to Jerusalem in 630 and the Byzantine collapse in Syria that
followed. One of the sources through which the "Farewell, Syria" tradition
is transmitted, alongside earlier Arabic accounts.

The Quran, especially Surah 4 (An-Nisa), verse 157, and Surah 5 (Al-Ma'ida)
on the nature of God. Primary source for the chapter's description of
Jesus's status as prophet rather than divine, the rejection of the
Trinity as shirk, and the crucifixion-denial tradition.

The Covenant of Umar (also called Umar's Assurance), 637-638. The terms
under which Jerusalem's Christian population and churches were guaranteed
protection following the city's surrender, in exchange for a special tax
and subordinate legal status.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Kennedy, Hugh. The Great Arab Conquests: How the Spread of Islam Changed
the World We Live In (2007). Standard modern narrative history of the
Rashidun and Umayyad conquests, source for the pace and scale of the
expansion from Arabia to Spain and Sindh within roughly a century.

Fouracre, Paul, ed., and other modern treatments of the Battle of Tours
(732) noting that the battle's later reputation as the single engagement
that saved Christian Europe significantly outstrips its treatment in the
earliest surviving sources, and that other Umayyad setbacks in this same
period proved temporary rather than final.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The authenticity and origin of the "Farewell, Syria" quote attributed to
Heraclius. The earliest attestations appear in Arabic sources before
Byzantine ones, raising real doubt about whether it originated as a
Byzantine lament at all rather than being retrofitted onto Heraclius
later. The chapter flags this explicitly rather than presenting the line
as a verified quotation.

The Battle of Tours as a civilizational turning point. A view popularized
by later European historians (most influentially Edward Gibbon) reading
backward from a much later vantage point; modern historians treat the
engagement itself as comparatively modest and note that Umayyad expansion
elsewhere continued to ebb and flow well after 732 rather than stopping
at Tours specifically.

The exact circumstances of Umar's refusal to pray in the Church of the
Holy Sepulchre. The core episode (his declining Sophronius's invitation,
and his stated reasoning) is well attested across multiple traditions,
though as with most detailed anecdotes from this period the precise
wording attributed to Umar should be read as tradition rather than a
verbatim transcript.

The chapter's Marcion/Islam comparison is explicitly framed as thematic
resonance, not causal lineage or influence — there is no historical
connection between Marcion (2nd century Asia Minor) and the origins of
Islam (7th century Arabia), and the chapter states this directly rather
than implying descent.


================================================================================
CHAPTER SEVENTEEN — THE CROWN AND THE KEYS
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Einhard. Vita Karoli Magni (Life of Charlemagne), c. 817-833. The primary
source for Charlemagne's reported surprise and displeasure at the manner
of his own coronation, written by a courtier who knew him personally,
though within a generation of Charlemagne's own death rather than as a
strictly contemporary account of the coronation itself.

The Liber Pontificalis (Book of the Popes), contemporary entries for Leo
III. Source for the 799 attack on Leo, his flight to Charlemagne, the
December 800 council and Leo's oath of purgation, and the coronation
itself.

Theophanes the Confessor, Chronographia, early 9th century. Byzantine
source for Irene's blinding of Constantine VI in 797, her deposition in
802, and her exile and death on Lesbos, including the account of the
collapsed marriage negotiation with Charlemagne.

Royal Frankish Annals (Annales regni Francorum), contemporary Carolingian
court chronicle. Source for the 813 coronation of Louis the Pious at
Aachen without papal involvement.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

McKitterick, Rosamond. Charlemagne: The Formation of a European Identity
(2008). Standard modern scholarly biography; source for the "renovatio
Romani imperii" seal motto, the Aachen palace chapel's deliberate
modeling on San Vitale in Ravenna, and modern historians' skepticism
toward Einhard's claim of genuine surprise.

Modern treatments of the "translatio imperii" concept and the historians'
debate over whether 800 or 962 (Otto I's coronation) represents the
Holy Roman Empire's real founding moment, and on the term "Holy Roman
Empire" itself being a later medieval and early modern development
projected backward onto Charlemagne's reign.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

Whether Charlemagne's surprise at the coronation, as reported by Einhard,
was genuine or diplomatic theater. Modern historians widely doubt a
ceremony of that scale and scripted acclamation could have proceeded
without Charlemagne's foreknowledge; the chapter presents Einhard's
account while noting this skepticism directly rather than treating the
"surprise" as settled fact.

Whether "prima sedes a nemine iudicatur" was a genuine, previously
settled ecclesiastical principle by 800 or primarily a convenient
precedent revived under pressure. The chapter traces it to the Symmachus
affair of 501-502 under Theodoric, which is well documented, without
claiming the doctrine was uncontested or universally accepted by 800.

The founding date of the "Holy Roman Empire." Historians are genuinely
divided between 800 (Charlemagne) and 962 (Otto I) as the meaningful
founding moment, and the name itself postdates both events by centuries.
The chapter presents both dates and states the anachronism directly.


================================================================================
CHAPTER EIGHTEEN — BAREFOOT IN THE SNOW
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Gregory VII, Dictatus Papae, 1075. The 27-proposition document itself,
including the claims that the pope may depose emperors, may be judged
by no one, and alone may use the imperial insignia.

Gregory VII, Registrum (letters), including his own account of Henry's
three days at the Canossa gate and his later letters defending the
decision to absolve Henry against contemporary criticism that the delay
looked more like political calculation than pastoral mercy.

Lampert of Hersfeld, Annals, contemporary German chronicle. Major source
for the Diet of Tribur's year-and-a-day ultimatum, Henry's winter
crossing of the Alps (including Bertha and the child Conrad being
lowered down the ice in oxhide sledges), and the events at Canossa.

Bonizo of Sutri and other contemporary pro-Gregorian and pro-Henrician
chroniclers, whose accounts of Canossa and the following civil war
diverge in emphasis depending on which side each author supported.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Cowdrey, H. E. J. Pope Gregory VII, 1073-1085 (1998). Standard modern
scholarly biography; source for the political reading of Canossa as a
strategic success for Henry rather than simple humiliation, and for
Gregory's own awareness of the criticism this drew.

Robinson, I. S. Henry IV of Germany, 1056-1106 (1999). Standard modern
biography of Henry IV; source for the Diet of Tribur, the civil war with
Rudolf of Swabia, the 1084 capture of Rome and installation of Clement
III, and Henry's death and excommunicated status in 1106.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

Whether Henry's three days at the gate should be read primarily as
genuine religious penance or as calculated political theater. The
chapter presents both readings rather than picking one, on the grounds
that the evidence supports Henry having understood and intended the
political effect while very plausibly also believing in the religious
framework he was using to achieve it — the two are not mutually
exclusive and this book has found the same combination before.

The precise composition and even the authorship of the Dictatus Papae
document itself is debated among scholars, with some historians treating
it as a set of chapter headings or working notes rather than a finished,
circulated declaration. The chapter treats it as Gregory's own
articulation of his claims, which is the mainstream reading, without
claiming certainty about exactly how or to whom it was originally
disseminated.

Matilda of Tuscany's exact level of agency in brokering the January 1077
reconciliation (as opposed to simply hosting it) varies by source; the
chapter's claim about her broader military and financial support for
Gregory, both before and after Canossa, is well documented independent
of that specific question.

CHAPTER NINETEEN — THE DEAD MAN ON THE THRONE
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Liutprand of Cremona, Antapodosis, mid-10th century. The best-known
narrative account of the Cadaver Synod, including Stephen VI's direct
interrogation of the corpse ("why did you usurp the universal Roman
see..."), though written decades after the event by an author who
elsewhere in the same work is known to conflate different popes and
embellish for rhetorical effect.
    The chapter uses Liutprand for the interrogation quotation while
    flagging his distance from the events and treating dramatic
    embellishments (the earthquake) as legend rather than fact.

The Liber Pontificalis and papal correspondence establishing Formosus's
career: his 866 legation to Bulgaria under Nicholas I, Prince Boris's
request that he be made archbishop there, his excommunication under
John VIII and restoration under Marinus, his 891 election, the 892
coronation of Lambert of Spoleto, and the February 896 coronation of
Arnulf of Carinthia.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Duffy, Eamon. Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes (4th ed.,
2014). Standard popular-academic account of the Cadaver Synod and its
place in the broader collapse of papal authority in this period.

Herbermann et al., eds., the Catholic Encyclopedia entries on Formosus,
Stephen VI, Boniface VI, Theodore II, John IX, and Sergius III, cross-
checked against modern secondary sources for the sequence of popes and
councils between 896 and 904 (the annulment under Theodore II, the
burning of the acts under John IX, and Sergius III's reversal and demand
for re-ordination).

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The earthquake reportedly striking the Lateran during or after the
synod. This detail is not well attested in the earliest sources and
reads as exactly the kind of providential flourish later, more
devotional retellings like to add; the chapter presents it explicitly as
legend rather than an established fact.

The precise wording of Stephen VI's interrogation of the corpse comes
from Liutprand of Cremona, writing decades after the fact and with a
documented record of conflating details between different popes
elsewhere in the same work. The chapter attributes the quotation to him
by name rather than presenting it as a verified transcript.

Whether Sergius III was personally present as a judge at the original
896-897 Cadaver Synod is asserted in some sources and disputed in
others; the chapter notes this attribution rather than stating it as
certain.

CHAPTER TWENTY — THE PORNOCRACY
================================================================================

PRIMARY SOURCES

Liutprand of Cremona, Antapodosis, mid-10th century. The primary source
for nearly every salacious specific claim about Theodora and Marozia
(the alleged affair with John X, the alleged relationship with Sergius
III), written decades later with an openly hostile agenda. Also the
primary source for the circumstances of John XII's death.
    The chapter treats Liutprand's political/factual claims (family
    control of elections, John XI's parentage dispute, the 932 coup) as
    generally reliable in outline while flagging his most colorful and
    least corroborated specific details, consistent with how this book
    has used him in Chapter 19.

Flodoard of Reims, Annals, 10th century. Near-contemporary alternative
source naming Alberic I of Spoleto, not Sergius III, as John XI's
father — the basis for treating the paternity question as genuinely
disputed rather than settled.

The Liber Pontificalis, entries for the popes of this period, and
the Diploma Ottonianum (962) itself, signed by John XII the day after
crowning Otto I, granting the emperor formal authority to confirm future
papal elections.

MODERN SCHOLARSHIP

Modern historians of the "Rule of the Harlots"/Saeculum obscurum period
have increasingly argued that the "pornocracy" framing itself — coined
by 19th-century Protestant polemicists, not any contemporary source —
does real interpretive work to reduce two politically significant women
(Theodora and Marozia) to sexual scandal rather than treating their
documented political authority on its own terms. This is a general
trend across recent secondary literature on the period rather than one
scholar's individual argument, and no single named source should be
cited for it without independently verifying that source made the claim.

Duffy, Eamon. Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes (4th ed.,
2014). Consulted for the broader narrative arc from John VIII through
John XII and Otto I's intervention.

WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED

The paternity of Pope John XI (Sergius III vs. Alberic I of Spoleto) —
sources disagree and the chapter presents both without resolving it.

The reliability of Liutprand of Cremona as a source for Theodora's and
Marozia's personal conduct specifically. Modern historians increasingly
read his account as shaped by a documented hostile agenda and a
misogynistic framing of powerful women's political authority as sexual
impropriety; the chapter states this explicitly rather than repeating
his claims as settled fact.

The circumstances of Pope John XII's death — stroke, murder by a
jealous husband, or some combination — rest entirely on Liutprand's
account and cannot be independently verified.

The term "pornocracy" itself is a later coinage (19th century), not a
contemporary label; the chapter uses it as the title because it is the
term readers are most likely to already know, while noting its origin
and its interpretive baggage.

non-trivial claim against outside sources before pushing. The sources
consulted during that check are exactly what belongs in this file — capture
them then, while they are in front of you, rather than reconstructing them
later from the finished prose. Reconstructed citations are how fabricated
citations happen.

THE "WHERE THIS IS CONTESTED" HEADING IS NOT OPTIONAL. If a chapter has no
contested points, that is worth a sentence saying so explicitly. A chapter
that appears to have none has usually just not been checked hard enough.
