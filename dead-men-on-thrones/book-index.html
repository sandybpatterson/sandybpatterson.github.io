01 Before the Book
  • Ignatius of Antioch A bishop marched out of Antioch in chains around 107 and sentenced to die in a Roman arena. He spent the walk writing seven letters, and those letters accidentally document a Christianity that was not yet one institution. He could not point anyone to an agreed set of Christian writings, because none existed.
  • the archives What Ignatius's opponents in Philadelphia demanded — written proof from the Jewish scriptures before they would accept a claim. His answer is the hinge of the chapter: his archive is Jesus Christ. Not a book. A person, and the memory of what that person did.
  • Polycarp Bishop of Smyrna, taught by people who had known the apostles, and the closest living link the mid-second century had to Christian origins. Ignatius writes to him as an old man worrying about a younger one. Returns in Chapter 2 to curse Marcion to his face.
  • the codex The bound-book format that eventually made a single-volume Bible physically possible. A standard scroll ran to about five meters and couldn't hold more than a fraction of what four gospels would need; Christians adopted the codex earlier and more heavily than almost anyone else writing Greek in this period, but it was still new and immature in the year 100.
  • Justin Martyr A Christian philosopher writing in Rome in the mid-150s. His offhand description of an ordinary Sunday — "the memoirs of the apostles" read aloud alongside the prophets — is the earliest evidence of certain texts becoming scripture in practice, decades before anyone listed which ones.
  • Muratorian Fragment A battered Latin manuscript, conventionally dated around 180, usually called the first attempt at a canon list. Its date is disputed — some scholars place it two centuries later — and either way it shows an argument in progress, not a settled canon.
  • Walter Bauer The German scholar behind the strong claim that in many regions what later got labeled heresy came first, and orthodoxy arrived afterward and rewrote the record of who was original. Most historians think he overstated it. Almost all of them accept the floor underneath it.
  • Tatian and the Diatessaron Around the 170s, Tatian solved the "which gospel" problem in Syria by weaving all four into one continuous narrative. For generations, Syriac-speaking Christians read that composite — not the four separate books — in church.
  • Gospel of Thomas A list of sayings attributed to Jesus with almost no narrative at all, circulating in Egypt and Syria with real readers who took it seriously. Recovered complete at Nag Hammadi in 1945. Returns in Chapter 3.
  • Phoebe, Prisca, and Junia Three women Paul names in his own letters — a deacon, a teacher, and one "outstanding among the apostles." Later centuries spent considerable effort explaining them away. The chapter's evidence that the early plurality was a kind of freedom, not only a problem.
  • the agape feast The shared meal where a stranger arriving in Corinth or Philippi was fed by people who had decided, with no institution instructing them, that the newcomer belonged.
  • canon A closed, fixed, authoritative list. The concept did not exist for Christian use during the period this chapter covers. That absence is the chapter's whole subject.
02 The Man Who Forced the Question
  • Marcion of Sinope A wealthy shipowner from the Black Sea coast who arrived in Rome around 139 or 140, handed the church two hundred thousand sesterces, and was excommunicated in 144 for teaching that the God of the Hebrew scriptures and the God of Jesus were two different beings. The organizing figure of this entire book.
  • the Demiurge Marcion's name for the god who made the material world — real, powerful, responsible for its law and its cruelty, and emphatically not the highest god. Above him sat a second, purely good God, a stranger to creation, who sent Jesus to liberate humanity from the first one's law.
  • Gospel and Apostle The two halves of Marcion's collection: one edited gospel resembling Luke, and ten edited letters of Paul. The first closed Christian scripture anyone has record of, roughly a century before the wider church produced its own.
  • the Antitheses Marcion's treatise laying the contradictions between the two gods and the two testaments side by side in columns, so a reader couldn't miss the argument. Lost, like everything else he wrote.
  • Tertullian Writing against Marcion around 207, half a century after his death, and the fullest surviving source on what Marcion actually taught. His hostility is why the material survives at all, and why it has to be read carefully.
  • Epiphanius A fourth-century bishop who compiled an enormous catalogue of heresies, and the only source for the story that Marcion was expelled from his father's church for seducing a virgin — more than two hundred years after the fact, with no earlier writer mentioning it. Returns in Chapter 7.
  • "the firstborn of Satan" What Polycarp said when Marcion asked whether he recognized him. Not an argument — a curse, from the church's most direct living link to the apostolic generation. The behavior of an institution that knew it was losing ground.
  • Adolf von Harnack The German scholar whose early-twentieth-century study credited Marcion with inventing the idea of a closed New Testament and forcing the wider church to answer in kind. The strong version of this book's own thesis.
  • Judith Lieu Author of the most important modern study of Marcion (2015), and the direct counterweight to Harnack. Her argument: nearly everything known about Marcion comes from hostile writers with every incentive to make him look uniquely dangerous, and canon formation was slower and messier than one shipowner forcing the church's hand.
  • Matthias Klinghardt and Markus Vinzent Two scholars who ask whether the knife went the other direction — whether Marcion's gospel was an earlier text the canonical gospels drew on, rather than an edited Luke. A minority position, and a live one.
  • Eznik of Kolb An Armenian writer still arguing against Marcionite communities in the fifth century, roughly three hundred years after the excommunication. Evidence that the movement Rome expelled outlasted the western Roman Empire.
03 The Lost Document
  • Papias of Hierapolis A bishop in the first decades of the second century who did something nobody else thought to do: went looking for where the stories about Jesus had actually come from. His five volumes are lost. What survives is a handful of quotations, including the Elder's remark about Matthew compiling sayings "in the Hebrew dialect." Eusebius later called him a man "of exceedingly small intelligence" — though he may be quoting Papias's own joke about himself.
  • the Synoptic Problem The puzzle of why Matthew, Mark, and Luke overlap so closely — sometimes word for word in Greek — and why they diverge exactly where they do. "Synoptic" is Greek for "seen together," which is literally what solving it requires.
  • Markan priority The widely accepted conclusion that Mark was written first and that both Matthew and Luke had a copy in front of them. It resolves most of the puzzle. It does not resolve all of it.
  • Q (Quelle) German for "source." The hypothetical document behind the roughly two hundred verses Matthew and Luke share that appear nowhere in Mark. It has never been found, no ancient writer ever mentions it, and it remains the dominant explanation anyway — an inference, not a discovery. First proposed by Christian Hermann Weisse in 1838, largely ignored until Heinrich Julius Holtzmann endorsed it in 1863.
  • the four-document hypothesis B. H. Streeter's 1924 extension: Matthew and Luke each drew on Mark, Q, and further material unique to each of them — M for Matthew, L for Luke — neither ever proposed as a single shared document the way Q was.
  • sayings gospel A list of things Jesus reportedly said with almost no story attached. Q, reconstructed, would have been one. So is the Gospel of Thomas. Two communities preserving teaching in the same bare form, before anyone decided a gospel had to be a narrative ending at an empty tomb.
  • Kloppenborg's stratified Q John Kloppenborg's 1987 argument that Q itself shows signs of distinct compositional layers, meaning it had its own editorial history rather than arriving whole. Kloppenborg himself warned against treating the oldest layer as automatically the most historically reliable.
  • the Farrer hypothesis The serious minority solution that needs no lost document: Mark wrote first, Matthew used Mark, and Luke used both. On this view the shared source isn't lost. It's Matthew.
04 Four Accounts, One Story
  • Celsus A Greek philosopher who wrote the first surviving sustained outside attack on Christianity around 177. His treatise is lost; it survives only because Origen quoted it at length in order to answer it. What bothered him most was that the four resurrection accounts don't match.
  • Contra Celsum Origen's point-by-point rebuttal, written roughly seventy years later. His defense was not that the gospels agree. It was that four identical accounts would be the real red flag — an argument still in use eighteen hundred years later.
  • the census problem Matthew places the birth under Herod the Great, who died in 4 BC. Luke ties it to the census under Quirinius, independently dated by Josephus to 6 AD. Not a stylistic mismatch — two starting dates for the same event, a decade apart, with no proposed fix reaching consensus.
  • the two genealogies Matthew and Luke both trace Jesus to David through Joseph and list almost entirely different names doing it, through different sons of David, with different generation counts. Presented in both gospels, without qualification, as Joseph's actual paternal ancestry.
  • the raised saints of Matthew 27 Dead holy people walking out of opened tombs into Jerusalem and appearing to many. Two verses, one gospel, never referenced again by anyone — including the three evangelists describing the same afternoon.
  • the Lazarus silence In John, raising a man four days dead is the event that convinces the authorities Jesus has to die. Matthew, Mark, and Luke never mention it. Not the miracle, not the man, not the plot it supposedly triggered.
  • Mark 16:8 Where the earliest and best manuscripts of Mark stop. The women find the tomb empty, are told he has risen, and flee. "And they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid." No resurrected Jesus appears on the page. Someone added an ending later.
  • the seven last words A composite. No single gospel gives Jesus more than three sentences from the cross, and they don't overlap. The cry of abandonment appears only in the two earliest accounts and quietly disappears as the gospels get later and the portrait gets calmer.
  • Simon of Cyrene Pulled from the crowd and forced to carry the cross in Matthew, Mark, and Luke. Absent from John, where Jesus goes out "bearing his own cross" — a small detail fitting John's larger portrait of a Jesus who is never a passive victim of anything.
05 The Cosmic Gospel
  • "Before Abraham was, I am" The sentence that ends the argument, and the reason the crowd picks up stones. Not "I existed before Abraham" — the bare present-tense phrase God used to name himself to Moses. Nobody in that temple needed it explained.
  • the Logos The Word: a cosmic principle present with God before creation, through whom all things were made, who at one particular moment became flesh. John's prologue opens where Genesis opens and puts a second figure inside the sentence.
  • Philo of Alexandria A Jewish philosopher writing in Greek in roughly the same decades as Jesus's ministry, who had already fused the Stoic idea of a world-ordering logos with Hebrew scripture's creative "word of the Lord." Whether John read him, absorbed him secondhand, or arrived somewhere similar independently is unsettled.
  • ego eimi "I am." The formula John repeats across the whole gospel — the bread of life, the light of the world, the good shepherd, the resurrection and the life, the true vine — training the reader to hear it, until it finally arrives in the temple with no metaphor attached at all.
  • the farewell discourse Four chapters of monologue after the last supper, unlike anything in the other three gospels, in which Jesus prepares his followers for his own absence.
  • the vine and the branches John 15. Real intimacy — "I have called you friends" — wired directly to continuous monitored productivity, framed by the threat of being cut off and burned if the fruit doesn't come. The book returns to this passage in Chapter 56 on clinical rather than literary terms.
  • Thomas The one disciple who says out loud that he isn't convinced, and holds out for the same evidence everyone else in the room was already given. He gets it, and then gives the highest confession of Jesus's identity anywhere in the gospel. The book's conclusion returns to him.
  • aposynagogos "Put out of the synagogue." The term behind John's three references to believers being expelled, and the evidence that this community understood itself as painfully and recently separated from the Jewish world it grew out of.
  • J. Louis Martyn and Adele Reinhartz Martyn tied the expulsion language to a specific late-first-century synagogue curse and read John's community as recent refugees from a formal split. Reinhartz leads the pushback, reading it as the gospel's own rhetoric defining insider and outsider. What survives the argument is the rupture itself.
06 The Man Who Built the Theology
  • the Damascus road Told three times in Acts, differently each time — who heard the voice, who saw the light, who fell. One author, one book, no attempt to reconcile them. Whatever happened, the writer wasn't working from a transcript.
  • Acts versus the letters Most of what people think they know about Paul comes from Acts, written by someone else decades later, shaping him into the hero of its own narrative. What Paul said about himself survives separately, in his own voice. The distinction runs through the whole chapter.
  • the seven undisputed letters Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon. The letters nearly all scholars accept as Paul's own.
  • the pastorals 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus. Most critical scholars think they were written after Paul's death by followers using his authority to settle questions he never lived to face. Writing in a revered teacher's name wasn't fraud in the ancient world — but it means two different Pauls sit inside the same New Testament.
  • E. P. Sanders The scholar whose 1977 study broke a five-hundred-year reading. Working through surviving Jewish literature from Paul's own period, he found that Second Temple Judaism was not a religion of earning salvation by a ledger of deeds at all.
  • covenantal nomism Sanders's term: a Jew was already inside the covenant by grace, born into it, and keeping the law was the response to belonging rather than the price of admission. If that's right, "works of the law" wasn't a swipe at moral effort — it meant the boundary markers that decided who counted as inside.
  • the New Perspective on Paul The movement Sanders launched, which reframed Paul's argument from individual guilt to Gentile inclusion. Not universally accepted. But its critics now have to argue with Sanders rather than around him.
  • "an epistle of straw" What Luther called the Epistle of James in his 1522 preface, because James says a person is justified by works "and not by faith alone" — the only place in the Bible that phrase appears, and it is there to be rejected. Luther removed the line from later editions. He left James at the back of his German New Testament anyway.
  • Galatians 3:28 "Neither Jew nor Greek... neither slave nor free... no male and female." One of the most radically egalitarian sentences in the ancient world, and quite possibly a baptismal formula already in use before Paul quoted it.
  • 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 "The women should keep silent in the churches." The passage wanders to a different location in part of the manuscript tradition — the signature of a later note absorbed into the text — and sits three chapters after Paul gives instructions for how women should pray and prophesy aloud in the assembly.
  • the closing clock Paul expected the world to end within his own lifetime, and included himself among those who would still be alive to see it. His instructions were triage for a movement he thought had no next generation. Later centuries treated them as binding forever.
07 The Brilliant Heretic
  • Origen of Alexandria Seventeen when his father was beheaded and the family's property seized, running the city's catechetical school not long after, and for the next forty years the most productive mind the early church produced. Tortured at nearly seventy, condemned three centuries after his death, and indispensable to the tradition that condemned him.
  • Leonides Origen's father, arrested in Alexandria in 202 and executed. Origen wanted to die with him. His mother hid his clothes, and a teenager with nothing to wear stayed home.
  • the Hexapla Origen's monumental parallel Old Testament, laying the Hebrew text and four Greek translations side by side in columns so a reader could see exactly where the versions diverged. No complete copy survived antiquity.
  • On First Principles The first systematic Christian theology — God, the soul, free will, and the end of all things, worked through with a rigor nobody in the movement had attempted before.
  • the three levels Origen's method: reading scripture literally, morally, and spiritually at once. It is the tool that let Christian intellectuals keep the entire Hebrew Bible — everything Marcion wanted cut — without defending every verse as plain fact. Augustine used it. The medieval church built systems on it.
  • apokatastasis The eventual restoration of all rational beings to God. Everyone. Possibly including the devil. A God whose punishments corrected rather than excluded permanently. The most hopeful claim anyone in this book makes, and the one the institution spent three centuries deciding could not be allowed to stand.
  • the castration story Reported by Eusebius, who admired him, and doubted by scholars since the nineteenth century — because the man's entire life's work was a machine for turning literal readings into symbolic ones. A different kind of source problem from a hostile witness inventing something: an admiring source passing on something he heard rather than saw.
  • the Decian persecution The empire-wide campaign launched in 250 that put Origen, by then nearly seventy, under torture designed to break a prisoner without killing him. He didn't recant. He didn't recover either.
08 Closing the Book
  • Athanasius Bishop of Alexandria, driven out of his own see five separate times by five different emperors, who in 367 buried inside a routine letter about the date of Easter the earliest surviving list of exactly the twenty-seven books of the New Testament, in the order still printed today.
  • the Thirty-Ninth Festal Letter That letter. Not the moment the canon was decided and not the moment the argument started — the first time anyone wrote the final answer down in one place and made a point of saying so.
  • the Shepherd of Hermas A long, strange, visionary work about repentance that had nothing to do with any apostle, claimed no connection to one, and was read as scripture in real churches for generations. Copied into Codex Sinaiticus right after Revelation with no visual signal that a reader had crossed a boundary.
  • the Didache "The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles" — a practical manual on baptism, fasting, the Eucharist, and how to spot a traveling apostle angling for a free meal. Not condemned, just no longer copied, and lost for a millennium until a single copy turned up in a Constantinople monastery library in 1873. A text read into obsolescence.
  • Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Alexandrinus Two of the great luxury Bibles of the ancient world, and evidence of what their scribes thought belonged: Sinaiticus carries the Shepherd and Barnabas, Alexandrinus carries 1 and 2 Clement, in both cases bound in without apology.
  • Dionysius of Alexandria A third-century bishop who picked Revelation apart on pure grounds of Greek style and argued its author could not possibly be the author of John's gospel. Modern scholars still find the argument largely persuasive. The book kept its traditional attribution anyway.
  • Hebrews Brilliant, clearly important, unmistakably early, and anonymous — it never names its author. Asked who wrote it, Origen's surviving answer is the closest thing to an admission of defeat anyone in this book puts in writing: only God knows.
  • 2 Peter The book that squeaked in by the narrowest margin — last among all twenty-seven in early patristic citation, sitting in Eusebius's "disputed" pile, reading nothing like 1 Peter. Jerome noticed the problem and accepted it anyway, and the doubts went quiet. Nobody found new evidence. The most credentialed person in the room decided the argument was over.
  • Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) Regional North African synods, not meetings of the whole church, which ratified what individual bishops and ordinary congregations had already mostly agreed on. Real authority in the West, none in the East. Popular retellings routinely inflate them into the council that decided the Bible.
  • what actually decided it Not a single test applied consistently by a tribunal. A rough convergence of imperfect ones — a plausible claim to apostolic origin, broad use across many regions rather than passionate use in one, and consistency with a theological consensus that had hardened out of two centuries of argument.
09 The Emperor's Church
  • the Milvian Bridge 28 October 312. Maxentius put the river at his back, rigged a pontoon crossing as a fallback, and drowned in his own armor when it gave way under his own retreating army. Constantine rode into Rome the next day carrying his brother-in-law's head on a spear.
  • Lactantius A Christian writer with connections at Constantine's court, writing within a decade of the battle, and the earliest and most modest account of the vision: a dream, the night before, instructing Constantine to mark his soldiers' shields.
  • the chi-rho The monogram formed by overlaying the first two Greek letters of "Christ." What the dream told him to put on the shields.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea The church historian, and the reason the vision story can be watched growing. Writing while Constantine lived, he mentions divine assistance and no vision. Writing after Constantine died, he has the entire army seeing a cross of light above the sun in daylight, with words beside it. He says he had it from the emperor himself, under oath.
  • "the Edict of Milan" A document that does not exist. No edict by that name has ever been found and none was issued at Milan. What survives is a letter Licinius sent from Nicomedia two months later — preserved because Lactantius copied it out — restoring confiscated property and guaranteeing everyone the right to follow whatever religion they chose.
  • Galerius and the Edict of Serdica The persecutor who ended the persecution first, in 311, dying and trying to be less unpopular than he had lived. Constantine gets the credit history remembers.
  • pontifex maximus Chief priest of Rome's traditional state cults — a title Constantine held for his entire reign, never renounced, never quietly dropped, while simultaneously funding churches. He left no evidence he saw a contradiction worth resolving.
  • the Arch of Constantine Dedicated by the Senate in 315, crediting the victory to guidance "instinctu divinitatis" — the divinity, singular, deliberately unnamed — and carrying a relief of Sol Invictus, a cult Constantine kept minting onto his own coins for another decade or more. Evidence the belief-versus-calculation question doesn't resolve as cleanly as either side would like.
  • the Donatists A North African faction who refused to accept a bishop because one of the men who ordained him had handed scriptures over to be burned during the persecution. The question underneath — whether a sacrament performed by an unworthy man is still a real sacrament — resurfaces under different names for the next thousand years.
  • Arles, 314 The council Constantine convened, with bishops from as far as Gaul and Britain, to settle the Donatist dispute. He gained nothing from it and pursued it anyway — and established the exact template he would use a decade later at Nicaea.
  • Crispus and Fausta Constantine's eldest son and his wife, dead within weeks of each other in 326, on his orders, with no official explanation ever issued and none surviving. Every account of motive is later speculation, and the most lurid one comes from a hostile pagan historian writing more than a century afterward.
  • Helena and the True Cross Constantine's mother, who made a real, documented pilgrimage to Jerusalem around 326–328. The story that she personally discovered Christ's actual cross is absent from every contemporary source and first appears decades after her death — the relic resurfaces, still very much a going concern, three centuries later in Chapter 16.
  • Constantinople Dedicated in 330 on the site of Byzantium, chosen for hard strategic reasons rather than religious ones. He called it New Rome. Everyone else was already calling it Constantinople within his lifetime. It outlasted the western empire by close to a thousand years.
  • Eusebius of Nicomedia The bishop who baptized Constantine on his deathbed in 337 — not the historian, a different Eusebius, and specifically a prominent defender of the theological position the church had gathered at Nicaea twelve years earlier to condemn.
10 One Letter's Difference
  • Paphnutius of Thebes An Egyptian bishop at Nicaea whose right eye had been gouged out and whose hamstrings had been cut during the Great Persecution. Constantine reportedly summoned him to the palace to kiss the ruined socket — a detail sourced to a single writer a century later and worth holding a little loosely, unlike the underlying scene it illustrates.
  • Constantine's entrance at Nicaea By Eusebius's own admiring account, the emperor entered last, after every bishop had already taken his seat, glittering in purple and gold as the room rose for him — the same author already caught elsewhere exaggerating Constantine's battlefield vision, so the flourish is worth a grain of salt even though the underlying deference isn't in dispute.
  • Arius A presbyter in Alexandria who taught, with real logical elegance, that the Son was generated by the Father and therefore could not be co-eternal with him — a creature, however exalted, rather than an identical share of the same uncreated being. Recalled from exile by Constantine in 335, dead before his restoration to communion could take place in 336.
  • the Thalia Arius's theology set to verse, with separate songs reportedly written for sailors, millers, and travelers, pitched to tunes they already knew. An early and effective use of popular music as a doctrinal delivery mechanism.
  • homoousios and homoiousios "Same substance" and "similar substance" — one Greek letter, iota, the entire distance between a Son who is fully God and a Son who is merely the highest and best approximation of one.
  • the Nicene Creed The compressed, memorizable formula the council produced, built almost entirely out of clauses aimed at Arius's specific claims.
  • the dating of Easter A second, non-Christological decision the same council made: fixing the resurrection's feast day permanently to a Sunday, overruling churches that had kept it on the same calendar date as the Jewish Passover regardless of weekday.
  • Athanasius Returns from Chapter 8. Deposed and exiled by Constantine himself in 335 — the first of the five exiles already mentioned — not by some later, less sympathetic emperor.
  • Eusebius of Nicomedia Returns from Chapter 9. Spent a decade working the imperial court to rebuild sympathy for Arius, using connections among the women of Constantine's household, and baptized the dying emperor in 337 — undoing, from the inside, the outcome of the council Constantine himself had convened.
  • Constantius II Constantine's son and successor in the East. Never a strict Arian himself, but hostile to the Nicene settlement and to Athanasius personally. Persecuted, within a single generation, the exact Christians his father had gaveled into orthodoxy.
11 Making It Mandatory
  • the Battle of Adrianople 9 August 378. Valens led roughly thirty thousand men against the Goths and lost so completely his body was never recovered. Left the eastern empire without an emperor, without an army, and with a Gothic force loose in the Balkans that nobody was left to stop.
  • Theodosius I A Spanish general recalled from retirement after his own father was executed two years earlier under murky court politics. Made emperor of the East to clean up a catastrophe he had no part in causing, and went on to make Nicene Christianity the law of the Roman Empire.
  • the Edict of Thessalonica 27 February 380. Not toleration and not mere favor — the first Roman law naming one specific version of Christian belief and ordering every subject to hold it, branding everyone else "foolish madmen." Its actual target was intra-Christian, not paganism, whatever the popular version of this story says.
  • the Serapeum Alexandria's great temple of Serapis, desecrated and demolished by Bishop Theophilus's mob in 391. A soldier struck the cult statue with an axe. The sky did not fall.
  • the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed The expanded creed produced at the First Council of Constantinople in 381, adding language on the Holy Spirit that Nicaea's original text had left almost entirely unaddressed. The version still recited in churches today, under a name almost nobody reciting it has ever heard.
  • foederati The status Theodosius negotiated for the Goths in 382 — settled inside imperial territory, self-governing, obligated to fight for Rome when called on. A pragmatic solution from the same man who, in religious matters, had just decided pragmatism had no place at all.
  • Ambrose of Milan The bishop who denied Theodosius communion for months after the Massacre of Thessalonica in 390, until the emperor performed public penance. Proof that making Christianity mandatory had handed the church real leverage over the throne it depended on.
12 The Creed as Weapon
  • Rufinus of Aquileia A scholar and translator who, writing a creed commentary around 404, gave later generations the legend that each of the twelve apostles contributed one article of the creed in turn. His own broader scholarship is the reason we know the story isn't literally true.
  • the Old Roman Creed The baptismal formula behind what became the Apostles' Creed, taking shape at Rome over the second century and reaching something close to its classic form around 180 — in the specific decades right after Marcion built a rival church around his own fixed, edited canon.
  • the Symbolum Latin for "token" — the kind of broken coin or tile two parties would each keep half of to prove their identity later. Applied to the creed: not a devotional poem, a password, matched clause by clause against a candidate standing in the baptismal water.
  • "maker of heaven and earth" The creed's opening clause, and the whole argument of Chapter 2 undone in five words: one God, both Father and creator, where Marcion had insisted on two.
  • "descended into hell" The one clause in the creed that isn't as old as the rest — absent from the earliest surviving text (337), first mentioned by Rufinus himself, and not universal until roughly 650.
13 The Ghost in the Machine
  • Ulfilas Consecrated a bishop around 341 and sent north of the Danube to evangelize the Goths, the people his own grandparents had reportedly been taken captive from. Built the first Gothic alphabet and used it to translate most of the Bible into Gothic, deliberately skipping the Books of Kings.
  • the Codex Argenteus The one surviving substantial copy of Ulfilas's Gothic Bible — a deluxe sixth-century edition in silver and gold ink on purple vellum, made generations after his death, almost certainly for Theodoric's own court in Ravenna. Now held at Uppsala University in Sweden.
  • Eusebius of Nicomedia Returns from Chapters 9 and 10. The same bishop who rehabilitated Arius at court and baptized the dying Constantine personally ordained Ulfilas and sent him north — handing an anti-Nicene theology directly to a people the empire couldn't easily correct once it changed its mind.
  • Homoian Christianity The more historically precise name for what Ulfilas actually taught the Goths — not Arius's own theology exactly, but a related formula holding the Son merely "like" the Father, without Nicaea's homoousios or Arius's sharper subordinationism.
  • Constantius II Returns from Chapter 10. Constantine's son, whose anti-Nicene sympathies were still shaping imperial religious policy in the 340s, at exactly the moment Eusebius of Nicomedia sent Ulfilas across the Danube.
  • Clovis King of the Franks, converted directly to Nicene Catholicism — traditionally dated to Christmas Day, 496, though the exact year is disputed — skipping the Arian phase nearly every other major Germanic power passed through. Baptized at Reims by Bishop Remigius.
  • Huneric Vandal king of North Africa from 477, who ran the harshest campaign of anti-Catholic persecution of any Arian Germanic ruler — exiling clergy, seizing churches, and issuing an edict in 484 that openly recycled Rome's own old anti-heretical laws against the Catholics it now ruled.
  • Victor of Vita A Catholic bishop who lived through Huneric's persecution and wrote it down in detail, preserving the only substantial surviving account — written by a man with every reason to make the persecution look as total as he could.
  • Theodoric the Ostrogoth Arian king who ruled Catholic-majority Italy from 493 to 526, for most of that span protecting Catholic worship rather than suppressing it. Ended his reign by executing the philosopher Boethius and his father-in-law Symmachus on suspicion of treason historians still can't fully verify.
  • the Third Council of Toledo Convened in 589, where the Visigothic king Reccared formally renounced Arianism on behalf of his entire kingdom — the last major Germanic Arian monarchy to give the position up, two hundred and eight years after Theodosius first made Nicene Christianity Roman law.
14 Into the Vacuum
  • Romulus Augustulus The last Western Roman emperor by popular reckoning — sixteen years old, deposed by Odoacer on 4 September 476 without a fight, and pensioned off to a villa near Naples rather than killed. Most modern historians actually count someone else as the last legitimate holder of the title.
  • Odoacer The Germanic officer who deposed Romulus Augustulus and declined to become emperor himself, sending the imperial regalia to Constantinople instead. Received the rank of patrician from Zeno in exchange, and kept striking coins in Julius Nepos's name for years afterward.
  • Julius Nepos The emperor Romulus Augustulus's own father had deposed to make room for his son — still recognized as legitimate emperor by Constantinople even after 476, and murdered at his own country house in 480. The technically correct answer to "who was the last Western Roman emperor."
  • the cursus publicus Rome's imperial relay-station network for messages, officials, and requisitioned horses, run on a fixed guaranteed schedule for half a millennium. Simply stopped existing in the West once nobody was left to enforce the local compulsion it depended on.
  • Sidonius Apollinaris A Gallo-Roman aristocrat and former urban prefect of Rome who became bishop of Clermont in 469 and organized the city's actual physical defense against Euric's Visigoths from 473 to 475, then was exiled by the king he'd fought once Rome ceded the city anyway.
  • Severinus of Noricum A monk on the upper Danube frontier who spent the decades after 453 organizing food distribution and negotiating directly with Germanic warlords for the release of Roman captives — moral standing functioning, in practice, as government. Died in 482.
15 The Monk Who Made the Papacy
  • Gregory the Great Prefect of Rome by 573, then a monk, then reluctant pope from 590 to 604. Ran the church's vast estates like a state, negotiated directly with the Lombards over the emperor's own objections, and wrote the training manual future bishops would carry for the next thousand years.
  • the Patrimony of Saint Peter The Roman church's own accumulated agricultural estates across Sicily, southern Italy, Sardinia, Corsica, Dalmatia, and North Africa — administered by Gregory with the granular attention of a finance ministry.
  • the "Fool" letter Emperor Maurice's lost 595 letter accusing Gregory of treason and folly for negotiating peace with the Lombards without imperial permission. Gregory's own reply didn't deny the charge.
  • the Regula Pastoralis Gregory's handbook for bishops, written in the early 590s, recommended to Charlemagne's own bishops in 813 and translated into Old English by King Alfred in the 890s.
  • the Anglii/angeli story Bede's century-later account of Gregory punning on enslaved English boys in a Roman market — "not Angles, but angels." Almost certainly embellished; what's independently documented is Gregory's actual 596 dispatch of Augustine's mission to England.
  • servus servorum Dei "Servant of the servants of God" — the title Gregory adopted to rebuke the patriarch of Constantinople for calling himself "ecumenical." Still used by every pope since.
16 The Prophet and the Power
  • Heraclius Byzantine emperor who reversed total defeat by Persia, recovering the True Cross and restoring it to Jerusalem in a personal triumphal procession in 630 — six years before an entirely new power broke Byzantine authority in the same territory for good.
  • the Battle of Yarmouk Fought in 636 in Syria, the battle that broke Byzantine military power in the Levant so completely it never recovered there.
  • the Hijra Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina in 622, marking the moment his movement became a governing political community. Chosen as year one of the Islamic calendar for exactly that reason.
  • the Pentarchy The five ancient patriarchal sees — Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. Three of the five passed permanently out of Christian political control within a decade of Heraclius's own triumph in the same region.
  • the Covenant of Umar The terms Caliph Umar set for Jerusalem's surrender in 637–638, guaranteeing the city's churches and Christian residents protection. Umar himself declined to pray inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.
  • the Battle of Tours Fought in 732 near Poitiers, where a Frankish force under Charles Martel turned back an Umayyad raiding expedition. Later inflated by European historians into the battle that saved Christian civilization.
17 The Crown and the Keys
  • Charlemagne King of the Franks, crowned Emperor of the Romans by Pope Leo III on Christmas Day, 800. Had already been styling himself Rome's heir in stone, at his own palace chapel in Aachen, years before any pope made it official.
  • Pope Leo III Survived an assassination attempt by Roman nobles in 799, fled to Charlemagne for protection, cleared himself of the charges against him by personal oath, and two days later crowned his protector emperor.
  • prima sedes a nemine iudicatur "The first see is judged by no one" — the doctrine Leo's 800 synod leaned on, first established three centuries earlier when Pope Symmachus faced almost the same situation under Theodoric the Ostrogoth.
  • Empress Irene Sole ruler of Byzantium from 797, having blinded her own son to keep power. Her sex, not the blinding, was what Western writers cited as disqualifying her — the pretext that left Rome's throne "vacant" enough to fill with Charlemagne.
  • renovatio Romani imperii "The renewal of the Roman empire" — the motto on Charlemagne's own official seal, and the ambition his court had already been building toward before Leo III's coronation made it official.
  • the 813 Aachen coronation Charlemagne's own attempt to crown his son Louis co-emperor without any pope involved. Louis was crowned a second time anyway, by Pope Stephen IV in 816.
18 Barefoot in the Snow
  • Henry IV King of the Germans, excommunicated by Gregory VII in 1076. Crossed the Alps in winter with his wife and young son to reach the pope first, stood barefoot in the snow at Canossa for three days in January 1077, and turned apparent humiliation into a political victory.
  • the Dictatus Papae Twenty-seven blunt propositions Gregory VII circulated in 1075 asserting papal supremacy in the starkest terms yet — that the pope could depose emperors and could be judged by no one.
  • Matilda of Tuscany Countess who hosted the Canossa reconciliation and was Gregory VII's single most important secular ally — financing his campaigns, leading her own troops in armor, and willing her inheritance to the Roman church.
  • the Diet of Tribur The October 1076 assembly that gave Henry his year-and-a-day ultimatum to obtain absolution or lose his throne.
  • Rudolf of Swabia The rival king German princes elected in March 1077 after concluding Canossa hadn't settled anything. Killed in battle in October 1080.
  • the Concordat of Worms The 1122 agreement that finally ended the investiture fight, decades after both Henry and Gregory were dead — splitting a bishop's office between church and king.
19 The Dead Man on the Throne
  • Pope Formosus Bishop of Porto, then pope from 891 to 896. Crowned two rival emperors in successive years trying to escape Spoleto family control; died naturally in April 896, nine months before his own corpse was put on trial.
  • the Cadaver Synod The January 897 trial convened by Pope Stephen VI, in which Formosus's exhumed body was dressed in papal vestments, propped on the papal throne, convicted, mutilated, and thrown in the Tiber.
  • Stephen VI The pope who ordered the Cadaver Synod, closely aligned with the Spoleto family. Deposed and imprisoned within months; strangled to death before the year was out.
  • Ageltrude and the House of Spoleto The family whose decades-long effort to control the papacy as a dynastic asset drove both Formosus's coronations under duress and Stephen VI's posthumous revenge trial.
  • Sergius III Pope from 904, reportedly present as a judge at the original Cadaver Synod, who later reversed Formosus's rehabilitation and demanded re-ordination of the bishops he'd consecrated.
20 The Pornocracy
  • Theophylact and Theodora A Roman aristocrat who held the title of vestararius, the papacy's own treasurer, and his wife, a senatrix in her own right. Between them and their daughter, the family controlled who became pope for two generations.
  • Marozia Daughter of Theophylact and Theodora, mother of a pope, and effective ruler of Rome — until her own son overthrew her at her own third wedding in 932 and kept her imprisoned for life.
  • Pope John X Installed in 914, a genuinely capable pope who personally led a coalition army against Saracen raiders in 915. Smothered in prison in 928 on Marozia's orders once her own son needed the papacy cleared for him.
  • Alberic II Marozia's son, who deposed his own mother mid-wedding in 932 and ruled Rome directly as a secular prince for twenty-two years, controlling five papal elections without ever holding the office himself.
  • Pope John XII Alberic II's son, installed as pope around age eighteen in 955. Crowned Otto I emperor in 962, then conspired against him; deposed in 963, briefly regained the office by force, and died within months.
  • the Diploma Ottonianum The charter John XII himself signed the day after crowning Otto I, granting the emperor authority to confirm future papal elections — later turned against John himself.
21 The Babylonian Captivity
  • the Outrage of Anagni The September 1303 assault on Pope Boniface VIII by forces loyal to Philip IV of France and the Colonna family, who held him for three days in his own family palace.
  • Pope Clement V Elected 1305, crowned in Lyon, never set foot in Rome. Settled the papacy permanently in Avignon in 1309.
  • the Avignon Papacy The nearly seventy-year period (1309–1377) during which seven successive popes resided in Avignon rather than Rome.
  • Petrarch The Italian poet and scholar who, around 1350, coined "the Babylon of the west" for Avignon. Martin Luther reused the "Babylonian Captivity" framing in a 1520 treatise of his own.
  • Pope Gregory XI The last Avignon pope, who returned the papacy to Rome in January 1377, driven by some combination of Catherine of Siena's appeals and the War of the Eight Saints.
  • Catherine of Siena A Dominican tertiary and mystic whose forceful letters and personal visit urged Gregory XI back to Rome. Later canonized and named a doctor of the church.
  • the War of the Eight Saints Florence's 1375–1378 revolt against Papal States administration, which Gregory XI needed to address in person.
  • Pope Urban VI Elected 1378 under pressure from a Roman mob. His volatile conduct led the same cardinals who elected him to annul the election four months later, citing duress.
  • Pope Clement VII Elected by the same cardinals in September 1378 in opposition to Urban VI, and returned to Avignon — the start of the Western Schism.
  • the Council of Pisa (1409) Attempted to end the schism by deposing both rival popes and electing Alexander V instead. Neither original claimant resigned, producing three popes at once, resolved only at Constance (1414–1418).
22 The Great Divorce
  • Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida Papal legate who placed a bull of excommunication against Patriarch Michael Cerularius on the Hagia Sophia's altar, July 16, 1054 — weeks after Pope Leo IX, whose authority backed the mission, had already died.
  • Patriarch Michael Cerularius Excommunicated Humbert and the Roman legates in return roughly a week later. Deposed and exiled in 1058; died in 1059 before he could stand trial for treason and heresy.
  • the Photian Schism (863–867) An earlier, briefer rupture between Rome and Constantinople, resolved within a decade — a rehearsal of the mutual distrust that resurfaced in 1054.
  • the filioque clause Added locally in sixth-century Spain, refused by Rome itself for two centuries, and finally adopted under German imperial pressure in 1014.
  • the Fourth Crusade (1204) Diverted from the Holy Land into a three-day sack of Constantinople. The chapter argues this, not 1054, actually cemented lasting Eastern-Western mistrust.
  • Niketas Choniates Contemporary Byzantine historian whose account of the 1204 sack is the source for the woman on the patriarch's throne — a detail popularly exaggerated beyond what his own account supports.
  • the Catholic-Orthodox Joint Declaration (1965) Paul VI and Athenagoras I jointly lifted the mutual excommunications of 1054, nine hundred and eleven years later.
