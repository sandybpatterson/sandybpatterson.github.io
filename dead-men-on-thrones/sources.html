Appendix E

Further Reading

The scholars and sources behind this book, chapter by chapter — including every place it takes a position other serious people disagree with.

This book does not carry footnotes in its main text. That is deliberate: the argument is made in prose, and you should be able to follow it without stepping over a numbered marker every third sentence. But every specific claim in these chapters rests on something, and this is where that something is written down.

Each chapter is grouped under three headings. Primary sources are the ancient texts the chapter actually draws on — including the hostile ones, and noting where a text survives only inside a later writer's rebuttal. Modern scholarship is the named researchers behind the historical claims. Where this is contested is the one that matters most.

That third heading exists because this book's whole argument is that institutions flatten messy history into clean stories that serve them. An appendix that hid its own uncertainty would be doing exactly the same thing.

Ancient dates in this material are frequently approximate and frequently disputed. Where a date is genuinely argued over, it is marked "c." or the dispute is named outright. Where a firm date appears here, it is firm.

Covers Chapters 1–12. Entries for the remaining chapters will be added as they are written.

01Before the Book

Primary Sources

  • Ignatius of Antioch, the seven lettersEphesians, Magnesians, Trallians, Romans, Philadelphians, Smyrnaeans, and Polycarp — written under guard en route to execution, conventionally c. 107. The "middle recension" of seven is the text scholars generally accept as authentic; a longer thirteen-letter version is a later expansion.The Philadelphians passage about "the archives" is the anecdote this chapter is built on.
  • Justin Martyr, First Apology, c. 155–157Chapter 67 describes an ordinary Sunday: "the memoirs of the apostles" read aloud alongside the prophets, for as long as time allows.The single most important sentence in the chapter — texts acquiring scriptural function in practice, decades before anyone listed which ones.
  • The Muratorian FragmentA damaged Latin manuscript naming most of what became the New Testament. Discovered by Ludovico Antonio Muratori and published in 1740.
  • Tatian, Diatessaron, c. 170sThe four gospels woven into one continuous harmony, which became the standard gospel text for Syriac-speaking Christians for generations.
  • Gospel of Thomas; Gospel of Peter; Gospel of the HebrewsThomas survives complete in Coptic from the Nag Hammadi find of 1945; Hebrews survives only in fragments quoted by later writers.
  • Paul, Romans 16Phoebe named as deacon, Prisca as co-worker, Junia as "outstanding among the apostles." The chapter's closing section comes straight out of Paul's own greeting list.

Modern Scholarship

  • Walter Bauer, Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest ChristianityGerman original 1934; English translation 1971. The strong thesis that in many regions what later got labeled heresy was simply the local Christianity, with Roman-flavored orthodoxy arriving afterward and rewriting the record.
  • Bart D. Ehrman, Lost Christianities (2003)The accessible general treatment of the plural-Christianities picture this chapter describes.
  • Bruce M. Metzger, The Canon of the New Testament (1987)Still the standard reference work on how the list got made.

Where This Is Contested

  • The date of Ignatius's lettersThe traditional c. 107 is not settled. A substantial body of scholarship places them in the 130s or 140s, and a minority argues later still.
  • The date of the Muratorian FragmentThe conventional c. 180 is directly challenged by Albert C. Sundberg, "Canon Muratori: A Fourth-Century List," Harvard Theological Review 66 (1973), and at book length by Geoffrey Mark Hahneman, The Muratorian Fragment and the Development of the Canon (1992). If they are right, the "first canon list" is roughly two centuries later than the textbooks say.
  • Bauer's thesisMost historians today think Bauer overstated his case. The picture varied enormously by region, and plenty of communities held something recognizably continuous with later orthodoxy from very early. The strong version is disputed; the floor underneath it is not.
02The Man Who Forced the Question
A standing warning for this chapter Almost nothing known about Marcion comes from Marcion. No manuscript of his gospel survives. No copy of the Antitheses survives. Every substantive claim about what he taught reaches us through writers trying to destroy his movement, most of them writing decades to centuries after his death. That is not a reason to disbelieve everything they say — hostile sources quote their opponents at length precisely in order to refute them, which preserves material that would otherwise be lost entirely. It is a reason to hold every biographical detail loosely.

Primary Sources

  • Tertullian, Against Marcion, c. 207–208Five books, and the single fullest source on Marcion's theology and his edited gospel — written roughly half a century after his death by someone determined to refute him verse by verse. The "wasps building nests from a wasp" line is Tertullian's.
  • Irenaeus, Against Heresies, c. 180Carries the Polycarp encounter — "I recognize the firstborn of Satan." Irenaeus claims the story from people who were present, and is the closest thing to a contemporary witness in the chain.
  • Epiphanius of Salamis, Panarion, c. 375–378The catalogue of heresies carrying the story that Marcion was expelled from his father's church at Sinope for seducing a virgin — more than two hundred years after the fact, with no earlier source mentioning it.
  • Eznik of Kolb, Against the Sects, 5th centuryEvidence that Marcionite communities were still active, and still worth refuting in Armenia, roughly three hundred years after the excommunication.

Modern Scholarship

  • Adolf von Harnack, Marcion: The Gospel of the Alien GodFirst edition c. 1920; second, expanded edition 1924. The origin of the strong Marcion-as-hinge thesis this book largely tells.
  • Judith M. Lieu, Marcion and the Making of a Heretic (Cambridge, 2015)The most important modern treatment, and the direct counterweight to Harnack. Argues canon formation was slower and multi-causal, and that the singular-arch-heretic story served the people telling it.
  • Matthias Klinghardt, The Oldest Gospel and the Formation of the Canonical GospelsGerman 2015; English edition 2021. Argues Marcion's gospel was not an edit of Luke but an earlier text that Luke — and Matthew, and Mark — drew on.
  • Markus Vinzent, Marcion and the Dating of the Synoptic Gospels (2014)Goes further still: the canonical gospels as a set written partly in response to Marcion rather than before him.

Where This Is Contested

  • Three live positionsHarnack's — Marcion is the hinge, which is most of this chapter's narrative. Lieu's — Marcion is the earliest visible symptom of a pressure building anyway, from Gnostic movements broadly, Montanism, competing apostolic claims, and the technical shift from scroll to codex that made large bound collections physically possible. Klinghardt and Vinzent's — the direction of dependency between Marcion's gospel and Luke may run the other way entirely.
  • The chapter's own positionIt lands on "both things can be true at once, and probably are," and leaves it unresolved. That is the honest answer, not a hedge.
  • What is firmThe 144 excommunication date. The 200,000 sesterces and its return come from Tertullian and are plausible but single-sourced.
03The Lost Document

Primary Sources

  • Papias of Hierapolis, Exposition of the Sayings of the LordFive volumes, first decades of the second century. Lost. Survives only in quotations preserved by later writers, principally Eusebius, Historia Ecclesiastica 3.39, which carries the Elder's remark about Matthew compiling the sayings "in the Hebrew dialect."
  • Gospel of Thomas114 sayings, almost no narrative frame, no passion, no resurrection. Recovered complete in Coptic at Nag Hammadi in 1945 — the chapter's evidence that the sayings-gospel was a real genre and not a scholarly invention required to make Q plausible.

Modern Scholarship

  • Christian Hermann Weisse (1838)Early formulation of what became the two-source hypothesis.
  • B. H. Streeter, The Four Gospels: A Study of Origins (1924)The classic English statement of the case for Q.
  • Austin Farrer, "On Dispensing with Q" (1955)Developed by Michael Goulder and, most accessibly, by Mark Goodacre, The Case Against Q (2002). The serious minority position: Mark first, Matthew used Mark, Luke used both. No lost document required.
  • Matthias KlinghardtAs above. His argument was developed in part specifically to solve the Synoptic Problem without Q.

Where This Is Contested

  • Q's entire existenceNo fragment has ever been found, and no ancient writer quotes it by name or credits it as a source. It is an inference from the agreements between two other documents, not a discovery. The chapter says this plainly and does not retreat from it.
  • Papias's testimonyWhether the Elder was describing anything like Q, something else entirely, or simply garbling the origins of canonical Matthew is genuinely unresolved.
04Four Accounts, One Story

Primary Sources

  • Celsus, The True Word, c. 177–180Lost. Survives only as quoted inside its own rebuttal.
  • Origen, Contra Celsum, c. 248Quotes Celsus at such length in order to answer him that the lost attack can be substantially reconstructed. Origen's defense — that four identical accounts would be the real red flag — is the argument the chapter puts on the table and declines to settle.
  • Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews, Book 18The census under Quirinius, independently dating it to 6 AD.
  • Codex Sinaiticus and Codex VaticanusBoth 4th century, and both ending Mark at 16:8. Eusebius and Jerome each report that nearly every Greek manuscript known to them did the same.

Where This Is Contested

  • The census problemMatthew places the birth under Herod the Great, who died in 4 BC; Luke ties it to the Quirinius census of 6 AD. Proposed reconciliations include an earlier unrecorded census, a mistranslation of Luke's Greek, and a deliberate literary anachronism. None has carried the field.
  • The ending of MarkTextual critics overwhelmingly hold the original ended at 16:8 — about as close to settled as New Testament textual criticism gets. But Irenaeus appears to know some version of the longer ending by the 170s, so it circulated early and was not invented centuries later.
  • The two genealogiesThe "one of them is secretly Mary's" harmonization is nowhere stated in either text, and would still be beside the point in a culture tracing legal descent through the father.

If You Want the Full Case

  • Bart D. Ehrman, Jesus, Interrupted (2009)The gospel discrepancies at book length for a general audience.
  • Raymond E. Brown, The Birth of the Messiah (1977, rev. 1993) and The Death of the Messiah (1994)The standard scholarly treatments, from a committed Catholic scholar — useful precisely because they are not written to embarrass the text.
05The Cosmic Gospel

Primary Sources

  • The Gospel of JohnThe prologue (1:1–14); the ego eimi sayings; the temple confrontation at 8:58; the farewell discourse (chapters 14–17), with the vine and branches at chapter 15; Thomas at 20:24–29.
  • Exodus 3:14"I Am Who I Am." The Septuagint, the Greek translation in wide use in the first century, renders it with the same phrase John repeatedly puts in Jesus's mouth.
  • Philo of AlexandriaThe Logos as mediator between an unreachable God and the material world, fusing the Stoic rational principle with Hebrew scripture's creative "word of the Lord." Writing in Greek in roughly the same decades as Jesus's ministry.

Modern Scholarship

  • J. Louis Martyn, History and Theology in the Fourth Gospel (1968)The influential reconstruction tying John's aposynagogos passages to the Birkat ha-Minim, and reading John's community as recent refugees from a formal split.
  • Adele Reinhartz, Cast Out of the Covenant (2018)The leading pushback. Reads the expulsion language as the gospel's own rhetoric, defining insider and outsider, rather than a report of one datable legislated event.

Where This Is Contested

  • John's relationship to PhiloWhether the author read Philo, absorbed the idea secondhand, or arrived somewhere similar independently out of Jewish wisdom tradition is unsettled. The chapter's own observation is worth keeping: Philo's Logos recurs throughout his work, while John's appears once, gloriously, and never again.
  • The Martyn thesisNow substantially contested — both on whether the Birkat ha-Minim was in use early enough, and on whether the expulsion language is reportage at all. What survives the dispute is the broader picture: this community understood itself as painfully and recently separated from the Jewish world it grew out of.
  • The dating of Johnc. 90–110 is standard and not seriously disputed.
06The Man Who Built the Theology

Primary Sources

  • The seven undisputed lettersRomans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon. The six disputed: Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, and the pastorals — 1 and 2 Timothy, Titus.
  • Acts of the ApostlesThe three accounts of the Damascus road — chapters 9, 22, and 26 — which differ on who heard the voice, who saw the light, and who fell. Also the sole source for Paul's Roman citizenship, which his own letters never mention.
  • The Epistle of James, 2:14–26The only place in the Bible the phrase "faith alone" appears, and it appears in order to be rejected.
  • Luther, Preface to the New Testament (1522)The "epistle of straw" line, which Luther removed from later editions.The blunter "Jimmy into the stove" remark comes from the Table Talk tradition — after-dinner recollections written down by students, not Luther's own published prose. Reported, not documented.

Modern Scholarship

  • E. P. Sanders, Paul and Palestinian Judaism (1977)The study that broke Luther's reading. Second Temple Judaism operated on what Sanders called covenantal nomism: inside the covenant by grace, keeping the law as response rather than price of admission.
  • James D. G. Dunn, "The New Perspective on Paul" (1983)Coined the label for the movement Sanders started. Developed most widely by N. T. Wright.

Where This Is Contested

  • The New Perspective itselfNot universally accepted. The most serious pushback comes from theologians who argue it understates how seriously Paul does talk about sin and individual reconciliation. But even Sanders's critics now have to argue with him rather than around him.
  • 1 Corinthians 14:34–35Three live positions: a later interpolation Paul never wrote — supported by the verses migrating to a different location in part of the manuscript tradition, a recognized signature of a marginal note absorbed into the text; Paul quoting a Corinthian slogan in order to refute it in the next verse; or authentic and meant as written. No settled resolution.
  • Galatians 3:28 as a pre-Pauline baptismal formulaWidely held, not certain.
  • The disputed letters are not equally disputedThe pastorals sit at a meaningfully higher level of scholarly confidence about post-Pauline authorship than Ephesians, Colossians, and 2 Thessalonians, which are more genuinely split.
07The Brilliant Heretic

Primary Sources

  • Eusebius, Historia Ecclesiastica, Book 6The principal source for Origen's life — his father Leonides's arrest under Septimius Severus in 202, his mother hiding his clothes, the letter to his father in prison, the catechetical school, the torture under Decius.Eusebius is broadly sympathetic to Origen. That matters for the castration story: this is not a hostile source inventing something damaging, it is an admiring source repeating something he had heard.
  • Origen, On First PrinciplesThe first systematic Christian theology, and the source for apokatastasis. Also the Hexapla — the six-column parallel Old Testament, surviving only in fragments and later citations.
  • Jerome and Epiphanius on Origen's outputJerome's careful tally lands under two thousand titles; Epiphanius claims six thousand. The chapter uses the discrepancy rather than picking the bigger number.

Where This Is Contested

  • The self-castrationSeriously doubted by scholars since at least the late nineteenth century, on the grounds the chapter gives: the entire intellectual project was a machine for turning literal readings into symbolic ones. The live alternative is that "eunuch" was used by Alexandrian teachers for chosen celibacy, and a metaphor calcified into a reported event.
  • The 553 condemnationThe standard summary is that the Second Council of Constantinople condemned Origen. Modern scholarship has raised real doubts about whether the fifteen anathemas against Origenism were issued by the full ecumenical council at all, or by a smaller local synod — generally placed in 543, under Justinian — whose findings were later folded into the bigger council's reputation.
  • Origen's deathGenerally placed in 253 or 254, from injuries sustained under the Decian persecution.
08Closing the Book

Primary Sources

  • Athanasius, Thirty-Ninth Festal Letter, 367The earliest surviving document listing exactly the twenty-seven books of the New Testament, in the order still printed today.
  • Eusebius, Historia Ecclesiastica, c. 325The four-bin sort of candidates into accepted, disputed, spurious, and outright forgeries. Book 7.25 preserves Dionysius of Alexandria's stylistic argument that the author of Revelation cannot be the author of John's gospel; Book 4.23 preserves Dionysius of Corinth on 1 Clement being read aloud in worship generations after it was written.
  • The Shepherd of Hermas and the Epistle of BarnabasBoth appended to Codex Sinaiticus (4th century) with no visual signal marking a boundary. 1 and 2 Clement get the same honored placement in Codex Alexandrinus (5th century).
  • The DidacheLost for roughly a millennium; a single surviving copy found by Philotheos Bryennios in a Constantinople monastery library in 1873, bound into an eleventh-century manuscript, and published in 1883.
  • Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397)Reconfirmed at Carthage in 419.

Modern Scholarship

  • Bruce M. Metzger, The Canon of the New Testament (1987)As Chapter 1.
  • Harry Y. Gamble, Books and Readers in the Early Church (1995)The physical and technological side: scroll to codex, copying, circulation. The chapter's argument that geography and reach decided the canon depends on this material.

Where This Is Contested

  • "The first canon list"The chapter refuses this framing, noting that Origen appears to assume a closed twenty-seven-book set in a casual aside more than a century earlier.
  • The authority of Hippo and CarthageRegional North African synods, not ecumenical councils. Real influence in the West, no binding authority in the East, where questions about Revelation and the shorter catholic epistles kept circulating for generations. Popular retellings routinely inflate these into "the council that decided the Bible."
09The Emperor's Church

Primary Sources

  • Lactantius, On the Deaths of the PersecutorsWritten within roughly a decade of the Milvian Bridge, and the earliest and most modest account of the vision: a dream, the night before, instructing Constantine to mark his soldiers' shields. Lactantius also preserves the full Latin text of the Nicomedia letter history calls the Edict of Milan, and the text of Galerius's 311 Edict of Serdica.
  • Eusebius, Historia EcclesiasticaWritten while Constantine was alive. Mentions divine assistance at the battle without describing any vision at all.
  • Eusebius, Life of ConstantineWritten after Constantine's death — and now the whole army sees a cross of light above the sun in daylight, with words beside it, followed by a dream that night explaining it. Eusebius says he had it from Constantine directly, under oath.Three tellings, two by the same author decades apart, each larger than the last. The escalation is the point of the section.
  • Zosimus, Historia Nova, c. 500The hostile pagan source for Fausta being shut in an overheated bathhouse — written more than a century and a half after the events with an evident grudge, and unsupported by anything closer to them.
  • The Council of Arles, 314Convened by Constantine to adjudicate the Donatist schism, with bishops from as far as Gaul and Britain.
  • The Arch of Constantine, Rome, dedicated 315Physical evidence for the chapter's "belief versus calculation" argument: the inscription's deliberately unnamed "instinctu divinitatis," and a sculpted Sol Invictus relief — cross-checked against numismatic evidence, since Constantine's own mints kept striking Sol Invictus coin types as late as 326.
  • Eusebius, Vita Constantini, book 3Primary source for Helena's pilgrimage to Jerusalem (c. 326–328) and the start of construction on the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.Does not describe her finding the True Cross — that detail is absent from every source contemporary with Helena and first appears only decades after her death.

Where This Is Contested

  • Sincerity versus calculationThe oldest argument in Constantine studies. The chapter presents both readings and lands where most serious historians now land — leaning sympathetic without abandoning the cynical reading — then makes the better point, which is that belief and utility were probably never fully separable for him.
  • "The Edict of Milan"No document by that name has ever been found and none was issued at Milan. What survives is Licinius's letter from Nicomedia two months later. This matters because the popular version is wrong in a way that flatters Constantine.
  • The deaths of Crispus and FaustaNo official explanation was ever issued and none survives in any contemporary record. Every account of motive is later speculation.
  • Helena's discovery of the True CrossAlmost certainly legendary in its specifics — no contemporary source, first attested decades after Helena's death, following the same escalation pattern this chapter already tracks for the Milvian Bridge vision. Her actual pilgrimage is treated as fact; the discovery narrative is flagged as later legend. Planted here to pay off in Chapter 16, where the same relic resurfaces under Heraclius three centuries later.
  • Firm datesMilvian Bridge, 28 October 312. Milan meeting, 313. Galerius's edict, 311. Arles, 314. Crispus and Fausta's deaths, 326. Foundation of Constantinople, 330. Constantine's baptism and death, 337. None of these is in serious dispute.
10One Letter's Difference

Primary Sources

  • Socrates Scholasticus, Historia Ecclesiastica, Book 1Early-to-mid 5th century. The source for Paphnutius's mutilation and for Constantine's reported habit of kissing the ruined eye socket.Writing roughly a century after Nicaea. The chapter flags the specific detail as possibly more vivid than precisely accurate while treating the underlying scene as not in dispute.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea and AthanasiusBoth attended the council and both gave attendance estimates — Eusebius "more than two hundred and fifty," Athanasius "three hundred, more or less." Neither used the number 318.
  • Hilary of PoitiersOn the number 318 as a sign of divine design, connecting it to Abraham's 318 servants in Genesis 14 and to the Greek numeral spelling of the number, which opens with letters that also open the name Jesus.
  • The Nicene Creed, 325The text itself — one God, one Lord Jesus Christ, begotten not made, of one substance with the Father.
  • Arius, the Thalia ("Banquet"), c. 323Largely lost; known mainly through hostile quotation, principally Athanasius's own citations of it in his polemics against Arius.The same epistemic situation as Marcion in Chapter 2: the primary source survives mainly inside the writing of people trying to destroy it.

Modern Scholarship

  • Edward Gibbon, The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire (1776–1789)The "single diphthong" line.Quoted for the line, not relied on for the history — Gibbon's own Enlightenment contempt for the controversy is itself a data point, and the chapter says so outright.
  • R. P. C. Hanson, The Search for the Christian Doctrine of God: The Arian Controversy, 318–381 (1988)The standard academic treatment of the whole period this chapter and the next several span.
  • Lewis Ayres, Nicaea and Its Legacy (2004)The major modern account arguing there was never one settled "Nicene" theology, in 325 or in 381 — consistent with this chapter's own point that the settlement did not hold.

Where This Is Contested

  • The 318 numberNot a real headcount. Symbolic, probably assigned after the fact — both eyewitnesses who bothered to estimate gave smaller, vaguer figures. Modern historians generally estimate 200 to 300.
  • The Paphnutius storySourced to a single writer roughly a century after the event. Vivid and widely repeated, but not independently corroborated.
  • Arius's deathThe surviving graphic account comes from a hostile source, treated with the same skepticism this book applies to Zosimus on Fausta (Chapter 9) and Epiphanius on Marcion (Chapter 2).
  • "Three more emperors" in the closing lineRhetorical shorthand, not a precise count. Constantius II and Theodosius certainly belong on that list; Julian and Jovian's reigns were brief and largely orthogonal to this specific fight.
11Making It Mandatory

Primary Sources

  • Ammianus Marcellinus, Res Gestae, Book 31The major contemporary account of the Battle of Adrianople, 9 August 378, by a historian writing within a few years of the event. His narrative ends shortly after the battle.
  • The Edict of Thessalonica ("Cunctos populos"), 27 February 380Codex Theodosianus 16.1.2. The text itself, including the "foolish madmen" (dementes vesanosque) line.
  • Codex Theodosianus 16.10, the 391–392 decreesIncluding the line "no one is to go to the sanctuaries, walk through the temples" — the harder measures against pagan worship the 380 edict itself does not contain.
  • Socrates Scholasticus, Sozomen, and RufinusThe ancient church historians for the destruction of the Serapeum in 391, each giving slightly different detail. The chapter follows the widely repeated core: Theophilus's provocation, the pagan barricade, the demolition, and the soldier who struck the cult statue without the sky falling.
  • Ambrose of Milan, Letter 51Written to Theodosius to justify Ambrose's own conduct after the Massacre of Thessalonica.The primary source for the whole episode — the more cinematic version with Ambrose physically blocking a cathedral doorway comes from the medieval Golden Legend, not this letter.

Modern Scholarship

  • Stephen Williams and Gerard Friell, Theodosius: The Empire at Bay (1995)The standard academic biography for a generation.
  • Mark Hebblewhite, Theodosius and the Limits of Empire (2020)The first English-language biography in twenty-five years, reflecting more recent scholarship.

Where This Is Contested

  • What the Edict of Thessalonica actually targetedPopular retellings routinely describe it as the moment paganism became illegal empire-wide. Read on its own terms, its target is intra-Christian — Arians and other non-Nicene factions — and it does not name paganism at all. The harder anti-pagan measures came a decade later, in 391–392. The same kind of correction this book already made about "the first canon list" (Ch. 8) and "the Edict of Milan" (Ch. 9).
  • The Massacre of ThessalonicaHow much premeditation to assign Theodosius is genuinely unsettled. The chapter states this as an open question rather than picking a reading.
  • The Ambrose penance storyLetter 51 establishes that real, substantial penance was extracted. The specific dramatic staging most people picture — Ambrose bodily barring the cathedral door — is a later medieval embellishment with no support in the primary letter.
  • Adrianople's causal weightHistorians disagree on how directly the defeat connects to the western empire's collapse a century later. The chapter notes the disagreement rather than asserting a straight causal line.
12The Creed as Weapon

Primary Sources

  • Rufinus of Aquileia, Commentary on the Apostles' Creed, c. 404Source for the twelve-apostles legend and the earliest writer to mention the "descended into hell" clause — noting himself that it was not in Rome's own creed, only his own church's at Aquileia.
  • Marcellus of Ancyra, letter to Julius of Rome, c. 337The oldest surviving written text of the Old Roman Creed. Does not contain the descent clause.
  • Hippolytus (attributed), Apostolic Tradition, early 3rd centuryPreserves the creed in its interrogatory baptismal form — three questions, three answers, three immersions.Modern scholarship treats the text as likely a composite compilation; the attribution to Hippolytus himself is not fully settled.
  • Irenaeus, Against Heresies, and Tertullian, Against MarcionAs Chapter 2. Both also produced independent prose "rule of faith" summaries in roughly the same decades as the Old Roman Creed's formation, aimed at the same target.
  • Marcion's edited gospelAs Chapter 2. Reconstructed as beginning at Luke 3:1 combined with 4:31 ("...descended into Capernaum"), omitting the birth and infancy material entirely.

Modern Scholarship

  • J. N. D. Kelly, Early Christian Creeds (3rd ed., 1972)The standard modern study connecting the Old Roman Creed's formation to the anti-Marcionite crisis of the 170s–180s.

Where This Is Contested

  • The date and cause of the Old Roman Creedc. 180 and an anti-Marcionite purpose is the scholarly mainstream view following Kelly, not a settled certainty — there is an active modern debate about the creed's origin. The chapter attributes the dating to Kelly by name rather than presenting it as uncontested.
  • The "descended into hell" clauseAbsent from the earliest text (337), first mentioned by Rufinus at Aquileia specifically, not universal until roughly 650.
  • The twelve-apostles legendRufinus is the source, and on his own broader scholarship's evidence is not describing something that could be literally true — the document postdates the apostolic generation by roughly half a century.
  • Hippolytus's authorship of the Apostolic TraditionNot fully settled; the text may be a compilation from multiple periods.
13The Ghost in the Machine

Primary Sources

  • Victor of Vita, Historia Persecutionis Africanae Provinciae, late 5th centuryContemporary eyewitness account of Vandal King Huneric's persecution of Catholic Christians in North Africa, including the text of Huneric's 484 edict, which explicitly repurposes earlier Roman anti-heretical statutes.
  • Gregory of Tours, Historia Francorum, late 6th centurySource for the traditional account of Clovis's baptism at Reims, including roughly three thousand of his soldiers being baptized alongside him.Written roughly a century after the event; shaped by Gregory's own theological and political purposes. The chapter frames the soldier count as "tradition says," not a verified figure.

Modern Scholarship

  • Peter Heather, The Goths (1996) and Empires and Barbarians (2009)Standard modern treatment of Gothic history and the "Homoian" versus "Arian" terminology question — Heather's argument that Arianism functioned partly as a marker of Gothic identity once the Goths were settled among Nicene-majority Roman populations.
  • Uppsala University Library, published history of the Codex ArgenteusConsulted for the manuscript's sixth-century Ravenna origin, its likely connection to Theodoric's Ostrogothic court, and its present location at Uppsala.

Where This Is Contested

  • The exact date of Clovis's baptismThe traditional date is Christmas Day, 496, but historians have never fully settled it; some scholarship places it as late as 506–508.
  • The precise theological label for what Ulfilas taught"Arian" is the familiar shorthand, but the more historically precise term for the Gothic church's actual creed is "Homoian" — a related but distinct anti-Nicene position that avoided Arius's own more contested formulations.
  • The intentionality behind Huneric's 484 conference in CarthageVictor of Vita, the only detailed surviving source, was himself a victim of the persecution he describes; the chapter relies on his account for facts that are independently corroborated while noting he is not a neutral narrator.
14Into the Vacuum

Primary Sources

  • Anonymus Valesianus (Excerpta Valesiana), part II, early 6th centuryThe principal surviving narrative source for the deposition of Romulus Augustulus, Odoacer's dispatch of the imperial regalia to Constantinople, and the message that the West no longer required its own emperor.
  • Malchus of Philadelphia, fragments, late 5th century (preserved via Photius)Byzantine-side source for Zeno's negotiations with Odoacer and the insistence that Julius Nepos continue to be recognized as the legitimate Western emperor.
  • Sidonius Apollinaris, Letters and Poems, 460s–480sSidonius's own correspondence is the primary source for his career, his defense of Clermont against Euric, and his exile and later pardon.
  • Eugippius, Vita Sancti Severini, c. 511The sole detailed source for Severinus's activity in Noricum, written roughly three decades after his death by a monk of his own community.Written to celebrate its subject as a saint — a genre bias the chapter notes rather than treating the text as a neutral record.

Modern Scholarship

  • Bryan Ward-Perkins, The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization (2005)Source for the archaeological argument from pottery quality and roof-tile construction showing a genuine, measurable decline in ordinary living standards after the collapse of centralized administration.
  • Peter Heather, The Fall of the Roman Empire: A New History of Rome and the Barbarians (2005)Consulted for the broader mechanics of Western administrative collapse (cursus publicus, annona, provincial taxation) across the 5th century.

Where This Is Contested

  • Whether 476 is the meaningful "fall" date at allVersus 410 (the sack of Rome), 455 (the Vandal sack), or 480 (Julius Nepos's death). Most modern historians regard Nepos, recognized by Constantinople and never deposed by any Western authority, as the last legitimate holder of the title.
  • The reliability of Eugippius's Life of SeverinusWritten for hagiographic purposes roughly thirty years after its subject's death. The chapter draws on it for Severinus's concrete civic activities, corroborated by the text's own mundane, non-miraculous detail, without treating its miracle claims as historical fact.
  • The Ward-Perkins "catastrophist" readingInfluential but not unchallenged; some historians favor a "transformation" model emphasizing continuity over sudden collapse. The chapter follows Ward-Perkins's archaeological evidence specifically because it is the least disputable part of his case.
15The Monk Who Made the Papacy

Primary Sources

  • Gregory the Great, Registrum Epistularum, 590–604Roughly 854 letters survive; the primary source for his administration of the Patrimony of Saint Peter, the 595 Lombard negotiations and exchange with Emperor Maurice, and the dispatch of Augustine's mission to England in 596.
  • Gregory the Great, Regula Pastoralis, early 590sThe bishop's-handbook text itself; its later reception is covered under Modern Scholarship.
  • Bede, Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum, completed 731Source for the Anglii/angeli slave-market story and its accompanying puns, written over a century after the events it describes.The chapter treats this specific anecdote as likely legendary embellishment, while relying on Gregory's own Registrum for the verifiable fact of the 596 mission.
  • King Alfred, Preface to the Old English Regula Pastoralis, c. 890sPrimary source for Alfred's own stated reasons for choosing Gregory's text as one of the first works translated into Old English.

Modern Scholarship

  • R. A. Markus, Gregory the Great and His World (1997)Standard modern academic biography, source for the administrative and political detail of Gregory's papacy.
  • Modern musicological scholarship on Gregorian chantEstablishing that the mature chant repertory developed gradually over the century following Gregory's death, and that the "dictated by a dove" legend postdates him by roughly two centuries.

Where This Is Contested

  • The Archangel Michael/Castel Sant'Angelo visionNot attested in any source contemporary with Gregory; the 590 plague procession is historical, the angelic vision is later legend.
  • The Bede slave-market anecdote (Non Angli sed Angeli)Written over a century after the fact from oral tradition. The triple-pun structure signals literary construction rather than verbatim record; the actual 596 mission is independently documented in Gregory's own letters.
  • Gregory's authorship of Gregorian chantA popular, long-standing attribution modern musicology does not support in its traditional form; the chapter credits him only with organizing the schola cantorum.
  • The causal weight placed on Gregory for later medieval institutionsThis is the book's own argument about necessary institutional preconditions (Charlemagne's coronation, the Crusades), not a claim that Gregory intended or foresaw either development.
16The Prophet and the Power

Primary Sources

  • Theophanes the Confessor, Chronographia, early 9th centuryByzantine source for Heraclius's restoration of the True Cross in 630 and the collapse in Syria that followed; one of the sources transmitting the "Farewell, Syria" tradition.
  • The Quran, Surah 4:157 and Surah 5Primary source for Jesus's status as prophet rather than divine, the rejection of the Trinity as shirk, and the crucifixion-denial tradition.
  • The Covenant of Umar (Umar's Assurance), 637–638The terms guaranteeing Jerusalem's Christian population and churches protection following the city's surrender, in exchange for a special tax and subordinate legal status.

Modern Scholarship

  • Hugh Kennedy, The Great Arab Conquests (2007)Standard modern narrative history of the Rashidun and Umayyad conquests; source for the pace and scale of expansion from Arabia to Spain and Sindh within roughly a century.
  • Modern treatments of the Battle of Tours (732)Noting the battle's later reputation as the engagement that saved Christian Europe significantly outstrips its treatment in the earliest surviving sources.

Where This Is Contested

  • The "Farewell, Syria" quote attributed to HeracliusEarliest attestations appear in Arabic sources before Byzantine ones, raising doubt about whether it originated as a Byzantine lament at all.
  • The Battle of Tours as a civilizational turning pointA view popularized by later historians (most influentially Edward Gibbon); modern historians treat the engagement as comparatively modest.
  • The exact circumstances of Umar's refusal to pray in the Holy SepulchreThe core episode is well attested across multiple traditions, though precise wording attributed to Umar should be read as tradition rather than verbatim transcript.
  • The chapter's Marcion/Islam comparisonExplicitly framed as thematic resonance, not causal lineage — there is no historical connection between 2nd-century Marcion and the 7th-century origins of Islam, and the chapter states this directly.
17The Crown and the Keys

Primary Sources

  • Einhard, Vita Karoli Magni, c. 817–833Primary source for Charlemagne's reported surprise and displeasure at his own coronation, written by a courtier who knew him personally, within a generation of Charlemagne's death.
  • The Liber Pontificalis, contemporary entries for Leo IIISource for the 799 attack on Leo, his flight to Charlemagne, the December 800 council and Leo's oath, and the coronation itself.
  • Theophanes the Confessor, Chronographia, early 9th centuryByzantine source for Irene's blinding of Constantine VI, her deposition and exile, and the collapsed marriage negotiation with Charlemagne.
  • Royal Frankish AnnalsContemporary Carolingian court chronicle; source for the 813 coronation of Louis the Pious at Aachen without papal involvement.

Modern Scholarship

  • Rosamond McKitterick, Charlemagne: The Formation of a European Identity (2008)Standard modern biography; source for the "renovatio Romani imperii" seal motto, the Aachen chapel's modeling on San Vitale, and modern skepticism toward Einhard's claim of surprise.
  • Modern treatments of "translatio imperii" and the 800/962 founding-date debateOn whether Charlemagne's or Otto I's coronation represents the Holy Roman Empire's real founding moment, and on the name itself postdating both events.

Where This Is Contested

  • Whether Charlemagne's surprise at the coronation was genuineModern historians widely doubt a scripted ceremony of that scale could have proceeded without his foreknowledge; the chapter presents Einhard's account alongside this skepticism.
  • Whether "prima sedes a nemine iudicatur" was settled doctrine by 800The chapter traces it to the Symmachus affair of 501–502 under Theodoric, without claiming it was uncontested or universally accepted by 800.
  • The founding date of the "Holy Roman Empire"Historians are genuinely divided between 800 and 962; the name itself postdates both events by centuries. The chapter presents both dates and states the anachronism directly.
18Barefoot in the Snow

Primary Sources

  • Gregory VII, Dictatus Papae, 1075The 27-proposition document itself, including the claims that the pope may depose emperors, may be judged by no one, and alone may use the imperial insignia.
  • Gregory VII, Registrum (letters)Includes his own account of Henry's three days at the Canossa gate and his later letters defending the absolution against contemporary criticism.
  • Lampert of Hersfeld, AnnalsContemporary German chronicle; major source for the Diet of Tribur's ultimatum, Henry's winter Alps crossing (Bertha and Conrad in oxhide sledges), and the events at Canossa.
  • Bonizo of Sutri and other contemporary chroniclersPro-Gregorian and pro-Henrician accounts of Canossa and the following civil war diverge in emphasis depending on which side each author supported.

Modern Scholarship

  • H. E. J. Cowdrey, Pope Gregory VII, 1073–1085 (1998)Standard modern biography; source for the political reading of Canossa as a strategic success for Henry, and Gregory's own awareness of the criticism this drew.
  • I. S. Robinson, Henry IV of Germany, 1056–1106 (1999)Standard modern biography of Henry IV; source for the Tribur ultimatum, the civil war with Rudolf of Swabia, the 1084 capture of Rome, and Henry's death in 1106.

Where This Is Contested

  • Whether Henry's penance was genuine religious feeling or political theaterThe chapter presents both readings rather than picking one, on the grounds the two are not mutually exclusive.
  • The composition and authorship of the Dictatus PapaeSome historians treat it as working notes rather than a finished, circulated declaration; the chapter follows the mainstream reading without claiming certainty about its original dissemination.
  • Matilda of Tuscany's exact role in brokering the reconciliationVaries by source, though her broader military and financial support for Gregory is well documented independent of that specific question.
19The Dead Man on the Throne

Primary Sources

  • Liutprand of Cremona, Antapodosis, mid-10th centuryThe best-known narrative account of the Cadaver Synod, including Stephen VI's direct interrogation of the corpse, written decades after the event.Liutprand elsewhere in the same work is known to conflate different popes and embellish for effect; used here with that caveat attached.
  • The Liber Pontificalis and papal correspondenceSource for Formosus's career: the 866 Bulgarian legation, his excommunication and restoration, his 891 election, and the 892 and 896 imperial coronations.

Modern Scholarship

  • Eamon Duffy, Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes (4th ed., 2014)Standard popular-academic account of the Cadaver Synod and its place in the broader collapse of papal authority in this period.
  • Catholic Encyclopedia entries on Formosus, Stephen VI, Boniface VI, Theodore II, John IX, and Sergius IIICross-checked against modern secondary sources for the sequence of popes and councils between 896 and 904.

Where This Is Contested

  • The earthquake reportedly striking the LateranNot well attested in the earliest sources; presented explicitly as legend rather than established fact.
  • The precise wording of Stephen VI's interrogation of the corpseComes from Liutprand of Cremona, writing decades later with a documented record of conflating details elsewhere in the same work.
  • Whether Sergius III was personally present as a judge at the original synodAsserted in some sources and disputed in others; the chapter notes rather than asserts this.
20The Pornocracy

Primary Sources

  • Liutprand of Cremona, Antapodosis, mid-10th centuryThe primary source for nearly every salacious specific claim about Theodora and Marozia, written decades later with an openly hostile agenda.The chapter treats his political/factual claims as generally reliable in outline while flagging his most colorful, least corroborated details, as in Chapter 19.
  • Flodoard of Reims, Annals, 10th centuryNear-contemporary alternative source naming Alberic I of Spoleto, not Sergius III, as John XI's father.
  • The Liber Pontificalis and the Diploma Ottonianum (962)Signed by John XII the day after crowning Otto I, granting the emperor formal authority to confirm future papal elections.

Modern Scholarship

  • Modern historians of the Saeculum obscurum periodIncreasingly argue the "pornocracy" framing itself, coined by 19th-century Protestant polemicists rather than any contemporary source, does real work reducing two politically significant women to sexual scandal.
  • Eamon Duffy, Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes (4th ed., 2014)Consulted for the broader narrative arc from John VIII through John XII and Otto I's intervention.

Where This Is Contested

  • The paternity of Pope John XISources disagree between Sergius III and Alberic I of Spoleto; the chapter presents both without resolving it.
  • The reliability of Liutprand on Theodora's and Marozia's personal conductModern historians increasingly read his account as shaped by a documented hostile, misogynistic agenda; the chapter states this explicitly.
  • The circumstances of John XII's deathStroke, murder by a jealous husband, or some combination — rests entirely on Liutprand's account and cannot be independently verified.
  • The term "pornocracy" itselfA 19th-century coinage, not a contemporary label; used as the chapter title because it's the term readers are most likely to know, with its origin and baggage noted.
