# Chapter Thirty

### Here I Stand

The argument underneath the ninety five theses was, at its core, about money changing hands for something no money should have been able to buy. A Dominican friar named Johann Tetzel had been touring German towns selling indulgences, documents that promised to shorten a soul's time in purgatory, with a portion of the proceeds funneled back to Rome to help finance the ongoing rebuilding of St. Peter's Basilica. Tetzel is remembered for a jingle that captures the pitch perfectly, as soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs, though whether he personally coined it or simply preached a sales line that had already been circulating for decades is genuinely unclear from the surviving record. What is not unclear is what Luther objected to: that the church appeared to be selling the forgiveness of sin itself, reducing an internal act of repentance to a cash transaction a frightened relative could complete on someone else's behalf, with no indication the purchaser's heart had changed at all. That was the theological nerve the theses actually struck. Everything that followed, the spread this book has already told through the printing press that carried it, grew out of that one specific complaint about who gets to sell what.

On the night of December 10, 1520, a crowd of students and faculty gathered outside Wittenberg's Elster Gate around a bonfire that Martin Luther himself had called for. Five months earlier, Pope Leo X had issued Exsurge Domine, naming forty one of Luther's propositions heretical and giving him sixty days to recant them or face excommunication. Luther's answer that night was to feed the bull itself into the flames, along with several volumes of canon law, in front of witnesses who made sure word of it spread. It was not a private act of defiance. It was a public one, timed and staged, by a man who understood exactly what burning a pope's own document would mean to everyone who heard about it by morning. Three weeks later, on January 3, 1521, Leo X made the excommunication official with a second bull, Decet Romanum Pontificem. Luther was no longer a monk in a dispute with his own church. He was a condemned heretic, and condemned heretics, as the previous chapter already told once, through Jan Hus, did not usually get to choose what happened next.

---

What happened next was different, and everyone involved seemed to sense it would be. The newly crowned Holy Roman Emperor, Charles V, summoned Luther to the imperial diet at Worms in April 1521, under a promise of safe conduct that echoed Sigismund's hollow guarantee to Hus a century earlier almost exactly. This time the promise held, though not because anyone trusted it more. Luther arrived to a city packed with onlookers and was brought before the emperor, the assembled princes of the empire, and a stack of his own books piled on a table, and asked a single question: would he recant what he had written. He asked for a day to consider it, which he was granted, and returned the next evening with an answer that still gets quoted more confidently than the sources actually support. What the surviving accounts agree he said is that his conscience was captive to the word of God, and that he could not recant unless he were shown by Scripture or plain reason to be wrong, because to act against conscience was neither safe nor honest. The line everyone actually remembers, here I stand, I can do no other, does not appear in every contemporary record of the speech, and a number of historians think it was added to the printed version shortly after the fact rather than spoken in the hall that day. Nobody seriously disputes the substance of what he refused to do. The most famous four words of the whole episode may simply not be his.

---

The Edict of Worms, issued weeks later, declared Luther an outlaw of the empire, his writings banned, anyone sheltering him subject to the same penalty. He never faced it. Frederick the Wise, the elector of Saxony who had protected Luther for years without ever fully explaining why, arranged to have him seized on the road home by masked horsemen working on Frederick's own orders, a staged kidnapping meant to look like exactly the kind of violence the edict invited. Luther spent the next ten months hidden at Wartburg Castle, growing out his hair and beard, going by the alias Junker Jörg, a minor nobleman nobody at the castle was supposed to recognize. He did not spend the time idle. Working from the Greek text, he translated the entire New Testament into German in roughly eleven weeks, a pace that required inventing German phrasing for concepts the language had never needed to express before, since no one had ever tried to make scripture sound like something a farmer or a merchant actually talked like. It was published in September 1522, now remembered as the September Testament, and it did for the German language something closely parallel to what Gutenberg's press had already done for printed books in general: it took something that had belonged to specialists and put it directly into ordinary hands, in a form ordinary hands could actually read.

---

Some of those hands drew a conclusion from it that Luther had not intended them to draw. By 1524, peasants across southern and central Germany were in open revolt, citing grievances that were partly economic, forced labor, rising rents, the loss of common land, and partly theological, a direct appeal to the Christian freedom Luther himself had been preaching since Worms. Their most widely circulated manifesto, the Twelve Articles, quoted scripture to argue that serfdom itself was incompatible with a gospel that declared all believers equally free in Christ. It was, in its way, a serious and consistent reading of what Luther had written. Luther did not accept it. In 1525, with the revolt already spreading and already turning violent in both directions, he published a pamphlet whose title alone tells most of the story, Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants. It urged the region's princes to put the rebellion down without mercy. The peasants, he wrote, should be knocked down, strangled, and stabbed, secretly or openly, by anyone who could manage it, since nothing could be more poisonous or devilish than a rebel. The princes did not need much encouragement. By the time the fighting ended later that year, somewhere around one hundred thousand peasants were dead, a widely cited estimate rather than a precise count, and the theology that had told them they were already free had done nothing to protect them from the armies that disagreed.

Luther's central idea did not arrive from nowhere. Justification by faith alone, sola fide, is built on the same letters of Paul that this book's own opening chapters watched a wealthy shipowner named Marcion read nine centuries earlier and push toward his own extreme conclusion, that Paul's gospel of grace meant the God of law and the God of mercy could not possibly be the same being. Luther never accepted Marcion's answer, and this book is not claiming he did. But both men were doing the same essential thing to the same material: reading Paul's argument about grace as closely and as urgently as anyone in their respective centuries knew how, and discovering that it demanded more from the rest of the inherited tradition than the tradition was prepared to give up without a fight. Marcion's fight produced a canon. Luther's fight produced a continent split in two and, within eight years of his own defining moment at Worms, a hundred thousand corpses belonging to people who had believed his gospel more literally than he meant it.
