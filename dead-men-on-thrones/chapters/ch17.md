# Chapter Seventeen

### The Crown and the Keys

On Christmas Day, 800, the King of the Franks knelt to pray at St. Peter's Basilica in Rome, the way any Christian king might on the holiest day of the liturgical year. According to Charlemagne's own biographer, a Frankish courtier named Einhard who knew him personally and wrote his life within a few years of his death, what happened next caught the king entirely by surprise: Pope Leo III lowered a crown onto his head, the assembled Roman crowd broke into acclamation — "To Charles Augustus, crowned by God, great and peace-giving emperor, life and victory!" — and Charlemagne, according to Einhard's own account, was furious about it, declaring afterward that he would never have set foot in the church that day if he'd known what the pope intended. Modern historians are not at all convinced that furious surprise is the whole story. A coronation of that scale, in the city's principal basilica, with a scripted crowd chant ready to go the instant the crown touched his head, is not the kind of thing that happens by ambush. What Einhard's account almost certainly preserves is not confusion but discomfort — a genuine and lasting unease, on Charlemagne's part, about appearing to have received the Roman imperial title as a gift from the pope's own hand rather than having simply claimed it, as a conqueror who already ruled most of Western Europe might reasonably feel entitled to. That discomfort turned out to matter enormously for exactly the reason this book keeps returning to: whoever controls the ceremony that makes something official controls something close to the thing itself.

---

Leo III did not summon Charlemagne to Rome that Christmas out of nowhere. The previous spring, on the twenty-fifth of April 799, a faction of the Roman aristocracy — resentful of a pope who'd risen from ordinary clergy rather than their own noble ranks, and who therefore owed them nothing — ambushed Leo during a public procession, dragged him from his horse, and attempted to blind him and cut out his tongue on the spot, the traditional Byzantine method for permanently disqualifying a rival from holding office ever again. Leo escaped the attempt more or less intact and fled north across the Alps to Charlemagne's own court at Paderborn, throwing himself on the protection of the one power in Western Christendom strong enough to actually provide it. Charlemagne sent him back to Rome under armed Frankish escort and followed in person the next year to sort the matter out himself. What followed, in early December 800, was a formal council in Rome that could not settle the underlying accusations against Leo — bribery and adultery, among the specific charges his enemies had leveled — through ordinary process, because the assembled bishops decided there was no ordinary process available to apply. The principle they leaned on wasn't new. Three centuries earlier, a previous pope named Symmachus had faced almost exactly this situation — put on trial by his own enemies before a synod convened at the order of an Arian Gothic king this book's own thirteenth chapter has already met, Theodoric, ruling Italy from Ravenna — and the bishops judging that earlier case had reached for the same escape hatch: prima sedes a nemine iudicatur, the first see is judged by no one, answerable to God alone and to no earthly court, synod, or king. It was a genuinely convenient doctrine for a pope on trial to have available, and Leo's own synod in 800 revived it rather than invented it. Leo swore a solemn personal oath clearing himself of the charges, his accusers were exiled, and two days before Christmas the matter was formally closed. The coronation followed two days after that. A pope who owed his own restored office, quite literally, to Frankish soldiers standing between him and a mob that had already tried to blind him once was not a pope in a position to refuse whatever his protector wanted from that Christmas service — even if, as seems at least plausible, Charlemagne himself hadn't specified exactly how public or how permanent he wanted the gesture to be.

There was also a convenient vacancy to fill, or at least something the West chose to treat as one. The imperial throne in Constantinople was, in the year 800, occupied by a woman: the empress Irene, who three years earlier had arranged the arrest and blinding of her own son and co-emperor, Constantine VI, in order to keep sole power for herself. Frankish and papal writers alike seized on her sex, rather than the considerably more dramatic matter of the blinded son, as the disqualifying detail — a woman, in their view, simply could not hold the office of Roman emperor, whatever Byzantine law and two centuries of precedent said about it. That left the throne, by Western reckoning, empty in all but name, and an empty Roman throne was exactly the kind of vacancy a pope with a debt to repay and a king who already ruled most of the old Western Empire's territory could agree to fill together. The two courts were not strangers to each other before any of this. Irene herself had once arranged, and then broken off, an engagement between her own son Constantine and Charlemagne's daughter Rotrude, back in the late 780s, in a match that would have tied the two dynasties together a full generation before either side seriously considered reuniting the empire itself.

---

What each side got out of the arrangement was different, and each side understood its own end of the bargain clearly enough to spend the following years quietly maneuvering around the other's version of it. Charlemagne got the single most prestigious title left in the post-Roman world — Emperor, a title this book's own fourteenth chapter last saw stripped from a sixteen-year-old named Romulus Augustulus and pensioned off to a villa near Naples, revived now for a Frankish king who already controlled more territory than any Western ruler since. Leo got something with a much longer half-life: the precedent, established in front of the assembled population of Rome, that the title of Roman emperor now passed through the pope's own hands, that a man could rule the largest kingdom in Western Christendom and still need Rome's blessing to wear the name that made it official. It was the same institution this book's ninth through twelfth chapters watched spend the fourth century assembling a single, enforceable, agreed doctrine out of the wreckage Marcion's own second-century challenge had first forced the church to confront — and it was that same hard-won institutional coherence, not any new argument, that let one bishop in one city credibly claim the authority to make emperors at all.

None of this came out of nowhere on Charlemagne's side either. For years before the coronation, his own court had been explicitly, deliberately styling him as Rome's heir rather than merely Rome's admirer. His new palace complex at Aachen, built through the 790s, centered on an octagonal chapel modeled directly on the church of San Vitale in Ravenna — down to importing actual marble columns from Rome and Ravenna itself, with the pope's own permission, to build it. Charlemagne had toured Ravenna's Byzantine-era churches in person and, by every account, come away wanting something comparably imperial for his own capital. The seal he used for official documents carried the motto renovatio Romani imperii — the renewal of the Roman empire — years before any pope put a crown on his head to make the claim official. Leo III's coronation didn't invent Charlemagne's sense that he was reviving something Rome itself had lost. It formalized an ambition the king had already been building in stone.

Charlemagne, for his part, appears to have drawn his own conclusions about how much of that precedent he wanted to leave standing. In 813, near the end of his life, he arranged for his surviving son Louis to be crowned co-emperor — not in Rome, not by any pope, but in the palace chapel at Aachen, in front of the assembled Frankish nobility, with Louis reportedly taking the crown from the altar and placing it on his own head with his father looking on. No pope was invited. None was needed. Louis himself would go on to be crowned a second time, by a pope, a few years into his own reign — a sign of just how quickly the Roman ceremony reasserted itself as the version that actually mattered in the eyes of the wider Christian world, whatever a Frankish king managed to arrange once at home in his own chapel. Even so, the 813 ceremony was as close as Charlemagne could manage, this late in his own reign, to making the point that the crown was his family's to bestow, not the papacy's to grant — a point future emperors would spend the better part of the next five hundred years fighting popes over, in disputes this book's own next chapter picks up directly, without ever quite managing to settle it for good in either direction.

---

One further thread from that Christmas in 800 is worth following to its own quiet, ironic end, because it very nearly rendered the entire arrangement moot. In 802, with Irene still on the Byzantine throne and the awkwardness of two rival "Roman emperors" glaring at each other across the Mediterranean, Charlemagne's own court reportedly opened negotiations toward a marriage between the two of them — a match that, had it happened, would have reunited both halves of the old Roman Empire under a single married pair for the first time since before this book's own fourteenth chapter watched the West come apart. It didn't happen. Before the arrangement could be finalized, a faction of Irene's own court deposed her in October 802 and installed the imperial treasurer, Nikephoros, in her place. Irene was exiled to the island of Lesbos, where, according to the Byzantine chronicler who recorded her fall, she supported herself by spinning wool until her death less than a year later. The empress who might have handed Charlemagne the whole of Rome's inheritance through marriage instead spent her final months doing the most ordinary domestic labor available to a woman with nothing left, on an island a very long way from either throne.

None of what happened at St. Peter's that Christmas was called the Holy Roman Empire at the time — that name, and much of the elaborate constitutional machinery later centuries would build under it, developed gradually over the following centuries and didn't fully harden into standard use until well after 800. Serious historians still argue about whether the empire's real founding moment was Charlemagne's coronation at all, or whether it belongs instead to 962, when a different king from the same Frankish political inheritance, Otto I of East Francia, knelt in the same basilica for the same ceremony from a different pope, John XII, in a coronation deliberately modeled on Charlemagne's own. The pattern from 800 repeated itself almost exactly, and then went considerably further. John XII had summoned Otto to Rome in the first place because he needed a protector against an Italian rival who'd seized papal territory — the same basic transaction Leo III had made with Charlemagne a century and a half earlier, protection purchased with a crown. Within a year, the two men who'd just knelt together at that altar were openly at war with each other; Otto marched back into Rome, convened his own synod, and had the very pope who'd crowned him formally deposed. Whatever the crown and the keys were supposed to mean for each other in principle, in practice the two men holding them spent the following five centuries finding new and increasingly violent ways to test which one actually answered to the other — a fight this book's own next chapter picks up in a Tuscan snowbank, with an emperor standing barefoot outside a castle gate for three days running.
