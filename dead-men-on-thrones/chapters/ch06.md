# Chapter Six

### The Man Who Built the Theology

A light from the sky. A voice. A man thrown to the ground on the road to Damascus, blinded for three days, remade into the person who would do more than anyone else alive to decide what Christianity actually believed. It is the most consequential single conversion in the movement's history, and the Book of Acts, which is the only place the story is told in narrative form, cannot manage to tell it the same way twice.

In the first telling, the men traveling with Saul hear the voice but see no one. In the second telling — Paul's own speech, as Acts presents it, to a crowd in Jerusalem — his companions see the light but do not hear the voice speaking to him. In the third telling, before King Agrippa, everyone falls to the ground together, and Jesus's words to Saul run longer, fuller, more like a commissioning speech than a rebuke. Three versions, one author, all within a single book, and no serious effort made to reconcile them. Whatever happened on that road, the writer of Acts wasn't working from a fixed transcript. He was retelling a story that mattered too much to tell exactly the same way twice.

This matters more for Paul than for almost anyone else in this book, because so much of what people think they know about him comes from Acts rather than from Paul. Acts was written by someone else, decades later, shaping Paul into the hero of its own narrative. What Paul actually said about himself survives separately, in his own letters, in his own voice — and that voice is where the real work of this chapter has to start.

---

Paul tells us himself that he was a Pharisee, "advancing in Judaism beyond many of my own age," and that before his conversion he persecuted the followers of Jesus "violently, and tried to destroy it." He was from Tarsus, a major city in Cilicia known across the eastern Mediterranean as a center of Greek learning, which makes him something genuinely rare for his moment: a man trained with real rigor in both worlds at once, capable of arguing a fine point of Jewish law and quoting Greek philosophy in the same breath, at home in the synagogue and in the marketplace of a thoroughly Hellenized city. Acts adds that he held Roman citizenship, a privilege that would have made him unusually protected, unusually mobile, and unusually credible to Roman officials — a detail worth noting for what it explains about his later travels, and worth flagging as coming from Acts rather than Paul's own letters, which never mention it.

Whatever exactly happened on that road, something turned a zealous persecutor into the most consequential missionary and theologian the movement would ever produce — a man who would go on to write, in the space of maybe fifteen years, the documents that shaped Christian theology more than the words of Jesus himself managed to, if only because Paul got to his desk first.

---

Not everything carrying his name in the New Testament was actually written by him, and the split matters for nearly everything that follows in this chapter. Most scholars accept seven letters as genuinely Paul's: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and the short personal note to Philemon. The other six — Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, and the three "pastoral" letters, 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus — are disputed, and for the pastorals in particular, a majority of critical scholars think they were written after Paul's death by later followers writing in his name, using his authority to settle questions the historical Paul never lived to answer. Writing in a revered figure's name wasn't considered fraud in the ancient world the way it would be today; it was a recognized way of extending a teacher's voice into a new situation. But it means two different Pauls sit inside the same New Testament — one who wrote from his own hand under the pressure of live controversies, and one assembled afterward, by other hands, to speak for questions he never faced.

---

The clearest place to watch that split matter is the question of what Paul actually meant by faith and law, because for five hundred years the Western church built its entire understanding of that question on a misreading nobody noticed was a misreading.

Martin Luther read Paul's insistence that a person is justified by faith and not by "works of the law" as an argument against earning salvation through good deeds — a doctrine forged, not coincidentally, out of Luther's own tormented conscience as an Augustinian monk convinced he could never do enough to satisfy a righteous God. That reading dominated Protestant theology for four centuries. Then, in 1977, the biblical scholar E.P. Sanders published a massive study of surviving Jewish literature from Paul's own period and found something Luther's framework had never accounted for: Second Temple Judaism wasn't a religion of earning salvation through a ledger of good deeds at all. Its own internal logic, which Sanders called covenantal nomism, held that a Jew was already inside God's covenant by grace, born into it, and that keeping the law was the response to belonging, not the price of admission. If that's right, "works of the law" in Paul's letters wasn't a swipe at moral effort in general. It most plausibly meant the specific boundary markers — circumcision, food laws, the Sabbath — that determined who counted as inside Israel's covenant, and Paul's real argument was that Gentile believers shouldn't have to adopt Jewish identity markers to belong fully to God's people. A question about inclusion, reframed across fifteen centuries into a question about individual guilt, because the man doing the rereading was wrestling with his own guilt and needed Paul to be wrestling with it too. Not every scholar has fully accepted Sanders's picture — the "New Perspective on Paul" it launched has generated its own vigorous pushback, particularly from theologians worried it understates how seriously Paul does talk about sin and individual reconciliation with God. But even Paul's critics on this point now have to argue with Sanders, not around him. The old certainty that Luther simply had Paul right is gone and isn't coming back.

---

Paul wasn't the only apostolic voice on faith and works, and the other one flatly contradicts the reading Luther built a Reformation on. The Epistle of James asks, pointedly, "what good is it, my brothers, if someone says he has faith but does not have works? Can that faith save him?" and answers its own question: "faith apart from works is dead." Paul, in Galatians, says a person is justified by faith and "not by works of the law." James, seemingly answering that exact claim, says "you see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" — the only place in the entire Bible the phrase "faith alone" appears, and it's there specifically to be rejected.

Luther found this so difficult to square with his own reading of Paul that in his 1522 preface to the New Testament, he called James "an epistle of straw" compared to the real gospel, and by some later, more colorful accounts was blunter still about what he'd like to do with it — tossing "Jimmy" into the stove, as he reportedly joked, the way a certain priest in a folk tale had done with an inconvenient text. He relegated James to the back of his German New Testament, without a number in the main sequence of books. The passage doesn't survive in later editions of his preface; Luther himself apparently thought better of leaving it in print. But the instinct behind it — that an apostolic letter could be quietly demoted for disagreeing too directly with the theology it was supposed to confirm — says as much about how canons get used after they're closed as anything in the chapters this book has already spent on how they got closed in the first place.

---

Paul's most quoted line about equality and his most quoted line about restricting women don't just sit in tension. On the evidence, they may not even belong to the same author.

"There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus." Galatians 3:28 reads, by any measure, as one of the most radically egalitarian sentences in the ancient world, and many scholars think Paul is quoting an existing baptismal formula already in use in the young churches rather than composing the line fresh — meaning the sentiment may predate Paul himself, embedded in how new believers were welcomed in.

Against it stands 1 Corinthians 14:34-35: "the women should keep silent in the churches... for they are not permitted to speak." The passage sits awkwardly in its own chapter — skip straight from verse 33 to verse 36 and the argument flows better without it — and in some of the oldest surviving manuscript traditions, these two verses appear in an entirely different location, after verse 40 instead of after verse 33, the kind of wandering that textual critics recognize as the signature of a later note that got copied into different spots in different manuscript families before settling into the text most Bibles now print. A serious minority of scholars think Paul never wrote it at all. Others argue Paul is quoting a slogan from Corinth in order to refute it in the very next verse — a position with its own defenders, and its own critics, and no settled resolution. What isn't seriously contested is that in that same letter, three chapters earlier, Paul gives instructions for how women should behave while praying and prophesying out loud in the assembly — instructions that only make sense if women were already doing exactly what 14:34 says they can't.

And the more absolute restriction most people actually associate with Paul — "I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; she is to remain quiet" — comes from 1 Timothy, one of the six letters this chapter has already flagged as most likely written after Paul's death, by someone else, using his name.

---

Nearly every instruction Paul gives makes more sense once you register that he expected all of it to be temporary, because he expected the world itself to end within his own lifetime. Writing to the Thessalonians, he doesn't describe the Lord's return as a distant future event; he includes himself among those who will still be alive to see it, caught up in the clouds alongside those who have already died. Writing to the Corinthians a few years later, he tells unmarried believers not to bother seeking marriage, and married believers to live "as though they had none," because "the appointed time has grown very short" and "the present form of this world is passing away." These aren't timeless commands about how to structure a permanent institution. They are triage instructions from a man who believed there wasn't going to be a next generation to worry about — instructions later centuries would treat as binding forever, on a schedule Paul himself never expected them to reach.

---

One more fact about Paul is easy to miss and hard to explain away: for a man who built the theological foundation of an entire religion centered on Jesus, he almost never quotes him. Across thirteen letters bearing his name, and even limiting the count to the seven no one disputes, direct citations of anything Jesus said during his earthly ministry are vanishingly rare — a handful of possible echoes, one clear claim to be passing on words "from the Lord" about the Last Supper, and otherwise near total silence about the parables, the healings, the arguments with the Pharisees, all the specific material that would eventually fill four gospels Paul never lived to read. What fills his letters instead is the crucified and risen Christ — theology built almost entirely on the meaning of Jesus's death and resurrection, with comparatively little interest in reconstructing what the living man had actually said and done. The gospels hadn't been written yet when Paul was writing; Mark was still two decades away. But even granting him only oral tradition to draw on, the imbalance is real, and it tells you something about where this movement's theological center of gravity formed first — not around a teacher's remembered words, but around what his followers came to believe his death had accomplished.

Paul wrote fast, under pressure, convinced he was working against a closing clock. What his letters started, someone eventually had to finish properly — read slowly, argued through, pushed to its logical edges by a mind with no illusions that time was short. That work fell, a century after Paul died still expecting the end, to the most brilliant and most punished scholar the early church ever produced.
