# Chapter Nine

### The Emperor's Church

On the morning of October 28, 312, an army under a Roman general named Maxentius marched out of Rome to meet a rival claimant to the throne a few miles north of the city, at a stone bridge over the Tiber called the Milvian Bridge. The two men had been carving up a collapsing power-sharing arrangement between rival emperors for the better part of six years, ever since the old system had splintered the moment its architect stepped down, and they were, by that point, family — Maxentius's sister Fausta had married Constantine five years earlier, back when an alliance between their two branches of the imperial house still looked useful to both sides. It had stopped looking useful well before the two brothers-in-law brought armies to the same riverbank.

Maxentius had the river at his back and the numbers advantage, and he'd had his engineers rig a second crossing, a pontoon bridge of lashed boats, as a fallback in case the fighting went badly. It went badly. His line broke, his soldiers crushed onto the pontoon crossing trying to retreat into the city, and the boats — never built to hold a routed army — gave way under the weight. Maxentius drowned in his own armor in the river his engineers had meant to save him. Constantine rode into Rome the next day as its sole master, carrying his brother-in-law's severed head on a spear to prove it, and within a year controlled the entire western half of the Roman Empire.

That much is not disputed by anyone. What Constantine saw, or believed he saw, in the days before that battle is one of the most argued-over questions in the whole of ancient history, and the argument is worth walking through slowly, because the way the story grew over time tells you almost as much as the story itself.

---

The earliest account comes from Lactantius, a Christian writer with connections at Constantine's own court, composing his history within a decade of the battle. His version is modest: the night before the fight, Constantine was told in a dream to mark his soldiers' shields with a particular sign — a cross with its top bent into a loop, the monogram formed by overlaying the Greek letters chi and rho, the first two letters of "Christ." A dream, privately received, acted on the following morning. Eusebius of Caesarea, the church historian who will matter enormously to the next several chapters of this book, wrote an account of the church's history while Constantine was still alive and mentioned divine assistance at the battle without describing any vision at all. Then Constantine died, and Eusebius wrote a second book, a biography this time, and the story had grown considerably in the telling: now the entire army, marching by daylight at some unspecified point before the battle, saw a cross of light suspended over the sun with Greek words blazing beside it — in this sign, conquer — followed that same night by a dream in which Christ himself told Constantine what the symbol meant and instructed him to carry a copy of it into battle. Eusebius insists he heard the story from Constantine's own mouth, under oath, years after the fact. He may well have. People also, provably, remember their own formative moments more vividly and more meaningfully with every year of hindsight, especially once the moment in question has become the hinge their entire life turned on. Three tellings, two of them from the same author decades apart, each one larger than the last — a shape this book has now traced often enough to recognize on sight.

---

What isn't in question is what Constantine actually did with the power the battle handed him. In 313 he traveled to Milan to meet Licinius, the emperor who controlled Rome's eastern territories, and the two men agreed on a policy of religious toleration across the whole empire — a policy popularly remembered as "the Edict of Milan," though no single document by that name has ever been found, and none was issued at Milan itself. What survives is a letter Licinius sent out from Nicomedia two months later, preserved because Lactantius copied its full Latin text into his own history, restoring confiscated Christian property and guaranteeing every person the right to follow whatever religion they chose. Even that wasn't the true starting point. Two years earlier, in 311, the emperor Galerius — the man most responsible for the empire's most recent and most brutal round of persecution, dying by then of an illness his contemporaries described in stomach-turning detail as divine punishment — had already issued his own edict from Serdica formally ending the campaign against Christians, less out of any change of heart than a dying man's last attempt at damage control. Constantine gets the credit history remembers. The actual policy of not persecuting Christians began with a persecutor trying to die less unpopular than he'd lived.

What toleration did not mean, and this matters more than the celebrated part of the story usually lets on, was a Christian empire. Paganism remained entirely legal. Temples kept operating. Pagan senators kept their seats, their careers, their public honors. Constantine himself held the position of pontifex maximus, the Roman state's chief priest of its traditional cults, for his entire reign — the same title every emperor since Augustus had carried, never renounced, never quietly dropped, an office whose entire purpose was presiding over exactly the sacrifices and rites Christian teaching considered idolatry. He funded new temples for his own family's cult alongside the churches he was simultaneously bankrolling, apparently unbothered by running both operations out of the same treasury. The most powerful Christian patron in the history of the religion up to that point spent his career as the Roman state religion's own high priest, and left no evidence he saw a contradiction worth resolving.

---

In 330, partway through a religious settlement he was still visibly improvising as he went, Constantine dedicated an entirely new capital on the site of the old Greek city of Byzantium, on the strait dividing Europe from Asia. The location was chosen for hard strategic reasons — closer to the empire's most active frontiers, far from a Roman Senate whose loyalty he had every reason to distrust after a decade of civil war — not for any specifically religious purpose. He called it New Rome. Within his own lifetime, everyone else was already calling it Constantinople instead. It would outlast the western half of the empire that produced it by close to a thousand years, and would go on to host most of the church councils the rest of this book has reason to describe, including, two centuries later, the very council that would revisit Origen's reputation. None of that permanence was obvious in 330. What the city looked like in its first years wasn't a Christian capital so much as an ordinary Roman one that happened to have a Christian founder — pagan statuary and monuments, stripped wholesale from older cities to furnish the new one quickly, went up alongside the churches without apparent concern for the mismatch.

---

Whether any of this reflected genuine belief or pure political calculation is a question historians have argued for longer than most of the doctrinal fights later chapters will cover, and the physical evidence Constantine left behind doesn't resolve it either. The triumphal arch the Roman Senate dedicated to him in Rome in 315, three years after the battle that supposedly changed everything, carries an inscription crediting his victory to guidance "instinctu divinitatis" — by the prompting of the divinity, singular but deliberately unnamed, phrasing vague enough to satisfy a pagan senator and a Christian bishop reading the same stone without either one feeling misrepresented. The arch's own sculpted decoration includes a large relief of Sol Invictus, the unconquered sun, a cult Constantine had been personally associated with well before 312 and kept minting onto his official coinage for another decade and more afterward — Sol Invictus imagery still circulating on Constantine's own bronze coins as late as 326, the same year he had his own son and wife killed. The cynical reading of all this has real evidence behind it: an emperor who had just clawed his way through a decade of civil war had every practical reason to want a single, coherent, loyal religious community he could patronize directly, in a way the old diffuse pagan priesthoods, tied to local elites and civic rivalries, never quite offered any emperor. The more sympathetic reading, which most serious historians now lean toward without fully abandoning the first, points to what Constantine actually spent his own capital doing once the war was won: funding massive church-building projects, including the first basilica over what was believed to be Peter's grave in Rome; exempting Christian clergy from civic taxes and duties at real cost to imperial revenue; and, most tellingly, personally intervening for years in an obscure North African schism that offered him no political upside whatsoever. A faction in Carthage, later called Donatists, refused to accept a newly elected bishop on the grounds that one of the men who'd ordained him had handed over scriptures to be burned during the persecution — a traditor, a hander-over, and therefore, in their view, incapable of validly ordaining anyone else regardless of how sincerely he'd since repented. It was a dispute about whether a sacrament performed by an unworthy man was still a real sacrament, which is a more consequential question than it sounds and one this book will watch resurface under different names for the next thousand years. Constantine, remarkably, treated it as his problem to solve. He convened bishops from across Gaul and Britain at Arles in 314 specifically to adjudicate it, then personally reviewed the case again two years later when the Donatists refused to accept the verdict. He gained nothing from any of it. He pursued it anyway, at length, in his own surviving letters, with what reads on the page like genuine exasperation that Christians could not simply stop fighting each other now that fighting Rome was no longer required of them — and, not incidentally, established the exact template he would use again, at much larger scale and with much higher stakes, a decade later. Belief and utility were probably never fully separable for him. Very little about how power works, in this book or anywhere else, suggests they usually are.

---

Whatever else was true about him, Constantine ran his household with the same appetite for lethal force he'd shown every rival on a battlefield. In 326, with no official explanation ever issued and none surviving in any contemporary record, he had his eldest son Crispus — a capable young general who had helped him win his final war against Licinius — arrested and put to death. Weeks later, his wife Fausta, Crispus's stepmother, was killed as well, reportedly at the urging of Constantine's own mother, Helena — the same Helena who, not long after, in her seventies, traveled to Jerusalem and became, by later tradition, the reason an entire category of medieval relic exists at all. Sometime around 326 to 328, on a pilgrimage Constantine funded and Eusebius himself describes in some detail, Helena visited the sites associated with Christ's life and death and oversaw the beginning of construction on what would become the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. What Eusebius does not describe, anywhere, is Helena finding anything. The story that she personally located the actual cross Christ died on, buried under a pagan temple deliberately built over the site to hide it, first shows up in surviving sources decades after both Helena and Eusebius were dead — which places the True Cross discovery in exactly the same category as the daylight vision this chapter opened with: a story that gets more specific and more miraculous the further you get from anyone who could have actually witnessed it. Fragments of a cross claimed to be the one Helena found were circulating within the following generation regardless of how the discovery actually happened, and this book will watch those fragments cross paths with an emperor's own army three centuries later. A much later pagan historian, writing more than a century afterward with an evident axe to grind against Constantine's memory, claimed Fausta was shut in an overheated bathhouse until she suffocated — a vivid, gruesome detail with no support in anything written closer to the events, and worth treating exactly as skeptically as this book has treated every other lurid detail supplied by a hostile source working from a comfortable distance. What can be said without speculation is starker and, in its way, worse: two people close enough to the emperor to share his own name and household were dead within weeks of each other, on his own orders, and he never explained why to anyone whose account survives.

He waited until he was dying to be baptized — a common enough practice for the era, when baptism was widely understood to wash away every sin committed up to that moment, and postponing it as long as possible was less a crisis of faith than a practical strategy for a man who expected to keep sinning. The bishop who performed it, in 337, was Eusebius of Nicomedia — not the historian, a different Eusebius, and specifically one of the more prominent defenders of a theological position the church had gathered, twelve years earlier, at a lakeside town called Nicaea, specifically to condemn.

The empire's first Christian emperor left behind a church that was richer, safer, and more publicly powerful than it had ever been in its three centuries of existence, and no closer to agreeing with itself about who Jesus actually was. He had wanted, more than almost anything else his own surviving letters reveal about him, a religion unified enough to help hold his fractured, exhausted empire together after a decade of civil war. What he got instead, almost immediately, was a fight over a single vowel — one he would personally have to referee — that would occupy the rest of his reign and outlast it by decades.
