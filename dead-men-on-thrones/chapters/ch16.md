# Chapter Sixteen

### The Prophet and the Power

On the twenty-first of March, 630, the Byzantine emperor Heraclius walked into Jerusalem on foot, carrying a fragment of wood Christians across the empire believed was the actual cross Jesus had died on. He had spent the previous six years doing what no Roman emperor before him had managed against the Persian Empire: reversing a total, humiliating defeat. Persian armies had overrun Byzantine Syria, Palestine, and Egypt in the 610s and carried the relic itself off to their own capital as a war trophy. Heraclius rebuilt an army, invaded Persian territory directly, broke the Persian forces at Nineveh in 627, and negotiated the cross's return. The procession into Jerusalem in the spring of 630 was, by any measure available to the people watching it, the high-water mark of Christian imperial power in that generation — an emperor personally restoring the empire's holiest relic to the empire's holiest city, having just proven Rome could still, after everything, win.

He did not get to enjoy it for long. Within six years, at a river valley in Syria called the Yarmouk, an army representing a religious and political movement that had not existed at all when Heraclius was born broke Byzantine power in the Levant so completely it never recovered. Jerusalem itself — the city he had just walked into as a conqueror — surrendered in 637, and the city's own Christian patriarch, Sophronius, refused to hand it over to anyone less than the caliph in person. Umar came. Once the city was his, Sophronius offered to let him pray inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre itself — the traditional site of the crucifixion and resurrection, and the single most sacred building in Christendom. Umar declined, reportedly explaining that if he prayed there, his own followers might one day claim the spot for themselves in his memory and convert it into a mosque; he prayed outside instead, and the terms he then set, later formalized as the Covenant of Umar, guaranteed the city's churches would remain standing and its Christian residents would keep their property, their worship, and their lives, in exchange for a special tax and a now-permanently subordinate legal status. It is not the story of a building burned or a population put to the sword. It is the story of a conqueror making a specific, deliberate choice to leave the conquered faith's holiest site alone — and of that same faith losing, all the same, any further political say over the city built around it. Antioch, one of Christianity's oldest and most important sees, fell the same October. Alexandria, Egypt's capital and the intellectual capital of the ancient Christian world, held out a few years longer before surrendering in 641 or 642. Later tradition put a single line of Greek in Heraclius's own mouth as he watched the retreat from Syria unfold — "Farewell, a long farewell, to Syria" — though the earliest versions of that story turn up in Arabic sources before they turn up in Byzantine ones, which should make anyone quoting it as the emperor's own verified words at least a little cautious about who actually wrote his exit line for him. What isn't in doubt is the outcome. Three of Christianity's five most ancient and prestigious bishoprics — the sees traditionally ranked alongside Rome and Constantinople as the church's five great apostolic centers, a grouping later theologians would formalize as the Pentarchy — passed out of Christian political control within about a decade of an emperor's own personal triumph in the same territory, and none of them has been under Christian rule for any sustained stretch since.

---

The movement that did this traced its own founding not to Muhammad's birth, around 570 in Mecca, nor even to the first revelations he reported receiving around 610, but to a single migration in 622: his relocation, along with his earliest followers, from Mecca to the city of Medina, where he built the first functioning Muslim political community rather than simply a persecuted religious minority. That migration, the Hijra, became year one of the Islamic calendar precisely because it marked the moment the movement became a state as well as a faith — the same kind of fusion of religious and political authority this book has already watched Christianity itself back into, slowly and reluctantly, through Constantine and Theodosius and now Gregory, except accomplished here in a single deliberate act by the faith's own founder. Muhammad died in 632, having unified most of the Arabian Peninsula under the new community's authority. What followed under his successors, the Rashidun caliphs, was one of the fastest and most complete territorial expansions in recorded history, and the conquests of Syria, Palestine, and Egypt in the 630s and early 640s were only its opening phase.

By the early 700s, less than a century after Muhammad's death, forces operating under the Umayyad Caliphate had carried the same movement from the Arabian Peninsula to the Atlantic coast of Spain in one direction and to the banks of the Indus River in the other. In 711, an Umayyad army crossed from North Africa and broke the Visigothic kingdom this book's own thirteenth chapter already watched convert to Nicene orthodoxy little more than a century earlier — the same kingdom, now overrun by an entirely different power professing an entirely different faith. In 712, a young commander named Muhammad bin Qasim led a separate Umayyad campaign into the Indus valley in what is now Pakistan, establishing the first sustained Muslim political presence on the Indian subcontinent. The advance into Western Europe stalled at Tours, in central Gaul, in 732, when a Frankish force under Charles Martel turned back a raiding expedition — a battle later European historians, writing centuries afterward, would inflate into the single moment that saved Christian civilization from total conquest, a reading modern historians treat with real skepticism given how modest the engagement looks in the earliest surviving accounts and how many Umayyad setbacks elsewhere in this same period turned out to be temporary rather than final. Exaggerated or not as any one battle's civilizational hinge point, the underlying geography wasn't: from the Atlantic to the Indus, in barely a hundred years, was a scale and speed of expansion without much precedent in the ancient or medieval world.

---

What that expansion did to the specific argument this book has spent its last several chapters tracking wasn't theological. Nobody at a council debated Islam out of the Christian world; nobody won that argument the way Athanasius's side eventually won at Nicaea and Constantinople. It was simply removed from Christian political control by conquest, the same blunt instrument that had converted Visigothic Spain to Arianism and then back to Catholicism within the span of this book's own thirteenth chapter. Of the five ancient patriarchal sees that had spent centuries jockeying for precedence and prestige within a nominally unified church, three — Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem — now sat inside the territory of a different religion's caliphate, their Christian populations reduced to a protected but subordinate minority, their patriarchs no longer operating with anything resembling the political weight Rome or Constantinople could still claim. Constantinople itself came close to following them; Arab forces besieged the city directly in the 670s and again in 717–718, the second time with a combined army and fleet historians estimate in the tens of thousands. The city held both times — the second siege broken by a combination of the capital's triple-layered land walls, a Bulgar army the emperor Leo III paid to attack the besiegers from outside, a brutal winter that starved and froze the attackers faster than it starved the defenders, and Greek fire, the Byzantine navy's own closely guarded incendiary weapon, which burned on the surface of the water and turned the Arab fleet's own ships into torches. But holding twice against sieges that large was not the same as being safe, and the empire spent the following centuries permanently on the defensive, increasingly cut off from the western provinces it had once helped administer.

That left Rome. Not because Rome had out-argued anyone, and not because the pope had done anything differently than he'd been doing already — but because the field of serious ecclesiastical rivals had just been thinned from five ancient, roughly comparable sees to one and a half, entirely by events on a completely different frontier that had nothing to do with Christian doctrine at all. This book's previous chapter already showed what a pope with real administrative machinery, and no functioning empire left to answer to, could accomplish inside Italy on his own initiative. This chapter's accident of geography handed the next two centuries of that same office something Gregory himself never had: a Christian world in which no other bishop, anywhere, could plausibly claim to speak with comparable inherited authority. Rome's later medieval primacy is often told as the story of popes patiently building a case for supremacy, argument by argument, decree by decree — and much of that argument-building is real and covered in this book's later chapters. But the argument had an audience that kept shrinking for reasons Rome didn't control, and an empty room is a much easier room to be the only voice in.

---

None of which is the whole of what this movement was, or what it thought it was doing, and the honest version of this chapter owes its reader the theology as well as the conquest. Islam did not arrive as a rival empire indifferent to what Christians actually believed; it arrived making a specific, pointed argument about it. The Quran treats Jesus with real reverence, as one of the most significant prophets God ever sent — but explicitly, repeatedly, as a human prophet and nothing more, not as God incarnate and not as one person of a triune godhead. The doctrine this book's tenth and eleventh chapters watched get hammered into fixed, mandatory shape at Nicaea and Constantinople — one God in three persons, the Son fully divine and fully human at once — is precisely what the Quran identifies as the church's central error, a form of shirk, the attribution of partners to a God who tolerates none. Even the crucifixion itself, the specific historical event this book's twelfth chapter watched the Apostles' Creed insist on in loving physical detail against Marcion's own objections five centuries earlier, gets a different account: in the Quran's telling, Jesus was not killed on the cross at all, but taken up to God directly, with the appearance of crucifixion left behind for someone else to bear.

It is worth naming, carefully and without overstating it, what that argument rhymes with. This book's own second chapter told the story of a shipowner from Sinope who looked at the young church's inherited theological complexity — a God entangled with the Hebrew scriptures, a Christ whose relationship to that God nobody had yet pinned down — and concluded the whole architecture needed simplifying, even if his own solution, a second and separate deity entirely, went a direction almost the exact opposite of the one Islam would take five centuries later. Marcion wanted two gods where the church insisted on a confusing one. Islam insisted on one God with a clarity and force the church's own finished Trinitarian formula, in its view, had compromised. Neither position descends from the other; there is no line of transmission from a Pontic shipowner in 144 to an Arabian merchant in 610, and this chapter isn't claiming one. What both movements share, five centuries and several thousand miles apart, is the same underlying reaction to the same target: the church had built an elaborate, hard-won, formally ratified answer to the question of who Jesus was and how God could be both one and plural, and twice, from two entirely unconnected directions, someone looked at that finished cathedral of doctrine and said, in effect, this is not simpler, and it should have been.

Rome did not know, in the seventh century, that it had just become the last significant voice in a Christian world it no longer had to share with Alexandria, Antioch, or Jerusalem. It would take another century and a half, and one specific Frankish king willing to make the pope an offer no bishop of Rome had ever been in a position to accept before, for that narrowed field to turn into something resembling a throne.
