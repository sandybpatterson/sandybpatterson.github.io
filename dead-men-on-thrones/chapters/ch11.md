# Chapter Eleven

### Making It Mandatory

On the ninth of August, 378, the Roman emperor Valens led roughly thirty thousand men against a Gothic army near the city of Adrianople, in what is now European Turkey, and lost so completely that his own body was never recovered. One account has him taking shelter in a farmhouse with his wounded bodyguard while the battle collapsed around him; the Goths, not knowing an emperor was inside, set the building on fire and burned everyone in it before anyone realized who they'd killed. Something between ten and fifteen thousand Roman soldiers died with him — a large fraction of the entire eastern field army, gone in an afternoon. Historians still argue about exactly how much blame belongs to Adrianople for the empire's eventual collapse a century later, but nobody disputes what it did immediately: it left the eastern half of Rome without an emperor, without an army worth the name, and with a Gothic force loose in the Balkans that nobody was left to stop.

Into that vacuum stepped a Spanish general named Theodosius, and his own recent history made him an unlikely choice for the job. His father, also named Theodosius and also a celebrated general, had been executed only two years earlier — 376, under circumstances that still aren't fully clear, most likely the casualty of a rival faction that had gained the ear of the young emperor Gratian. The son had retired to his family's estate in Spain rather than press the matter, which was the sensible thing to do with an executed father and no army of his own. Gratian sent for him anyway once Adrianople left the East with no general left standing who hadn't just lost to the Goths, and the man who arrived in 379 to rebuild a shattered empire was someone whose most recent personal experience of Roman power was watching it kill his own father over politics nobody had bothered to fully explain to him.

The empire he inherited was too broken to survive another leader who wanted a fight with his own church on top of everything else.

---

On the twenty-seventh of February, 380 — less than two years after Adrianople, with the Gothic war still unresolved — Theodosius, jointly with his co-emperors Gratian and Valentinian II, issued a law from Thessalonica that did something no Roman government had ever done. It didn't just tolerate Christianity, the way Constantine's settlement had. It didn't just favor it with money and legal privilege, the way every emperor since Constantine had done to one degree or another. It named a single, specific version of Christian belief — the faith "which the divine Peter the Apostle transmitted to the Romans," as the bishops of Rome and Alexandria currently held it — and ordered every subject of the empire to hold it too. Everyone else got a name, and the law used it without softening anything: "since, in our judgment, they are foolish madmen, we decree that they shall be branded with the ignominious name of heretics." Follow this creed, the law said, and you may call your assembly a church. Deviate from it, and the emperor's own law had already decided what you were before you'd said a word in your defense.

It is worth being precise about who that law was actually aimed at, because the popular version of this story routinely gets it slightly wrong in the same direction popular versions of this book's earlier chapters have gotten things wrong: by making the moment cleaner and more total than the document itself was. Read the Edict of Thessalonica on its own terms and its target is intra-Christian — Arians and the other non-Nicene factions this book's last chapter spent an entire section describing, the churn of rival creeds that had never fully resolved since 325. Paganism isn't named in it at all. The sweeping later description of this as the day "a thousand years of Roman religion" got outlawed overnight describes something real, but it describes a process this edict starts rather than a light switch this edict flips — the harder measures against pagan sacrifice and temple worship specifically came from Theodosius a decade later, in a fresh round of edicts in 391 and 392 that finally spelled it out in terms the 380 law never used: "no one is to go to the sanctuaries, walk through the temples." What the Edict of Thessalonica actually did, on the day it was issued, was tell every Christian in the empire which version of their own faith was now the only legal one.

What the 391 decree actually did, on the ground, is worth one specific example rather than a summary. In Alexandria that same year, the city's bishop, Theophilus, had cult objects from the Serapeum — the great temple of Serapis, one of the most famous religious sites left standing in the ancient world — publicly desecrated and paraded through the streets. Pagan worshippers rioted, retreated into the temple complex, and barricaded themselves inside along with a number of Christian hostages. Theophilus, backed now by imperial law rather than merely by conviction, brought troops. The pagans inside were spared. The building was not. Contemporary accounts describe the demolition crew hesitating before the temple's colossal statue of Serapis itself, on the old superstition that striking a god's image would bring the sky down — until one soldier swung an axe into it anyway, the statue came apart in pieces, and nothing in the sky changed at all. The temple was leveled. What Constantine had left standing out of caution and what Theodosius's own 380 edict hadn't even mentioned was, within a single generation, being torn down by imperial permission, in daylight, with a bishop directing the crew.

---

The following year, Theodosius convened a council at his new capital to make the theology behind that law official. Roughly a hundred and eighty bishops gathered at Constantinople in 381, and where Nicaea had spent its energy on the relationship between Father and Son, this council turned to a question Nicaea's own creed had left almost entirely unaddressed: what about the Holy Spirit? A faction usually called the Pneumatomachians — "fighters against the Spirit" — held that the Spirit, whatever its exact status, wasn't divine in the same full sense as the Father and Son. Constantinople settled it the same way Nicaea had settled Arius: by expanding the creed. The Spirit was declared "the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who together with the Father and the Son is worshiped and glorified." The result is the version of the creed still recited in churches every week, technically distinct enough from the original 325 text that scholars call it the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed rather than simply the Nicene Creed, though almost nobody reciting it on a given Sunday has ever heard the longer name or knows there were two councils, fifty-six years apart, behind the words they're saying.

What made this council different from every theological gathering this book has described so far wasn't its content. It was its backing. A council's conclusions had always needed the wider church to actually accept them to matter — Nicaea's own creed took fifty-six years and multiple reversals to stick, as the last chapter spent its whole length showing. Constantinople 381 didn't have that problem, because the emperor who'd convened it had already made deviation from Nicene orthodoxy a matter of civil law the year before. Theology and imperial statute had fully merged. Getting the doctrine wrong wasn't a pastoral concern anymore. It was, as a matter of written Roman law, madness.

---

The military crisis that had put Theodosius on the throne in the first place, meanwhile, got settled with a pragmatism his religious edicts never showed. Rather than continuing a war Adrianople had already proven Rome could lose badly, Theodosius negotiated a peace with the Goths in October 382 on genuinely unusual terms: they would settle inside imperial territory, in the Balkans, as foederati — formally allied troops rather than conquered subjects — obligated to fight for Rome when called on, but left largely to govern themselves. It was a practical solution to a problem imperial pride had refused to solve pragmatically for the previous four years, negotiated by the same man who, in religious matters, was simultaneously deciding that pragmatism had no place at all — flexible enough to let the empire's newest enemies keep their own leaders and their own laws, and rigid enough to make his own subjects criminals over a single disputed letter of Greek.

None of this made the emperor himself untouchable, and the clearest proof of that came a decade later, in a city whose name this chapter has already used once before for a very different reason.

In 390, a Roman general stationed in Thessalonica had a popular charioteer arrested over what most accounts describe as a sexual scandal involving one of the general's own household. A riot followed. The general was lynched by the mob. Theodosius's response, whether ordered in a fit of genuine rage or issued and then too slow to be recalled once his temper cooled — accounts differ on how much premeditation to assign him — was to have his troops lock the gates of the city's amphitheater during a public games and kill everyone inside. Estimates of the dead run into the thousands, cut down over roughly three hours with no attempt to separate rioters from spectators who'd simply come to watch a race.

Ambrose, the bishop of Milan, did something no bishop had done to a reigning emperor in the entire span of this book: he refused to let Theodosius into church. Not a formal excommunication in the later technical sense, which the church hadn't yet fully formalized as a procedure, but a direct, public denial of communion until the emperor did visible penance for what his soldiers had done. Theodosius resisted for months. He eventually gave in, appeared before the congregation at Milan without his imperial regalia, and was readmitted to communion at Christmas. The single most powerful man in the Mediterranean world — the same man who had just finished making Christianity legally mandatory for everyone under his rule — spent the better part of a year locked out of the religion he'd made the law of the land, by a bishop with no army, no throne, and nothing to enforce the refusal with except the fact that Theodosius, having built his entire authority on the church's backing, could not actually afford to be seen defying it. The episode survives mainly through Ambrose's own letters, written to justify his own conduct, which is worth remembering before picturing anything too cinematic — later, more devotional retellings added dramatic flourishes, Ambrose physically barring a cathedral doorway with his own body among them, that the earliest sources don't actually describe. What the letters do establish plainly enough is the outcome: real public penance, genuinely extracted, from a man who had no legal obligation whatsoever to perform it.

That is the trade this chapter has been describing from both directions at once, and neither side got it for free. The state got a unified, legally enforceable orthodoxy it could use to hold a fracturing empire together, at the cost of tying its own legitimacy to a creed it no longer fully controlled. The church got soldiers, courts, and the full weight of Roman law behind a formula that had spent fifty-six years failing to enforce itself on persuasion alone. And in the same stroke, the church discovered it now had genuine leverage over the very throne it depended on — leverage a bishop in Milan was willing to use on an emperor's own doorstep, in a way no bishop writing a pleading letter from exile ever could have managed. The next chapter looks at what that finished, fully weaponized creed actually says, line by line, and exactly which older enemy it was still, more than two centuries later, built to fight.
