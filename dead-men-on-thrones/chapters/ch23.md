# Chapter Twenty-Three

### God Wills It

The call to war did not originate with Urban II at all. Eight months earlier, at a church council in Piacenza, envoys from the Byzantine emperor Alexios I Komnenos had arrived asking for help — modest help, by their own account, a contingent of Western mercenaries to reinforce an empire still reeling from its catastrophic 1071 defeat at Manzikert, which had cost it most of Asia Minor to the Seljuk Turks. What Alexios wanted was soldiers for hire. What Urban gave him, eight months later at Clermont, was something no Byzantine emperor had asked for and none of them would ever fully control: a holy war, preached directly to the people of Western Europe, promising the remission of sins to anyone who marched east and took the cross.

On November 27, 1095, in an open field outside Clermont, in central France, Pope Urban II stood before a crowd too large for any building in the city to hold and called for war. No transcript of that speech survives. What survives instead are five separate accounts, written down years afterward by five different men — Fulcher of Chartres, Robert the Monk, Baldric of Dol, Guibert of Nogent, and William of Malmesbury — and no two of them agree on what Urban actually said. Fulcher's version is generally judged the closest to whatever left the pope's mouth that day. Robert's is the most dramatic, the most quotable, and the most obviously written with the benefit of already knowing how the story turned out — Urban, in Robert's account, practically predicts the fall of Jerusalem four years before it happened. Whatever the crowd at Clermont actually heard, what echoed back out of that field for the next two hundred years was a single rallying cry: *Deus vult.* God wills it.

Four years later, in July 1099, the army that speech had summoned reached Jerusalem and took it. Two of the surviving eyewitness chronicles describe what followed in nearly identical, deliberately biblical language. Raymond of Aguilers writes of men riding in blood up to their knees and their horses' bridle reins. The anonymous *Gesta Francorum* has the crusaders wading in blood up to their ankles. Both images are lifted almost word for word from the Book of Revelation's vision of the apocalypse, where blood flows from a winepress "up to the horses' bridles" — which is a strong signal that neither writer meant the line as a literal measurement so much as a way of telling readers *this was the end of the world arriving on schedule*, exactly as Urban's call had promised. The rhetoric was inflated. The killing under it was not. The Muslim chronicler Ibn al-Athir, writing roughly a century later, put the dead inside the Al-Aqsa Mosque alone at seventy thousand — a figure most modern historians consider exaggerated, with a real total closer to a still-staggering forty thousand once the city's wartime-swollen refugee population is accounted for. Even Ibn al-Athir's own account records a few pockets of the city's defenders surrendering under terms the crusaders actually honored, granted safe passage out to Ascalon rather than killed where they stood — a detail that complicates the picture without undoing it. Whatever the true count, Jerusalem's Muslim and Jewish population was slaughtered in the days after the city fell, in a massacre real enough that it needed no apocalyptic exaggeration to be one of the worst atrocities of the entire crusading era.

---

This book excommunicated a man, nine centuries before Urban ever spoke at Clermont, for looking at the God who commands wars and floods and conquests in the Hebrew scriptures and concluding he could not possibly be the same God Jesus called Father. Marcion lost that argument. The church he was cast out of kept both testaments, kept the God of Joshua's conquest of Canaan bound in the same book as the God of the Sermon on the Mount, and insisted, against Marcion, that these were one and the same being. *Deus vult* is what that insistence sounds like turned into a battle cry: the God Marcion couldn't reconcile with mercy, invoked directly, by name, to sanctify an army marching to put a city to the sword. Marcion had wanted to cut the violent god out of the canon entirely. The church that excommunicated him for it built two centuries of holy war on the premise that he was wrong to try.

---

Jerusalem did not stay secure for long, let alone permanent. In 1144, the Muslim warlord Zengi took Edessa, the northernmost of the crusader states, prompting a Second Crusade meant to retake it. It never got close. Two large armies, one German and one French, marched into Anatolia, lost most of their strength to ambush and attrition before they even reached the Levant, and then, in a decision historians have puzzled over for nine hundred years, laid siege not to Edessa but to Damascus instead — a Muslim city that had, up to that point, been more useful to the crusader states as a rival to their actual enemies than as a target. The siege lasted four days and collapsed. The Second Crusade's surviving leaders went home having recaptured nothing, blaming each other more than the armies that had beaten them.

Jerusalem itself held for another four decades, until 1187. That year, the Kurdish general Saladin crushed the kingdom's army at the Battle of Hattin and marched on the city itself. When it surrendered that October, Saladin had every historical precedent available to repeat what the crusaders had done ninety years earlier. He didn't take it. He set a ransom — ten dinars a man, five a woman, two a child — and when the city's defender, Balian of Ibelin, told him thousands could never pay even that, Saladin negotiated a bulk price for the poor and then, by multiple accounts, simply started freeing people himself, without payment, alongside his brother al-Adil and the departing Christian nobles who spent their own money doing the same. It is one of history's more pointed ironies that the figure the crusading chronicles remember as the enemy handled the fall of Jerusalem with more mercy than the men who'd captured it in God's name. The war did not end there. Richard I of England spent the Third Crusade fighting Saladin to a stalemate rather than a reconquest, and the two men settled, in 1192, on a truce that left Jerusalem in Muslim hands and Christian pilgrims free to visit it — a compromise that satisfied almost no one and outlasted both of their reigns anyway.

---

By 1212, the crusading impulse had curdled into something considerably stranger. That year, in France and separately in Germany, waves of the young and the poor — a boy named Stephen of Cloyes leading one movement, a boy named Nicholas of Cologne leading another — set out on their own for the Holy Land, without an army, a king, or the church's sanction behind them, convinced that innocence and faith alone would part the sea for them the way it had for Moses. What actually happened to most of them is murkier than the popular version of the story admits. The familiar telling — thousands of literal children, drowned at sea or sold into North African slave markets by treacherous merchants — comes down to us largely through later chroniclers writing well after the fact, and modern historians don't agree on how much of it is real. The historian Peter Raedts has argued that the Latin word used for these crusaders, *pueri*, meant something closer to the socially "small" — landless, poor, marginal people of any age caught up in a fervent lay piety movement — rather than literal children; other scholars, notably Gary Dickson, have pushed back, arguing contemporary sources really do treat these crusaders as a distinct youth movement and that the label wasn't just medieval shorthand for poverty. What both sides of that argument agree on is that the movement was real, that it accomplished nothing, and that it dissolved somewhere on the road south, its participants scattered back to the villages they'd left or lost to hunger, disease, and the ordinary dangers of thirteenth-century travel — a smaller, sadder ending than the slave-market legend, and no less a symptom of what two hundred years of holy war rhetoric had done to the people who took it seriously. Later tradition holds that when Nicholas's group reached Rome, Pope Innocent III met with them, praised their devotion, and sent them home with a line that has followed the episode ever since: *these children put us to shame, for while they rush to recover the Holy Land, we sleep.* Whether Innocent said anything of the kind is no more verifiable than what Urban said at Clermont — but if he did, it is a remarkable thing for the head of the institution that had spent a century organizing armies for this exact purpose to say out loud.

---

This book has already told, in detail, what happened when a crusade turned against fellow Christians instead of reaching its stated target: the Fourth Crusade's 1204 sack of Constantinople, covered in the previous chapter, remains the starkest single instance of that pattern in the entire era. The crusading project limped on regardless, through further expeditions and further failures, until May 1291, when the Mamluk sultan al-Ashraf Khalil's army breached the walls of Acre, the last significant Christian stronghold left on the Levantine mainland. The defenders who survived the initial breach held out ten more days in a single tower before it, too, was undermined and collapsed; those left were killed or taken. Two centuries after Urban's speech at Clermont, the crusader states it had summoned into existence were simply gone.

---

None of it began in the Holy Land, and the part of the story that has aged worst didn't happen anywhere near Jerusalem at all. In the spring of 1096, months before the main crusader armies had even organized themselves for the march east, bands of crusaders moving down the Rhine under a minor noble named Emicho of Flonheim turned on the Jewish communities in the cities along their route instead — Speyer, Worms, Mainz, Cologne, and beyond, an estimated two thousand people killed in a matter of weeks, entire communities that had lived in those cities for generations wiped out or forced to convert at the point of a sword. Local bishops did not universally look away. In Speyer, Bishop John sheltered Jewish residents inside his own palace and held the losses there to a dozen. In Mainz, Bishop Ruthard tried to bar Emicho's men from the city outright, and when they broke in anyway two days later, the killing reached even the Jews he had personally taken into the episcopal residence for protection. Institutional mercy, where it existed at all, was real but not remotely sufficient against a mob already primed by months of apocalyptic preaching. Emicho's own apparent motive, by the accounts that survive, was a belief that destroying the Jews of the Rhineland would help bring about the end times the crusade itself was meant to hasten — the same eschatological fever that sent Urban's audience marching to Jerusalem, curdled into violence against people who had never left home and had nothing to do with who controlled the Holy Sepulchre. Two centuries of crusading rhetoric produced brief, unstable footholds in the Levant that a determined enemy eventually rolled back within a lifetime or two of each conquest. It produced, in the very first weeks of the very first crusade, a wound in the Rhineland that never had a comparable expiration date at all.
