# Chapter Eight

### Closing the Book

Every year, the bishop of Alexandria sent a letter to the churches of Egypt announcing the date of Easter — a genuinely practical piece of correspondence, since the date moved and someone with an accurate calendar needed to tell everyone else when to start fasting. In the year 367, the bishop was Athanasius, a man who had already been driven out of his own see five separate times by five different emperors for refusing to soften his position in the Arian controversy this book will get to shortly — a fight over one iota of Greek spelling that had already cost him most of his adult life by the time he sat down to write this particular letter. The letter he sent that year did something no Easter announcement had done before. Buried inside the calendar business, Athanasius listed, book by book, in the exact order still printed in every Bible sold today, the twenty-seven texts he considered the New Testament. Four gospels. Acts. Fourteen letters credited to Paul, Hebrews among them. Seven more attributed to other apostles. Revelation, closing it out. He named the list specifically to settle arguments that had been running for generations — churches in his own province were still reading the Shepherd of Hermas and the Epistle of Barnabas as if they belonged, and some congregations elsewhere were still unsure what to do with Revelation at all. His letter is the earliest surviving document that draws the boundary exactly where it still sits.

Calling it the first time the canon was decided, though, gets the shape of the story wrong, and this book has learned by now to be suspicious of any sentence that starts with "the first time." A letter written by Origen more than a century earlier — the same restless, tortured scholar from the last chapter, who spent his career reading everything the movement had produced — already appears, on close reading, to assume a closed set of twenty-seven books in a casual aside to a congregation that would have needed no explanation to follow him. Eusebius, writing his history of the church around 325, sorted the candidates into four careful bins — accepted, disputed, spurious, and outright forgeries — without pretending the sorting was finished business. Athanasius's letter isn't the moment the argument started or the moment it ended. It's the first time anyone wrote the final answer down in one place, in the order that stuck, and made a point of saying so.

---

What makes the letter worth reading closely is what Athanasius clearly still felt he needed to argue against, because popularity and canonicity were not remotely the same thing in the fourth century, and some of what lost were genuine bestsellers.

The Shepherd of Hermas was a long, strange, visionary work — parables, angelic figures, an aging shepherd delivering moral instruction through allegory, most of it concerned with repentance and whether sins committed after baptism could still be forgiven. It had nothing to do with any apostle. Nobody claimed it did. And it was read as scripture, in real churches, for real generations — read so seriously that when Codex Sinaiticus was copied in the fourth century, one of the great luxury Bibles of the ancient world, a scribe appended the complete text of the Shepherd right after the Book of Revelation, with no visual signal in the manuscript that a reader had crossed some boundary between real scripture and something else. Some modern scholars studying that codex have concluded the scribes weren't hedging or including it as a curiosity — they seem to have genuinely believed it belonged. The Epistle of Barnabas rode along in the same manuscript, same treatment. A separate great Bible, Codex Alexandrinus, gave the same honored placement to 1 and 2 Clement, letters from Rome to Corinth that a bishop named Dionysius of Corinth could still describe being read aloud in Sunday worship well over a century after they were written. None of these four books made Athanasius's list. All four had, at some point in some real congregation, functioned exactly like scripture functions: read aloud, taken seriously, treated as authoritative guidance for how to live.

A fourth book belongs in this company even though no great codex bound it in alongside scripture: the Didache, "the Teaching of the Twelve Apostles," a short, practical manual covering baptism, fasting, how to run the Eucharist, and how to tell a genuine traveling apostle from a con man angling for a free meal. Early Christian writers cited it with real respect, and some Eastern canon lists gave it serious consideration for inclusion. Then it essentially vanished — not condemned, just no longer copied — until 1873, when a Greek scholar named Philotheos Bryennios found a single surviving copy sitting in a monastery library in Constantinople, bound into an eleventh-century manuscript alongside several other early texts, and published it a decade later to considerable excitement among scholars who had known the book only by other writers' references to it for over a thousand years. Its absence from the New Testament isn't a story about it being rejected. It's a story about a text good enough to be read into practical obsolescence — once its instructions had shaped how baptism and the Eucharist were actually run, nobody needed to keep copying the manual.

What got the Shepherd, Barnabas, and Clement excluded wasn't a tribunal weighing their content and finding it wanting. It was geography and reach. Every one of them had a devoted regional following — Rome for Clement, a wide swath of the eastern Mediterranean for Hermas — and none of them had the one thing that mattered most by the fourth century: use across the whole spread of churches, not just a devoted pocket of them. A text that thrilled Alexandria but sat unread in Carthage wasn't going anywhere. The four gospels, by contrast, were being read in Gaul and Syria and Egypt and Rome, in translation and in the original Greek, by people who had never met each other and had somehow all landed on the same four books without being told to.

---

The books on the other side of that ledger — the ones that made it in despite real institutional nervousness — tell the same story from the opposite direction.

Revelation spent centuries as the most contested book that eventually made the cut. A third-century bishop of Alexandria named Dionysius picked the text apart line by line and argued, on pure grounds of Greek style, that whoever wrote it couldn't possibly be the same person who wrote the Gospel of John — a stylistic argument modern scholars still find largely persuasive, for what it's worth, even though the book kept its traditional attribution anyway. Its vivid, violent imagery had also made it a favorite proof text for Montanism, a prophetic movement the wider church had already spent a great deal of energy trying to contain, which didn't help its reputation among bishops who associated apocalyptic enthusiasm with people they'd already had to discipline. Some Eastern lectionaries left it out of the readings used in church services for centuries after it was formally considered canonical — accepted on paper, quietly avoided from the pulpit.

Hebrews had the opposite problem: brilliant, clearly important, unmistakably early — and anonymous. It never once names its author. The Eastern churches settled fairly early on attaching it to Paul's name, mostly because grouping it with his other letters was the easiest way to justify its authority, even though its vocabulary and style read nothing like Paul's authenticated letters. The Western churches were far more reluctant, and stayed reluctant much longer, precisely because a letter with no name attached made a shakier claim to apostolic authority than a movement worried about competing texts wanted to lean on. Origen, characteristically, refused to pretend the problem away. Asked who actually wrote Hebrews, his surviving answer is about as close to an admission of defeat as anyone in this book has yet made in writing: only God knows. It's a good line, and it happens to be the correct answer.

James spent its own long stretch under suspicion for a related reason — a short, blunt letter, thin on the kind of specific apostolic detail that made other texts easy to place, arguing a position on faith and works that would eventually put it on a collision course with Paul, and this book has already spent an entire chapter on what that collision did to Martin Luther thirteen centuries later. In its own century, the issue wasn't yet theological controversy. It was simpler and more bureaucratic: nobody was quite sure which congregation to credit it to, or which apostolic figure stood behind "James" specifically, and uncertainty about provenance was enough on its own to leave it sitting in Eusebius's "disputed" pile for a long time before it settled into the accepted one.

No book on the list took longer to earn its place, or squeaked in by a narrower margin, than 2 Peter. Eusebius, cataloguing the field around 325, put it flatly in his "disputed" pile — widely read, he noted, but not confidently attested — and modern counts of how often the early church fathers actually quoted it, compared with how often they merely discussed whether it belonged, put it dead last among all twenty-seven books by a wide margin. Jerome, writing decades after Athanasius's letter, noticed the same problem everyone before him had noticed: 2 Peter reads nothing like 1 Peter, different vocabulary, different rhythm, different concerns, the kind of stylistic gap a modern forensic linguist would flag immediately. His verdict was to accept it anyway, and once the most respected biblical scholar of his generation had signed off, the lingering doubts that had followed the book for two and a half centuries mostly went quiet. Nobody found new evidence. The most credentialed person in the room simply decided the argument was over.

---

None of this got formally ratified by the wider institution until a cluster of regional councils in North Africa, thirty years after Athanasius's letter, caught up to what individual bishops and ordinary congregations had already mostly agreed on. A council at Hippo in 393, and a follow-up at Carthage in 397 — convened under the shadow of Augustine, by then the influential bishop of the neighboring town of Hippo and already the most formidable mind in Western Christianity — issued a list of Old and New Testament books that matched, book for book, what Athanasius had written down three decades earlier. A second Carthage council in 419 confirmed it again, for good measure. It is worth being precise about what these councils actually were, because popular retellings routinely inflate them: regional North African synods, not meetings of the whole church, carrying real influence in the West but no binding authority over the East, where some of the same questions about Revelation and the shorter catholic epistles kept quietly circulating for generations afterward. Carthage didn't invent the canon in an afternoon of debate. It certified, with regional authority, an agreement that had already been forming for two centuries and had already been written down whole once before.

Ratification landed in 397 — two hundred and fifty-three years, almost to the pattern this book keeps circling back to, after Marcion sat down in Rome and forced the entire question by handing the church a list of his own. He'd been answered, eventually, decisively, and by an argument he never could have anticipated: not a rival theology defeating his, but centuries of ordinary congregations across an entire empire slowly, unglamorously agreeing on the same twenty-seven documents through nothing more dramatic than which books kept getting copied, translated, and read aloud on Sunday morning in more places than just one.

---

What decided the list, in the end, was never a single test applied consistently to every candidate. It was a rough convergence of several imperfect ones — a plausible claim to apostolic origin, even where that claim was closer to inherited attribution than demonstrated fact; broad use across many regions rather than passionate use in one; consistency with the theological consensus that had hardened, chapter by chapter, out of two centuries of exactly the fights this book has already covered. None of those tests was applied by a single tribunal with the power to enforce its verdict everywhere at once. That kind of reach — a decision made in Alexandria mattering in Carthage, mattering in Gaul, mattering wherever Latin or Greek was read — didn't exist for most of the story this chapter just told. It came from somewhere else entirely, and it arrived only a few decades before Athanasius picked up his pen: an emperor, fifty-some years earlier, deciding this particular sect no longer needed to be persecuted at all.
