# Chapter Eighteen

### Barefoot in the Snow

In the middle of December, 1076, a king of the Germans set out from Besançon with his wife, his infant son, and a small retinue, heading south into the worst winter anyone traveling with them could remember. Bertha, his wife, was pregnant or newly delivered through most of the trip; their son Conrad was not yet six. The route ran through Burgundy and into the territory of Bertha's own mother, Adelaide, the margravine of Susa, before climbing into the Alps themselves at the Mont Cenis pass — a crossing brutal enough in an ordinary winter that armies avoided it by choice. This was not an ordinary winter. Ice sheeted the mountain paths so completely that horses couldn't keep their footing at all; several were killed outright on the descent. Bertha and the boy were lowered down the worst stretches strapped into crude sledges made of stretched oxhide, sliding and skidding the whole way down while men on foot tried to keep the sledges from pitching over the edge. Nobody in the party died. It was, by the accounts that survive, close enough to call luck.

The king's name was Henry — Henry IV, ruler of the German kingdom and claimant to the imperial title this book's own seventeenth chapter watched Charlemagne first wring out of a reluctant pope. He was not fleeing an army, and he was not on pilgrimage in any ordinary devotional sense. He was racing a deadline that a council of his own most powerful subjects had set for him the previous autumn, running against a January that would decide, one way or another, whether he still had a throne to come home to.

---

The deadline existed because of a fight over a word that sounds almost as bureaucratic as the dispute this book's tenth chapter spent an entire chapter on a single iota — investiture, the ceremony by which a new bishop received the ring and staff that symbolized his spiritual office. For centuries, kings across Christian Europe had performed that ceremony themselves, appointing and effectively owning the bishops who ran vast tracts of their own kingdoms as landholders and administrators as much as clergy. Pope Gregory VII, elected in 1073 on a platform of stripping exactly that kind of secular control out of the church's own hands, decided the practice had to end. In 1075 he circulated among his own advisors a document later generations would call the Dictatus Papae — twenty-seven blunt propositions laying out what the papacy actually claimed for itself, no longer implied or argued case by case but stated as settled fact. The pope alone could depose emperors. The pope's own sentence could be reversed by no one. He could be judged by no one — the same principle this book's own seventeenth chapter traced to Pope Symmachus and a synod convened under Theodoric the Ostrogoth six centuries earlier, now sharpened into a formal, numbered claim rather than a convenient escape hatch improvised under pressure. Of all the princes of the earth, the document specified, only the pope's feet were to be kissed. He alone could use the imperial insignia. He alone could depose or reinstate any bishop, with or without that bishop's own local synod's consent. It reads less like a list of theological positions than like a state's own founding constitution, drafted unilaterally by one party and circulated among nobody but its own author's staff.

Henry, who had been appointing bishops the way every king before him had, ignored the ban. In January 1076, he convened his own bishops at a synod in Worms and had them formally declare Gregory deposed — not a private grievance but a counter-claim of exactly the same shape Gregory had just made, sovereign authority asserted against sovereign authority, each side insisting it alone had the standing to remove the other. Gregory's answer, delivered the following month, went further than any pope had gone against a reigning king in living memory: he excommunicated Henry outright, and — this was the genuinely unprecedented part — formally released every one of Henry's subjects from their oaths of loyalty to him. A spiritual penalty had just been converted, in a single stroke, into a live political weapon capable of dissolving a kingdom from the inside.

---

It worked exactly as designed. The German princes, many of whom had their own reasons to want a weaker king, gathered at Tribur that October and gave Henry an ultimatum with real teeth: obtain absolution from the pope within a year and a day of the excommunication, or lose the throne for good. Worse, from Henry's position, the princes had already arranged for Gregory himself to travel north that February to preside over a formal assembly at Augsburg — to sit, in effect, as a judge on Henry's continued fitness to rule, with the German nobility as the jury and Henry's crown as the verdict. Whatever happened at that meeting was going to happen without Henry able to shape it, unless he reached the pope first, on his own terms, before Gregory ever crossed into German territory. That was the calculation behind a winter crossing of the Alps with a pregnant wife and a five-year-old son: not piety outrunning good sense, but a narrow, urgent, entirely rational bet that getting to Gregory first mattered more than getting there safely.

Gregory, hearing that Henry was already over the mountains and moving toward him, didn't press on toward Augsburg. He took shelter instead at Canossa, a fortress in the Apennine foothills belonging to one of his most powerful remaining allies, the Countess Matilda of Tuscany — genuinely uncertain, for a moment, whether the king approaching him was coming to beg or to make war.

Matilda herself was not a neutral innkeeper caught in the middle. She was Gregory's single most important secular ally in Italy, had been since before his election, and would go on to bankroll his military operations directly — sending part of her own treasury south to Rome to fund papal troops within a few years of hosting this reconciliation — and to lead her own soldiers into the field in armor when war with Henry eventually came for her too. She would later will her entire personal inheritance, lands on both sides of the Alps, to the Roman church itself, a bequest that gave the papacy a source of independent territorial wealth it would still be arguing over centuries later. Whatever happened at her castle's gate that January, it happened with the owner of the castle fully invested in one side of the outcome.

Henry was coming to beg, and by every surviving account he committed to the performance completely. Henry arrived at Canossa on the twenty-fifth of January, 1077, dressed not as a king but as a penitent — barefoot in the snow, wrapped in a rough woolen garment against the cold rather than any of the regalia that marked his actual rank — and stood outside the castle gate asking to be let in. Gregory, by his own later account, made him wait. For three days running, Henry stood there in the same wretched clothes while the pope debated, inside, whether to grant what he was asking. On the third day, worn down by his own advisors' pleading as much as by Henry's persistence, Gregory relented, absolved him, and readmitted him to communion.

---

Read as spectacle, it looks like total humiliation — the most powerful secular ruler in Western Christendom reduced to begging at a locked gate for three days in the snow. Read as strategy, it looks almost the opposite, and the second reading is the one that actually explains what happened next. Gregory was, before he was anything else, a priest, and a priest could not refuse absolution to a sincerely repentant sinner standing in front of him asking for it — Henry knew that rule as well as Gregory did, and used it. By forcing Gregory to choose between his own priestly obligations and his political alliance with the German princes waiting at Augsburg, Henry took away the one thing the Tribur ultimatum actually depended on: a pope willing to sit in judgment on him as a still-unabsolved sinner. Once absolved, Henry was, in the church's own eyes, no longer the man the princes had built their case against. Gregory had won the theological argument completely and lost the political one in the same afternoon, trapped by the very doctrine of unconditional priestly authority his own Dictatus Papae had just finished writing down. He seems to have understood exactly what he'd done almost immediately. In his own later letters defending the decision, Gregory acknowledges that observers close to him accused the delay and the eventual absolution of looking less like pastoral mercy and more like a ruler simply outflanked by a more agile opponent — a criticism a pope secure in having made the right call doesn't usually feel the need to argue against at such length.

The princes were furious, and not remotely fooled. They met without Gregory that March, declared Henry deposed regardless of his restored standing with the church, and elected a rival king, Rudolf of Swabia, in his place — plunging Germany into three years of open civil war that Gregory, caught between his priestly obligation to Henry and his political sympathy for the princes who'd elected Rudolf, never fully resolved in either direction. When the war began turning in Henry's favor, Gregory re-excommunicated him rather than accept the outcome. It didn't matter. Rudolf died of wounds from a battle on the river Elster in October 1080, and with his one serious rival gone, Henry marched south into Italy to settle his account with the pope directly, by force this time rather than penance. Most of Rome fell to him by March 1084, with Gregory himself holed up under siege in the Castel Sant'Angelo — the same fortress this book's own sixteenth chapter last saw as the site of an angel's sword and a plague procession. Henry didn't stop at occupying the city. He had his own hand-picked archbishop, Guibert of Ravenna, enthroned as a rival pope under the name Clement III, and a week later had that same manufactured pope crown him emperor at St. Peter's — the identical ceremony this book's own seventeenth chapter watched Leo III perform for Charlemagne, staged this time with a pontiff Henry had built for the purpose, in the same building, for the specific reason that the office still needed a pope's hands on the crown even when the real pope was under siege a few hundred yards away and refusing to cooperate. Gregory, rescued from the city days later by Norman allies whose own sack of Rome in the process turned much of the population against him, spent his final year as a pope with no capital of his own, dying in exile at Salerno the following spring. His own last recorded words, as later tradition preserved them, were unrepentant to the end: I have loved justice and hated iniquity; therefore I die in exile.

The actual fight over investiture outlived them both by decades, and ended not in anyone's total victory but in a split so tidy it reads almost like an accountant's solution to a theological war. The Concordat of Worms, signed in 1122, gave the church the ring and staff — the specifically spiritual symbols of a bishop's office, now conferred by churchmen alone — while leaving kings the scepter, the symbol of the estates and secular obligations that came attached to the job. Both sides got to keep calling it a victory. Both were, in the narrower sense that actually mattered to each of them, telling the truth.

Neither man who stood on opposite sides of that castle gate in January 1077 lived to see the compromise that finally ended the fight they'd started. Henry died in 1106, still formally excommunicated by Gregory's own successors, his own son having turned against him in the fight's final years. Gregory had been dead for over two decades by then, buried at Salerno under an epitaph that skipped the exile and the riots and remembered him, simply, as a lover of justice. The document that made them enemies in the first place, the Dictatus Papae, outlived both of their reputations completely — its most radical claims never fully enforced in Gregory's own lifetime, and yet still sitting in the papal registers centuries later, waiting for a later pope with better timing to try cashing them in again.
