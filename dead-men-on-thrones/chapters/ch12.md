# Chapter Twelve

### The Creed as Weapon

Around the year 404, a scholar and translator named Rufinus, working in the northern Italian city of Aquileia, sat down to write a line-by-line commentary on the creed his own church used at baptisms. He told his readers a story about where it came from. After the resurrection, Rufinus wrote, the eleven remaining apostles gathered in Jerusalem before scattering across the world to preach, and — so that none of them would teach a different message once separated — each contributed a single article of faith, in turn, until the twelve of them had assembled one shared statement of belief. He even calls it, in Latin, the Symbolum: literally a token, the kind two parties would each keep half of to prove their identity to each other later. Twelve apostles, twelve matching clauses, one password for the whole church.

It is a beautiful story, and Rufinus himself is the reason we know it isn't true. The same document, in the plainer, less mythologized form it actually took, can be traced to Rome roughly fifty years after the last apostle had already died — a fact available to anyone who reads Rufinus's own broader scholarship rather than just his most quotable paragraph. By the time he was writing his commentary, the real reason his church's creed existed had already been forgotten, or at least set aside in favor of a better story. Nobody remembered — or nobody found it worth mentioning to the congregation, which is very nearly the same thing two centuries on — that the whole document had been built, article by article, to make one specific, once-notorious Roman heretic structurally impossible to recite along with.

---

The creed Rufinus was commenting on descends from what scholars now call the Old Roman Creed — a baptismal formula that took shape in the church at Rome over the second century, reaching something close to its classic form by around 180. That date is not a coincidence, and it is not this book's own theory: it is close to the scholarly consensus, most fully argued by J. N. D. Kelly in the standard modern study of these texts. A church that had spent a century and a half without needing a single fixed statement of belief — the plural, uncatalogued world this book opened its very first chapter inside — suddenly needed one, in the specific decades right after a wealthy shipowner named Marcion had built a rival church around his own edited, fixed canon and forced Rome to answer the question of what its members actually affirmed. The Old Roman Creed reads, clause by clause, like Rome's answer.

Rome wasn't alone in reaching for this tool, which is itself evidence the crisis was real rather than a single city's overreaction. Around the same decades, both Irenaeus, writing in Gaul, and Tertullian, writing in Carthage — this book's two most-cited sources on Marcion's own theology — independently produced their own prose summaries of what they called the rule of faith, aimed at the identical target. Neither wrote a fixed, word-for-word formula the way Rome did; each restated the same core claims in his own phrasing, city by city, without needing to coordinate. What all of them agreed on, without apparently comparing notes, was the shape of the answer: one God, one Christ genuinely incarnate, one creation not to be disowned. Three separate churches, three separate authors, converging independently on the same list of things Marcion specifically had to be wrong about.

The word Rufinus used for it, Symbolum, wasn't originally a churchy word at all. In ordinary Roman usage a symbolum was a broken token — half a coin, half a tile, snapped in two so that two parties to an agreement could each keep one piece and prove their identity later by fitting the halves back together. Applied to a creed, the image is precise rather than decorative: this wasn't a devotional poem, it was a password, a fixed set of words a new convert had to match against the church's own half before being let in. And the earliest evidence for exactly how it was used bears that out. A church manual usually attributed to a Roman presbyter named Hippolytus, from the early third century, preserves the creed not as something recited in one unbroken block but as a set of three direct questions put to a candidate standing in the water: do you believe in God the Father Almighty? Do you believe in Christ Jesus? Do you believe in the Holy Spirit? Three questions, three answers, three separate dunkings underwater to match. A convert wasn't reciting a summary. They were being interrogated, article by article, and only allowed through the water once they'd answered correctly.

"I believe in God the Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth." The very first sentence, before the creed has said one word about Jesus, closes the door Marcion had spent his career trying to hold open. There is one God. That God is both the Father and the maker of the material world — the same act of creation Marcion had assigned to a separate, lesser, flawed being distinct from the true God revealed in Christ. A convert reciting this single opening line was already, whether anyone explained it to them or not, denying the entire architecture of Marcion's theology before reaching the second clause.

The next cluster of clauses does the same work from the opposite direction. Born of the Virgin Mary. Suffered under Pontius Pilate. Crucified. Dead. Buried. Four short, blunt, almost aggressively physical claims, piled on top of each other with no theological cushioning at all — a specific mother, a specific Roman official whose name anchors the event to an actual, checkable moment in imperial administration, an actual death, an actual burial in an actual grave. This is not how you write a creed if your concern is elegance. It is exactly how you write one if your concern is Marcion, whose own edited gospel — this book has already described it, back in Chapter Two — began with Christ already grown, descending directly into a synagogue at Capernaum, with no birth narrative at all. Not trimmed. Removed entirely, because for Marcion there was nothing there worth keeping: a being who never truly took on matter in the first place had no genealogy to record and no manger to be born into. That follows directly from his larger conviction that matter itself was the lesser god's flawed handiwork — Marcion's Christ could not have taken on a real, solid human body at all, only the appearance of one. Marcion's Jesus only appeared to be born, appeared to suffer, appeared to die, in the same way a projection appears on a wall without ever actually touching it: a real presence, but never real flesh. Every clause in this stretch of the creed is a nail driven into that specific idea. Not appeared to suffer. Suffered. Not seemed to die. Dead. Buried, which is the least metaphorical thing you can do to a body.

---

One more clause belongs in this story, and it belongs there with an asterisk this book has learned to expect by now. "He descended into hell" — or, in some translations, more accurately, "descended to the dead" — is not in the earliest recoverable text of this creed at all. The oldest surviving written version, preserved in a letter from a bishop named Marcellus of Ancyra around 337, doesn't have it. Rufinus himself, the same commentator who gave later generations the twelve-apostles legend, is also the earliest writer to mention this specific clause — and he says so directly: it wasn't in the Roman church's own creed, only in his own church's version at Aquileia. It didn't become a standard, universal part of the text for roughly another two and a half centuries. The Greek word behind it, hades, doesn't necessarily mean a place of punishment at all; it can mean simply the grave, the realm of the dead in the plain, unadorned sense every ancient person already understood death to involve. Once added, though, the clause does the same work as everything around it, just pushed one step further: if Christ's death was real enough that he had to go all the way into death's own territory, docetism's escape hatch — a Christ who only seemed to suffer and therefore never truly needed rescuing from anything — closes completely. A later addition, in other words, still built to fight the same original argument, even after most of the people adding it had likely stopped thinking about Marcion specifically at all.

---

Put the whole document back together and what you have is a fighting document wearing the costume of a simple statement of faith — one God who both made the world and fathered the Son, a Christ who was really born, really suffered, really died, really buried, and, eventually, really went wherever the actually dead go. Every clause answers a position this book has already spent two chapters describing in detail. None of it reads, on the page, like an argument. It reads like a list of facts a child could memorize, which is exactly the point: the fastest way to make a rival theology unrecitable is to build its refutation into the words believers say about themselves, rather than into an argument they'd have to be taught to make and could, in principle, be taught to unmake.

This is, by any reasonable count, one of the most recited documents human beings have ever produced — spoken aloud at baptisms, at countless Sunday services, in private devotion, across roughly eighteen centuries and several hundred languages, by people who overwhelmingly have never heard Marcion's name and would have no reason to. They are not reciting a summary of Christian belief in some neutral, timeless sense. They are refighting, every time, a specific theological war fought and functionally won in Rome in the second century, using the losing side's absence from the winning side's own victory statement as the final proof of how completely it lost. Marcion's church survived, in scattered communities, for centuries after his death, this book has already shown. His name did not survive the creed built to bury it. The words did.

Worth noting, too, exactly how far the document's reach actually extends and where it quietly stops. The Apostles' Creed as this chapter has described it remained, essentially permanently, a Western document — recited across the Latin-speaking, Rome-centered half of Christianity, in Catholic and later Protestant and Anglican liturgies alike. The Greek-speaking East, where Marcion's own movement had also once run deep and where plenty of its own anti-Marcionite polemic had been written independently, never adopted this particular text as its baptismal standard. Eastern Christianity kept the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed from the last two chapters as its central confession instead, never fully absorbing Rome's own second-century answer to the shipowner into its own liturgy. Two churches, both descended from communities that had fought the identical second-century battle, ended up reciting two different victory documents — a small, easy-to-miss preview of exactly how far the practical distance between Rome and Constantinople would eventually grow.

That is what a genuinely finished fight looks like — total enough that the losing side eventually just disappears from the record, remembered only in the shape of what was built against it. The fight this book is about to turn to next did not finish nearly that cleanly. Arius lost at Nicaea, lost again in the years that followed, and somehow still ended up exported into Western Europe by the very people who overran the empire that condemned him.
