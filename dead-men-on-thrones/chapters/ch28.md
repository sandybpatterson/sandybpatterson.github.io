# Chapter Twenty-Eight

### The Man Who Died So Luther Could Live

On July 6, 1415, in a meadow outside Constance, a Bohemian priest named Jan Hus was led to the stake wearing a paper mitre painted with three devils tearing at a soul, inscribed with a single Latin phrase: *Hic est heresiarcha*, here is the heresiarch. As the fire was lit, according to the eyewitness account of Peter of Mladonovice, a student and companion who had followed him to Constance and stayed until the end, Hus began singing, calling on Christ to have mercy on him, and kept singing until the smoke took his voice. Mladonovice was a devoted partisan writing to preserve his teacher's memory, not a disinterested court reporter, so the exact words he recorded are closer to devotional testimony than transcript. That he sang at all, and kept singing as long as he could, is about as well attested as anything from that century gets. Nobody watching that morning needed a transcript to understand what they were looking at.

What brought a Czech priest to that meadow started years earlier and a country away, with a book he wasn't supposed to have. Sometime around 1401 or 1402, a Prague scholar named Jerome of Prague returned from studying at Oxford carrying copies of works by an English theologian named John Wycliffe, works Jerome had personally transcribed and smuggled home into a Bohemian intellectual circle already primed to receive them by two decades of scholarly traffic between Oxford and Prague that had opened after the 1382 marriage of the Bohemian princess Anne to England's King Richard II. Hus read Wycliffe closely, absorbed his relentless attacks on clerical wealth and corruption, and then did something Wycliffe himself never quite managed: he took the argument public, in a language ordinary people could actually understand. Appointed preacher at Prague's Bethlehem Chapel in March 1402, a chapel built a decade earlier for exactly this purpose, Hus preached in Czech rather than the Latin every other pulpit in the city used, at a moment when Bohemia's German-speaking clergy and Czech-speaking laity were already eyeing each other with real suspicion. He did not accept everything Wycliffe taught. He rejected Wycliffe's claim that bread and wine kept their ordinary physical substance even after consecration, a real and substantial theological break rather than a difference of emphasis, while going further than Wycliffe had on a separate point of practice: Hus wanted ordinary laypeople given the communion cup as well as the bread, a demand called utraquism that would outlive him by decades. What he was actually asking for, in both the pulpit and the cup, was direct, unmediated access, to scripture without a Latin barrier and to Christ's own blood without a priest standing permanently between the layperson and it. It is not a large step from that instinct to the instinct that got a wealthy shipowner excommunicated twelve centuries earlier for insisting ordinary believers could read a gospel he'd edited himself without the institution's permission to interpret it for them. Neither man thought of himself as continuing the other's argument. Both were punished for making a version of the same one.

---

Rome's patience ran out over the better part of two years, in three distinct stages rather than one clean break. In July 1410, Prague's archbishop ordered roughly two hundred of Wycliffe's manuscripts burned and excommunicated Hus for refusing to hand his own copies over. Hus appealed to Rome directly; when he declined to appear there in person to answer the charge, a second excommunication followed in February 1411. The real rupture came in 1412, when Pope John XXIII authorized the sale of indulgences to fund a war against a rival claimant to the Neapolitan throne, and Hus, along with Jerome of Prague, publicly denounced and burned the papal bull announcing it. That cost him the one thing that had kept him safe until then, the protection of King Wenceslas, and brought a harsher response than either excommunication before it: a renewed condemnation paired with an interdict on the entire city of Prague, denying the sacraments to everyone living there for as long as Hus remained inside its walls. He left the city that winter rather than let his own presence cost his neighbors their access to the sacraments, and spent much of the next two years in the Bohemian countryside writing his most important work, *De Ecclesia*, on what the church actually was and who actually had the authority to speak for it.

That question was still unresolved when the Council of Constance convened in November 1414, called first and foremost, as this book's own earlier account of the Avignon papacy has already described, to end forty years of two and sometimes three men simultaneously claiming to be pope. Sorting out competing claims to the papacy did not stop the same assembly from also making time for an inconvenient Czech priest. Hus arrived on November 3 carrying a promise of safe conduct from Emperor Sigismund himself, and was arrested on November 28, before Sigismund had even reached the city, after prelates convinced him that a pledge made to a man already under suspicion of heresy did not bind him the way an ordinary promise would. The legal doctrine that actually justified that reasoning, that no safe conduct protects an accused heretic, was not formally issued by the Council until September 1415, two full months after Hus was already dead. Whatever legitimate argument existed for treating the promise as void, it was assembled after the fact to explain a decision that had already been made and acted on. The same council, still in session, spent part of 1415 condemning the long-dead Wycliffe as well, and when that verdict finally reached its physical conclusion in 1428, on the order of Pope Martin V, a bishop had Wycliffe's bones dug out of consecrated ground at Lutterworth, burned, and the ashes scattered into the River Swift. The student was tried and burned in the flesh while he could still feel it. The teacher, dead thirty years, was burned in effigy of his own bones once the institution finally got around to it.

Bohemia did not accept any of this quietly. Within four years of Hus's execution, war had broken out between his followers and the combined forces of the pope and the empire, and under the military leadership of a one-eyed nobleman named Jan Zizka, the Hussites did something almost nobody expected: they won, repeatedly, against five separate crusades sent to crush them between 1420 and the early 1430s, using armored wagons chained together into mobile fortresses that broke conventional cavalry charges apart. The war that Hus's death had ignited was not, in the end, decided by any of those crusades. It ended in 1434 at the Battle of Lipany, where the movement's own moderate wing, the Utraquists, allied with Catholic forces to crush the more radical Taborites who had refused every compromise on offer. Out of that internal defeat came a negotiated settlement, the Compacts of Basel, finalized at Iglau in 1436, granting Bohemia's laity exactly the one demand utraquism had always centered on: the cup, as well as the bread, for anyone who wanted it.

---

A century later, in 1519, a German monk named Martin Luther found himself in a debate at Leipzig against a sharp theologian named Johann Eck, who kept steering Luther's own arguments toward a comparison Luther had not invited: you sound, Eck told him in front of a crowd that still despised the name Hus, like a Hussite. Luther's actual answer on the floor that day was more careful than legend later made it. He said only that some of the specific articles condemned at Constance struck him as plainly Christian and evangelical, propositions the true universal church could never rightly have condemned at all. It was a smaller admission than the dramatic one popular retellings later invented for him, and it was enough to send him looking for Hus's actual writings once the debate ended. He found more agreement there than he'd expected. In a letter to his friend Georg Spalatin dated February 14, 1520, having finally read what Hus had actually argued rather than what a century of hostile summary had told him Hus argued, Luther wrote it plainly: *sumus omnes Hussitae ignorantes*, we are all Hussites without knowing it.
