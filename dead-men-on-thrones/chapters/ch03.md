# Chapter Three

### The Lost Document

Sometime in the first decades of the second century, a bishop in the small Anatolian town of Hierapolis named Papias set out to do something that sounds obvious now and was not obvious then: he tried to find out where the stories about Jesus had actually come from. Papias wasn't content with what was circulating in his own church. He tracked down people who claimed to have talked to people who had known the apostles, and he wrote down what they told him in a five-volume work called *Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord*. The volumes themselves are lost. What survives is a handful of quotations other writers preserved, the most tantalizing of which concerns a document Papias says a man he calls "the Elder" told him about.

"Matthew," Papias reports the Elder saying, "compiled the sayings in the Hebrew dialect, and each one interpreted them as best he could."

Scholars have argued about that single sentence for more than a hundred years, and the argument is not close to over. It doesn't obviously describe the Gospel of Matthew we actually have, which was composed in Greek, not Hebrew or Aramaic, and reads as a unified narrative rather than a bare list of sayings needing individual interpretation. So what was the Elder talking about? A separate, earlier document — a collection of Jesus's sayings, written in a Semitic language, that fed into the Matthew we know but isn't identical to it? Nobody can say for certain. But Papias's strange, secondhand, badly preserved testimony is the earliest hint anyone has that behind the polished narrative gospels sat something rawer: a list of things Jesus reportedly said, assembled before anyone had woven them into a story.

He may have been describing exactly the kind of document that, a century and a half later, an entirely different set of readers would go looking for using an entirely different method, and find unmistakable fingerprints of without ever holding a copy.

---

Line up Matthew, Mark, and Luke side by side — which is literally what "synoptic," from the Greek for "seen together," means — and a pattern jumps out almost immediately. Long stretches of all three overlap closely, sometimes down to identical Greek phrasing, in a shared sequence of events. Most scholars now agree the explanation runs through Mark: it was written first, and both Matthew and Luke had a copy of it in front of them as they wrote, each adapting it for their own audience and purposes. That much resolves most of the puzzle. It does not resolve all of it.

Because there is a second body of material — somewhere around two hundred verses' worth — that shows up in both Matthew and Luke but nowhere in Mark at all. And this material behaves strangely. In places it is reproduced with an almost forensic precision: in one passage about serving two masters, twenty-seven of the twenty-eight Greek words match exactly between Matthew's version and Luke's. In another, on asking and receiving, all twenty-four words match. The Lord's Prayer appears in both, clearly related, clearly not independently invented. That is not the kind of overlap you get from two writers drawing on loose oral memory of the same general teacher. That is the kind of overlap you get from two writers copying, closely, from a shared written text.

And yet the material doesn't behave like straightforward copying either. Where Matthew and Luke reproduce Mark, they tend to follow his order, his sequence of episodes, fairly faithfully. Where they reproduce this other, non-Markan material, their order diverges wildly — Matthew gathers much of it into his long set-piece sermons, the Sermon on the Mount above all, while Luke scatters the same sayings across an entirely different structure, often on a journey narrative that doesn't exist in Matthew at all. If Luke had simply been copying Matthew, as some material would suggest, why would he take Matthew's tightly organized sermons and break them apart, scrambling an order that was, by any normal literary instinct, already effective?

The explanation that has dominated gospel scholarship since the nineteenth century solves both problems at once by proposing a second source behind Matthew and Luke, independent of Mark — a document neither writer invented, which each incorporated into his own gospel in his own way, arranging it to fit his own structure. German scholars gave it a suitably unglamorous name: Quelle, simply "source." Everyone since has just called it Q.

---

Reconstruct Q from the material Matthew and Luke share, and what emerges is strange in the same way Papias's testimony is strange. It has almost no narrative. No birth story. No miracles worth describing in detail. No arrest, no trial, no crucifixion, no resurrection. What it has, overwhelmingly, is sayings — teaching material, delivered with almost no scene-setting at all: the Beatitudes. Love your enemies. Ask and it will be given you. Do not worry about your life, what you will eat. The parable of the mustard seed. The parable of the lost sheep. John the Baptist's preaching about the one who is coming. A version of Jesus's testing in the wilderness. Warnings about the coming judgment, delivered in short, memorable, portable units — exactly the kind of material a community without paper to spare, or without full literacy, might have preserved by simply repeating it until it stuck.

If that reconstruction is right, Q would not have looked like Matthew, Mark, Luke, or John. It would have looked like something else entirely — a sayings gospel, a list of things Jesus reportedly said with barely any story attached to explain when or why. Which is worth pausing on, because this book has already met exactly that genre once before. The Gospel of Thomas, circulating in Egypt and Syria in roughly this same period, is precisely this kind of document: a hundred and fourteen sayings, almost no narrative frame, no passion, no resurrection. Two very different communities, on this reconstruction, seem to have preserved Jesus's teaching in the same bare, list-like form before anyone decided a proper gospel needed to be a story with a beginning, a middle, and an ending at an empty tomb.

---

Here is where the honest version of this chapter has to slow down, because Q is not a discovery. It is an inference. No fragment of it has ever been found, in Egypt or anywhere else, in an age that has yielded scraps of the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Peter, and dozens of other early Christian texts far more obscure than a document supposedly used by two of the four gospels that made the canon. No ancient writer — not Papias, not Irenaeus, not Origen, not anyone — ever quotes Q by that name or credits it as a source, which is a genuine silence for a hypothesis this influential to be built on. Everything about it is reconstructed backward, by scholars, from the agreements between two other documents.

That gap is exactly wide enough for real, serious dissent to live in. A minority of scholars, working in what's usually called the Farrer hypothesis, argue there is a simpler explanation that needs no lost document at all: Mark wrote first, Matthew used Mark, and Luke used both Mark and Matthew directly, rearranging Matthew's material to fit his own structure for his own literary reasons. On this view, the "shared source" isn't lost. It's sitting on the shelf. It's Matthew.

And there is a third possibility, raised by the very scholars this book has already had reason to cite once. Matthias Klinghardt's argument that Marcion's gospel might be an earlier text rather than an edited Luke — the theory introduced in the last chapter — was developed, in part, precisely to solve the Synoptic Problem without Q: if an early version of what became Marcion's gospel predates all three synoptics, it could be the common ancestor Matthew and Luke's shared material actually traces back to, rather than a redaction of Luke at all. Most scholars still prefer some version of Q. But the fact that three genuinely different, genuinely serious solutions to this puzzle are still being argued over, a century and a half after the question was first posed, says something the tidy textbook version of "the two-source hypothesis" tends to leave out.

---

What isn't seriously contested, whichever solution turns out to be right, is the underlying fact all three explanations share: the gospels are not simple transcripts of memory, set down by eyewitnesses recording what they personally saw. They are composite, literary documents, assembled by writers working with earlier written material in front of them — cutting, rearranging, expanding, tightening — the way any ancient historian or biographer worked, weighing sources and making editorial choices a modern reader would recognize immediately. Matthew is doing something to Mark. Luke is doing something to Mark, and to something else besides, whether that something else is Q, Matthew, or an early Marcionite text. Somebody, in every single case, was an editor before anyone thought to call them an evangelist.

That fact alone should have settled the question of whether the gospels are meant to be read as flat, uniform, mutually reinforcing testimony. It hasn't, mostly because most readers have never been told any of this happened. But once you know that Matthew and Luke were both quietly reworking material that predates them — sometimes agreeing down to the word, sometimes disagreeing about where a saying belongs, what year an event happened, or who was even present — it stops being surprising that the four gospels don't actually agree with each other about what happened. It starts being exactly what you'd expect. The next question is simply how far that disagreement goes.
