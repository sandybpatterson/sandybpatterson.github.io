# Chapter Twenty-Two

### The Great Divorce

On Saturday, July 16, 1054, a Roman cardinal named Humbert of Silva Candida walked into the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople during the celebration of the liturgy, approached the high altar in front of the assembled clergy, and set down a parchment. It was a formal bull of excommunication, naming Michael Cerularius, the Patriarch of Constantinople, condemned and cut off from the Christian church. Humbert turned and walked out. According to the account that survives, he shook the dust from his feet at the church door on his way, a deliberate echo of the gospel instruction for what to do when a town refuses to receive the apostles' message. A week later, Cerularius convened his own synod, condemned the document as a forgery and an act of aggression, and excommunicated Humbert and the legates who had traveled with him in return. Two churches, each insisting it was Christianity itself, had just formally cast the other out.

What almost nobody in either city seemed to grasp at the time was that the man Humbert claimed to be acting for no longer held the office. Pope Leo IX, who had sent Humbert to Constantinople and given him the authority to negotiate and, if necessary, to excommunicate, had died in Rome on April 19, 1054 — nearly three months before Humbert ever set foot in the Hagia Sophia. A papal legate's commission does not automatically survive the death of the pope who issued it, and by the time Humbert laid that parchment on the altar, the office backing his authority had been vacant for weeks. Whether Humbert knew and pressed on anyway, or genuinely believed his mandate still held, is not something the surviving sources settle. Either way, the single most famous act of the entire schism was carried out, at least on paper, by a man whose authority to carry it out had already expired.

---

The dispute itself had been building for generations before Humbert ever boarded a ship, and 1054 was not even the first time Rome and Constantinople had tried excommunicating each other. Two centuries earlier, a dispute over who had the right to be patriarch of Constantinople — Photius, an imperial official elected and ordained in a matter of days, versus Ignatius, the man he'd replaced — escalated into Pope Nicholas I excommunicating Photius in 863, and Photius returning the favor in 867 by convening his own council and excommunicating Nicholas along with the entire Western church. That rupture healed within a decade once the political situation in Constantinople changed and a more conciliatory pope worked out an accommodation with Photius directly. But the underlying mistrust it exposed never fully went away, and historians of the period have noted that the same suspicion resurfaced in the eleventh century the moment Rome again started enforcing Latin customs on Greek-rite communities under its authority — this time in southern Italy, the flashpoint that helped set up the confrontation with Cerularius.

Rome and Constantinople also disagreed over whether the Nicene Creed should include the filioque clause — the assertion that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father *and the Son*, not from the Father alone. The phrase had started, of all places, in sixth-century Spain, added locally at the Third Council of Toledo in 589 to shore up the fight against a lingering Arian heresy among the Visigoths, with no ecumenical council anywhere in the process. It spread north from there, and Charlemagne's court pushed hard for its adoption across the Frankish kingdom — hard enough that in 809 he convened a council at Aachen specifically to get Rome's blessing for it. Rome, at that point, refused. Pope Leo III not only declined to approve the addition, he had the creed's original, filioque-free wording engraved on two silver tablets and displayed at St. Peter's, precisely so that no future pope could quietly change it. That refusal held for exactly two centuries. In 1014, the German emperor Henry II arrived in Rome for his own coronation, was apparently startled to find the creed wasn't even being recited at Mass there, and got the reigning pope to adopt the Western, filioque-including version on the spot. Forty years later, that same addition — adopted under a German emperor's direct pressure, after two centuries of a Roman pope's own explicit refusal — was one of the standing grievances Humbert carried into Hagia Sophia. They disagreed as well over whether the bread used in the eucharist should be leavened, as the East insisted, or unleavened, as Rome practiced, a dispute that sounds almost comically narrow to modern ears and mattered enormously to people who believed the wrong bread on the altar was a theological error with eternal consequences. And they disagreed, underneath all three disputes, about something much larger and much harder to negotiate: whether the bishop of Rome held a unique, binding authority over the entire church, as Rome increasingly claimed, or was simply the senior among five roughly equal patriarchs, as Constantinople understood the ancient arrangement. Cerularius had recently closed the Latin-rite churches in Constantinople that used unleavened bread, a provocation in its own right. Humbert's mission, in theory, was to negotiate a settlement. The bull he left on the altar was the negotiation's failure, made permanent in writing.

---

Here is the detail modern historians keep returning to, because it complicates the story most people think they know: 1054 barely registered, at the time, as the founding date of anything. No one in Constantinople or Rome packed up and declared a new, permanent division of Christendom that week. Trade continued. Correspondence between Eastern and Western bishops continued. Ordinary Christians in the empire's Greek-speaking provinces and in the Latin West kept doing what they had always done, mostly unaware that two churchmen had traded condemnations in a cathedral. Historians studying the period have increasingly stressed that no serious scholar treats 1054 itself as the moment the schism actually happened — the split between Eastern and Western Christianity was the outcome of a much longer, slower drift in language, politics, and theology, of which the Humbert-Cerularius exchange was one dramatic scene rather than the whole play. The excommunications were real. Their supposed finality was not.

Cerularius himself did not go on to enjoy a long, triumphant tenure as the patriarch who had stood up to Rome. Four years later, in 1058, the Byzantine emperor Isaac I Komnenos, needing to consolidate his own shaky authority against a patriarch who had grown accustomed to acting as a power in his own right, demanded Cerularius's resignation. When Cerularius refused, Isaac had him arrested and exiled, and moved to charge him with treason and heresy. Cerularius never stood trial. He died in exile in January 1059, before the case against him could be heard — a churchman who had excommunicated a cardinal on behalf of the whole Eastern church, brought down not by Rome but by his own emperor, and denied even the formal reckoning his accusers wanted to give him.

What actually hardened the divide into something people on both sides would carry as permanent, generational grievance happened a century and a half later, and it had nothing to do with unleavened bread.

---

In April 1204, an army raised for the Fourth Crusade — meant, on paper, to retake Jerusalem — instead besieged and sacked Constantinople, the largest and richest Christian city in the world, over three days of looting that its own defenders were supposed to be. Crusaders melted down bronze statues for their metal, stripped churches of relics accumulated over eight centuries, and broke apart the silver altar screen of the Hagia Sophia itself to divide the metal among themselves. Wine was reportedly drunk from the church's own sacred vessels. The Byzantine historian Niketas Choniates, writing as an eyewitness to the aftermath, describes the cathedral's most sacred spaces being treated as plunder rather than as anything holy, and among the details he records is a woman — later tradition calls her a prostitute, though Choniates himself does not use that word and does not claim the crusaders ceremonially placed her there as a deliberate act of humiliation — climbing onto the patriarch's own ceremonial throne, the synthronon, and singing and dancing amid the general chaos of the sack. Whether that specific image happened exactly as later retellings have shaped it, the broader fact it stands in for is not contested: the greatest church in Christendom was ransacked by an army fighting under the cross, and the empire whose capital it was never fully recovered.

It is this — not Humbert's parchment, not Cerularius's countercharge — that Eastern Christians would spend the following eight centuries remembering as the true wound. A theological dispute over a clause in a creed can, in principle, be argued back to reconciliation. An army from your own supposed co-religionists looting your cathedral and toppling your empire is harder to file under theology. The schism that mattered, the one that actually shaped how millions of people on each side would think of the other for the rest of the medieval world's existence, was cemented by soldiers with torches in 1204, a century and a half after two churchmen had already, on paper, declared it settled.

---

The mutual excommunications of 1054 were not formally lifted for nine hundred and eleven years. On December 7, 1965, Pope Paul VI and Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras I of Constantinople jointly issued a declaration — read simultaneously at a public session of the Second Vatican Council in Rome and at a ceremony in Istanbul — removing the anathemas of 1054 from the churches' memory and expressing regret for the offensive words and actions on both sides. It was, by any measure, a genuine and significant act of reconciliation between two churches that had spent nine centuries defining themselves partly in opposition to each other. But the declaration itself was careful, in its own text, not to overclaim what it was accomplishing: it stated plainly that the original censures had never actually broken full ecclesiastical communion between Rome and Constantinople in the first place, and it did not, on its own, restore that communion now. Nine hundred and eleven years spent treating a mutual excommunication as a settled historical fact, formally reversed by a declaration whose own language insisted the excommunication had never quite done what everyone on both sides had assumed it did.

The deeper wound, the one this chapter has argued actually mattered more than Humbert's parchment, took even longer to be formally addressed and still hasn't fully closed. In May 2001, on a visit to Athens, Pope John Paul II spoke directly to the sack of Constantinople, telling Greek Orthodox Archbishop Christodoulos that "some events of the distant past have left deep wounds in the minds and hearts of people to this day," and naming the 1204 assault specifically as an act in which Latin Christians "turned against their own brothers in the faith." Three years later, in 2004, on the eight-hundredth anniversary of the sack, Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew I formally accepted the apology during a liturgy in Istanbul, telling the assembled congregation that "the spirit of reconciliation is stronger than hatred." An apology, like the 1965 declaration before it, is not the same thing as full communion restored. Rome and Constantinople remain, to this day, two separate churches.

---

It is not the first time this book has watched an institution draw a hard line around who counts as legitimately inside it and who has been cast out — Marcion did something structurally similar nine centuries earlier, deciding almost none of the inherited library belonged and forcing the wider church to answer with a canon of its own. What 1054 and 1204 add to that pattern is the other half of the story: how a boundary drawn in one dramatic, badly authorized moment can outlive every person who drew it, get formally reversed by two men standing on opposite sides of a continent nine centuries later, and still leave two churches that, to this day, are not in full communion with each other. The wound this chapter has traced was never really about a parchment on an altar or a woman on a throne. Those were the visible scenes. The actual damage was the kind that keeps happening quietly, for generations, long after anyone remembers exactly how it started.
