# Chapter Twenty

### The Pornocracy

In the autumn of 932, in a fortress overlooking the Tiber, Rome's most powerful woman was getting married for the third time, to a king. Marozia, daughter of one of Rome's ruling families and the mother of a sitting pope, was marrying Hugh of Provence, the King of Italy — a match that would have combined nearly every source of power in the peninsula under one household. The wedding never finished. Her own son from an earlier marriage, a young man named Alberic, turned on the gathering mid-ceremony, raised the city against his mother and new stepfather, and stormed the fortress before the vows were done being celebrated. Hugh escaped the only way left to him, climbing down the fortress walls on a rope in the dark. Marozia did not escape. Neither did the pope. Her own son, the one she'd arranged to have elected two years earlier, was in the building too, and Alberic took them both.

This book has already shown two popes seized, exiled, tried, and killed by the political currents around them. What happened across the ninety-three years bracketed by this chapter is a different scale of the same problem: not individual popes falling to individual conspiracies, but the office itself, for two consecutive generations, functioning as one aristocratic Roman family's household appointment — passed, contested, and occasionally auctioned among relatives with the same practical logic a family might apply to distributing an estate.

---

Twenty-four men held the office of pope between 872 and 965. Run the arithmetic and that's an average reign of well under four years apiece, in an era when a healthy adult pope, once installed, had no obvious reason to expect a short tenure. Some died of ordinary causes. A meaningful number did not, deposed or murdered by rivals as Rome's leading families fought, sometimes with actual armed retainers in the streets, over who got to control the single most prestigious office left in the Western world. At the center of the worst stretch of it sat one family in particular: Theophylact, a Roman aristocrat who held the title of vestararius, effectively the papacy's own treasurer and chief administrator, and his wife Theodora, whom contemporary sources credit with real independent political weight of her own — not merely an aristocrat's spouse but, by title and by evident practice, a senatrix in her own right. Between them, and later through their daughter, the family spent the better part of two generations deciding who got to be pope, and, on at least one well-documented occasion, arranging for the office to stay literally inside the family. Four generations of that family pass through this chapter in short order, and the shape of the tree is simpler than the cast list makes it look: Theophylact and Theodora's daughter Marozia; her sons Alberic II and, on the more contested reading of his parentage, Pope John XI; and Alberic II's own son, who closes the chapter out as Pope John XII. Grandmother, mother, two grandsons, one great-grandson — one family, one office, the better part of a century.

The daughter was Marozia, and no single figure in this stretch of papal history has had her reputation more thoroughly shaped by a single hostile source. Nearly everything salacious this period is remembered for — Theodora's own alleged affair with a pope she'd helped elevate, Marozia's alleged status as the mistress of Pope Sergius III years before her own political rise — traces back to one chronicler, Liutprand of Cremona, the same writer this book's last chapter already caught confusing details between different popes and dressing up a straightforward political trial with an earthquake nobody else recorded. Liutprand wrote decades after most of these events, with an openly hostile agenda toward the family he was describing, in language modern historians increasingly read as doing real, deliberate work to discredit two powerful women by reducing their political authority to sexual scandal — a move this book has by now learned to recognize regardless of which century it shows up in. What survives independent of Liutprand's more colorful claims is damning enough without them: a family that spent two generations installing, controlling, and when necessary imprisoning popes, including, eventually, its own children.

Not every pope this family touched was a puppet without accomplishments of his own, which is worth holding onto before the story gets grimmer. Pope John X, installed in 914, turned out to be a genuinely capable political and military operator — he personally organized a coalition of southern Italian princes against a Saracen stronghold dug in on the Garigliano River, and in 915 rode with the combined army himself to drive the raiders out for good, a pope leading troops in the field rather than merely blessing someone else's. It didn't save him. By the mid-920s his own protectors had died off one by one, and Marozia — who Liutprand claims resented John specifically over her mother Theodora's earlier relationship with him, though the political motive needed no personal one to explain it — marched on Rome with her new husband, had John seized, and had him smothered with a pillow in his own prison cell in May 928. The Garigliano campaign that had made him one of the more genuinely effective popes of the entire medieval period did nothing to protect him from the one family that had decided his usefulness was over.

---

What isn't seriously disputed is that Marozia had a son, probably around 910, who became Pope John XI in 931 — a young man Marozia maneuvered into the papacy while she herself held effective control of Rome, whatever the truth of his paternity, which the sources themselves can't agree on. The near-contemporary chronicler Flodoard names her first husband, Alberic of Spoleto, as the boy's father; Liutprand and the official papal record instead point to Sergius III, the same pope this book's nineteenth chapter already met reversing Formosus's rehabilitation, which would make the story of one dead pope's decade-long fight over legitimacy directly entangled with the parentage of a living one. Either reading makes the same underlying point: a sitting pope's actual job qualification, for at least one installment of the office, was being this particular woman's son.

It was that same son, ironically, who ended his mother's run. Alberic — her child by her first marriage, not John XI's full brother if Sergius III really was the pope's father — had grown into adulthood watching his mother rebuild her own power through a second marriage after his father's death and then reach for a third, to Hugh of Provence, that would have made Rome permanently subordinate to a foreign king's household. Whatever mixture of genuine outrage and plain political calculation drove him, Alberic moved against her at her own wedding, took the city, and kept both his mother and his half-brother the pope as prisoners for the rest of their lives. Marozia died in captivity sometime in the following years, the exact date lost. John XI spent what remained of his own pontificate confined to the Lateran Palace, pope in name, with his own younger half-brother the actual ruler of Rome in every way that mattered.

Alberic then did something almost nobody else in this stretch of the story bothered to try: he ruled Rome directly, as a secular prince, for twenty-two years, without ever once needing to become pope himself. He controlled papal elections completely during that entire span — five popes held the office across his rule, and all five did so with Alberic's direct approval and, by most accounts, at his effective appointment. It was a cleaner, more honest arrangement in its own way than anything that came before it: no more pretense that the office was being contested on spiritual grounds at all, just an acknowledged secular prince picking the men who'd wear the title while he actually ran the city.

---

Alberic's own son inherited the arrangement in the most literal way this book has yet recorded. Before he died in 954, Alberic extracted an oath from Rome's assembled clergy and nobility that his teenage son, Octavian, would be elected pope the moment the current pontiff died. It worked exactly as arranged. In 955, at somewhere around eighteen years old, Octavian became Pope John XII — taking a separate papal name from his baptismal one, a practice this particular succession helped establish as the norm rather than the exception it had been before him — combining, for the first time in this whole ninety-three-year stretch, the secular authority his father had built and the spiritual office his family had spent two generations only ever controlling from outside.

He does not appear to have taken either job especially seriously. The specific charges a Roman synod eventually brought against him in 963 read like an inventory of every worst-case scenario this book's title has been describing in the abstract since Chapter One: sacrilege, for ordaining a deacon in a stable; violence, for blinding and castrating political rivals with his own authority behind it; incest and adultery, for conduct witnesses described as turning the papal court into something closer to a brothel than a church; and, strangest of the specific accusations, invoking the pagan gods Jupiter and Venus for luck while playing dice. The man calling that synod was Otto I — this book's own seventeenth chapter already watched him receive the imperial crown from this same John XII's own hands in 962, a coronation John himself had requested specifically because he needed Otto's protection against his own Italian rivals. It took John less than a year to start conspiring against the emperor who'd just crowned him, and less than two for Otto to come back to Rome and have him formally deposed.

John did not go quietly. He refused to appear before the synod that tried him, sent back a defiant letter instead, and once Otto's army had moved on, John simply returned to Rome, convened a rival synod of his own, declared his deposition invalid, and resumed the papacy by force of will alone — a maneuver this book has watched attempted before and rarely seen work twice. It didn't need to work twice for him. He died within months, in May 964, at around twenty-seven years old, under circumstances that once again trace back mostly to Liutprand, who reports a stroke suffered in the middle of an adulterous encounter, possibly at the hands of an outraged husband rather than any illness at all. Whether the specific scene is true or simply the most satisfying ending Liutprand's own agenda could have asked for, the pattern by now should be familiar: a hostile chronicler, writing after the fact, supplying exactly the death a man like this "deserved" in the telling, whether or not anyone actually witnessed it happen that way.

---

What the ninety-three years between John VIII and John XII actually demonstrate has less to do with any individual pope's personal failings, real or invented, than with what happens to an institution once a single family stops treating it as a spiritual office at all and starts treating it as a hereditary asset. Theophylact's family didn't need to be uniquely wicked to produce this outcome. They needed only to notice, correctly, that the office controlling Western Christendom's moral and administrative authority had no defense against a family willing to simply take it and keep it — no electorate beyond Rome's own aristocracy, no institutional check strong enough to survive determined local force applied across two full generations. The men who eventually broke the pattern didn't do it with theology. Otto did it with an army, the same tool every secular power in this book has reached for once persuasion stopped being enough — and, in a detail neither man could have fully appreciated at the time, John XII himself signed the actual fix into existence. The day after crowning Otto emperor in February 962, John put his own name to the Diploma Ottonianum, a charter granting the emperor formal authority to confirm every future papal election before it could take effect. It took less than a year for John to start conspiring against the very emperor he'd just handed that power to, and less than two for the mechanism he'd personally authorized to be turned directly against him. The office survived the century that did this to it. What it survived with — an external secular check it had just finished demonstrating, at exhausting length, that it could not reliably impose on itself — is the part worth sitting with.
