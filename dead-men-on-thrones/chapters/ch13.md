# Chapter Thirteen

### The Ghost in the Machine

Sometime around 341, a man named Ulfilas — grandson, by one ancient account, of Roman captives a Gothic raiding party had carried off from Cappadocia a generation earlier — was consecrated a bishop and sent north of the Danube to evangelize the people his own ancestors had been taken by. The man who laid hands on him was Eusebius of Nicomedia, a name this book has already met twice: the bishop who spent a decade quietly working the imperial court to rehabilitate Arius after Nicaea, and the same man who baptized Constantine on his deathbed. Whatever else Eusebius believed, he believed it firmly enough to hand it directly to the missionary he was sending beyond the empire's own border, to a people the empire didn't yet fully control and couldn't easily correct.

Ulfilas took the assignment seriously enough to solve a problem nobody had solved yet: Gothic had no alphabet. He built one, adapting Greek and Latin letters to a language that had never been written down, and used it to translate most of the Bible into Gothic — the earliest substantial work of literature in any Germanic language, centuries before English, German, or any of their relatives had anything comparable. He left one deliberate gap. According to later tradition, he skipped the Books of Kings on purpose, on the theory that a people who already fought as readily as the Goths did had no particular need for more scripture about kings waging war. It is possibly the most practical translation decision in the history of Bible translation, and also, on reflection, a fairly good joke at his own converts' expense. The single copy of that translation substantial enough to still be read today, a deluxe sixth-century edition written in silver and gold ink on purple-dyed vellum and now known as the Codex Argenteus, wasn't made in Ulfilas's own lifetime at all — it was produced generations later, in Ravenna, almost certainly for the court of an Ostrogothic king this chapter will return to shortly. It survives now in a library in Uppsala, Sweden, the single most complete physical trace left of a language nobody has spoken in over a thousand years.

What Ulfilas taught the Goths, along with an alphabet and an edited Bible, was not, strictly speaking, Arius's own theology — a distinction later historians have been careful to draw and this book should be too. By the time Ulfilas was consecrated, the argument had moved on from Arius's original formula to something churchmen at the time called Homoian, from the Greek for "like": the Son was like the Father, deliberately without the more precise and more contentious language either Nicaea's homoousios or Arius's own subordinationism had used. It was the version of anti-Nicene Christianity that happened to hold real institutional power at exactly that moment in the middle decades of the fourth century, under an emperor, Constantius II, whose sympathies this book has already described, and it is the version Ulfilas carried north of the Danube and handed to the Goths as, simply, Christianity — not a faction, not a heresy, just what the word meant. That was not a permanent condition of the Roman church. It was a single frame in a fight that had been swinging back and forth since Nicaea and would keep swinging for another forty years, until Theodosius's edict and the Council of Constantinople finally locked Nicene orthodoxy in as the only legal option, this book's own last two chapters. Ulfilas's timing simply happened to fall on the wrong side of that eventual outcome. The Goths, converted just before the pendulum's final swing, kept the version of Christianity they'd actually been handed. Rome moved on without them.

---

That single accident of timing did not stay contained to one people in one place. Gothic Arianism spread, as the Goths themselves spread, to neighboring and related Germanic peoples across the fourth and fifth centuries — the Vandals, who would go on to rule North Africa; the Burgundians, settling in the Rhone valley; the Lombards, who would eventually take Italy itself. By the time the western half of the Roman Empire had fully come apart, in 476, most of the major powers replacing it across the old imperial map professed a form of Christianity the empire's own church had spent more than half a century declaring heretical.

One major exception is worth naming precisely, because it's easy to flatten this into "the Germanic tribes were Arian" as a simple, uniform fact, and the fact isn't uniform. The Franks, under their king Clovis, converted directly to Nicene Catholicism — traditionally dated to Christmas Day, 496, though historians have never fully settled the year and some push it as late as 508 — when Bishop Remigius of Reims baptized him along with, tradition says, three thousand of his soldiers in a single ceremony. It skipped the Arian phase almost every other major Germanic power had passed through, and did so, by most accounts, at least partly as a calculated alliance with the Catholic bishops and the Gallo-Roman population already living under Frankish rule in Gaul. It gave Clovis's dynasty a durable advantage the Arian kingdoms around it lacked: legitimacy, from day one, with the majority religion of the people it governed. The distinction mattered enormously to everyone living through it at the time, even if popular shorthand today tends to smooth it away.

---

Here is the irony this chapter can describe but not fully resolve, because it never fully resolved itself at the time either. The Catholic, Nicene population of the former Western Roman Empire — the ordinary provincial Christians whose bishops had spent from 325 to 381 building the entire theological and legal machinery this book's last several chapters have covered — spent the following century as religious minorities inside their own former empire, ruled by Arian Germanic kings who had inherited Rome's territory without inheriting Rome's finally-settled theology. The heresy Nicaea condemned in a single summer, and the Roman state spent fifty-six years and multiple emperors making sure stayed condemned, walked back in through the door Rome's own collapse had left open, carried by the people doing the collapsing.

Not every Arian kingdom left it at coexistence, and the Vandals in North Africa are the reason that sentence needs qualifying before it goes any further. Having crossed from Spain into Africa in 429 and taken Carthage itself by 439, the Vandal kings ran the harshest version of Arian rule anywhere in the former West. Huneric, who took the throne in 477, spent his reign forcing the issue directly: Catholic clergy exiled, Catholic churches handed over to Arian congregations, laypeople in royal service dismissed or beaten for refusing to convert. In February 484 he staged a conference in Carthage between Catholic and Arian bishops, ostensibly to settle the theological dispute once and for all — and used its predetermined outcome as the pretext for a kingdom-wide edict of persecution issued immediately afterward, an edict that, as Huneric's own chancery openly acknowledged, simply recycled the anti-heretical laws earlier Catholic Roman emperors had once written to use against groups that included, not incidentally, the Arians themselves. A Catholic bishop named Victor of Vita lived through it and wrote it down, chapter and beating by chapter and beating, in a history of the persecution that survives specifically because he judged it too important to let the winners write alone. Set beside Huneric's Africa, Theodoric's Italy looks less like the normal condition of Arian rule over a Catholic population and more like the exception that happened to get remembered.

Theodoric the Ostrogoth, ruling Italy itself from 493 to 526, is the clearest single case of what that gentler version actually looked like in practice, and it does not resolve neatly into either a story of persecution or a story of harmony. For most of his long reign, Theodoric — an Arian himself, ruling a population that was overwhelmingly Catholic — left Catholic worship alone, protected it as a matter of policy, and even intervened diplomatically on behalf of Arians being persecuted elsewhere in the Mediterranean, seeing himself as something like a protector of both communities at once. That held until his final years, when growing tension with the Catholic emperor in Constantinople curdled into real paranoia at his own court. In 525, convinced — on evidence historians still debate the strength of — that two of his most senior Roman officials, the philosopher Boethius and his father-in-law Symmachus, were conspiring against him with the East, Theodoric had them executed, almost certainly without anything resembling a fair hearing. A reign that had spent three decades demonstrating that an Arian king and a Catholic population could coexist ended with two of that population's most respected men dead on suspicion the record can no longer fully verify.

The arrangement did eventually end, kingdom by kingdom, though not quickly and not everywhere at once, and not always by the same route. The Lombards, who took most of Italy in the decades after Theodoric's Ostrogothic kingdom collapsed, seem to have held their Arianism more loosely than the Goths or Vandals ever had, and gave it up without any single dramatic confrontation — worn down over the late sixth and seventh centuries by a Bavarian-born queen named Theodelinda, married in succession to two Lombard kings, who spent her decades on the throne cultivating Pope Gregory the Great and the Catholic bishops around her court until the conversion had simply happened, more or less peacefully, underneath her. Visigothic Spain took the more theatrical route, and held out the longest as a genuinely Arian-ruled state: not until 589, when King Reccared stood before the Third Council of Toledo, read a public profession of the Nicene faith, and formally renounced Arianism on behalf of his entire kingdom — the culmination of years of work by a Catholic bishop named Leander of Seville, who had spent a long campaign converting the Visigothic royal house one member at a time before finally landing the king himself. It took two hundred and eight years, by that count, from Theodosius's edict to the last major Arian Germanic kingdom formally giving the position up.

---

None of this was a story anyone at Nicaea or Constantinople could have anticipated, which is exactly what makes it worth sitting with rather than smoothing into a tidier shape than it actually had. The empire won its own internal argument as completely as this book has already shown — a creed built to make the losing side unrecitable, a legal code that made deviation a form of madness, a council that finished the job in 381. Two and a half centuries earlier, the church had excommunicated a shipowner for arguing that Christianity should be simpler and more settled than the messy, multi-voiced thing it actually was, and had spent the centuries since building exactly the kind of unified, enforceable, single-answer orthodoxy Marcion himself might almost have recognized as a rival version of his own instinct — one truth, one text, one creed, no more arguing. And then the empire that had done all that winning collapsed anyway, on a schedule that had nothing to do with theology, and the people who inherited its western half turned out to be carrying a version of the very argument Rome thought it had closed for good. Marcion never managed to make the settled version stick by argument. History, it turned out, could unmake it by accident. The fight didn't get lost. It got exported, decades after Rome itself had stopped having it, to people who never knew they'd been handed the losing position in someone else's already-finished war.

What was left of the empire's authority, once the western half of it stopped existing as a political fact at all, had to come from somewhere else entirely — and by 476, there was only one institution left standing across the whole of the former West with enough structure, enough literate administrators, and enough recognized authority to even attempt holding the pieces together.
