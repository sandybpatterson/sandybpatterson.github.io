# Chapter One

### Before the Book

In the autumn of the year 107 or thereabouts — the exact year is argued over by people who have devoted their careers to arguing over it, and we will not settle it here — a bishop named Ignatius was marched out of Antioch in chains, handed over to ten Roman soldiers he described as leopards, and started walking toward Rome to be killed.

He knew where this was going. Rome fed condemned men to animals in the arena as a form of public entertainment, and Ignatius had been sentenced to exactly that. He had weeks, maybe longer, to think about it while he walked. What he did with that time was write letters. Seven of them survive, addressed to churches in the cities his route passed near or through — Ephesus, Magnesia, Tralles, Philadelphia, Smyrna — plus one to the Christians already in Rome, begging them not to intervene on his behalf, and one to a bishop named Polycarp, who he clearly loved and worried about in the specific way an old man worries about a younger one he's leaving behind.

Read those seven letters back to back and something becomes obvious fast: Ignatius is not writing to branches of a single, uniform institution. He is writing to strangers. Each community has its own customs, its own leadership structure, its own sense of what matters most, its own emerging sense of what counts as dangerous nonsense from a rival teacher down the road. He begs the Ephesians to stay unified under their bishop. He warns the Trallians about people who deny Christ suffered in the flesh. He tells the Philadelphians a story about a dispute he had with some Christians who apparently didn't accept certain claims unless they could find them written down — "in the archives," he says they told him, meaning the Jewish scriptures — and Ignatius's own answer to that challenge is revealing: he says his archive is Jesus Christ. Not a book. A person, and the oral memory of what that person did.

That is the entire problem of this chapter in a single anecdote. In the year 107, less than eighty years after the crucifixion, a bishop being marched to his death could not point his fellow Christians to an agreed set of Christian writings and say *this is ours, read it, you'll find your answer there.* No such set existed. Nobody had made one yet. Nobody had been asked to.

---

It is worth sitting with how strange this is, because most people who grew up around Christianity in any form absorb the opposite impression without ever being told it directly. The impression is that the New Testament arrived more or less as a package — that the apostles wrote it, the church received it, and everyone since has just been reading the same book Peter and Paul would have recognized. The actual first century and a half of the Jesus movement looked nothing like that, and the gap between the impression and the reality is where this entire book begins.

Paul's letters, the earliest Christian writings we have, were occasional documents — mail, essentially, fired off to specific congregations dealing with specific arguments, never intended to sit inside a single bound anthology. They circulated individually for decades before anyone started collecting them, and even once collections started forming in different cities, the collections didn't match. A Christian in Corinth around the year 100 might have access to a stack of Paul's letters copied by hand from other copies, full of the small errors and adjustments that happen every time a scribe recopies a document by candlelight. A Christian in Rome might have a different stack. Neither of them would have thought of what they possessed as "the Bible," because that idea — a single, closed, agreed-upon anthology — did not exist yet for anyone to possess.

The situation with the gospels was even less settled. Mark, most scholars think, was written first, sometime around the year 70, in the shadow of Rome's destruction of the Jerusalem temple. Matthew and Luke followed a decade or two later, both apparently drawing on Mark and on other material, written for different audiences with different emphases — Matthew clearly writing for Jewish Christians anxious about their relationship to the law, Luke writing for a wider Greco-Roman audience and going out of his way to make the story make sense to them. John came last, likely sometime between 90 and 110, and reads like a different kind of document altogether — less a chronicle, more a theological meditation dressed as a narrative.

Four gospels. But not *the* four gospels, not yet, not as a fixed and universally recognized set. In the same decades those four were circulating, so were others, in different communities, with different followings, some of which were substantial. A Gospel of Thomas — a list of sayings attributed to Jesus with almost no narrative at all — circulated in Egypt and Syria and had real readers who took it seriously as a source of Jesus's actual words. A Gospel of Peter was read in at least one Syrian congregation until a bishop investigated it in the late second century and decided parts of it were theologically suspect. A Gospel of the Hebrews, now lost except in fragments quoted by later writers, was used by Jewish-Christian groups who had their own distinctive sense of who Jesus was and what continuity with Judaism required. In Syria, a theologian named Tatian would eventually solve the "which gospel" problem in his own region by an entirely different route: around the 170s he wove all four familiar gospels into a single continuous harmony called the Diatessaron, and for generations afterward that composite text — not the four separate books — was what Syriac-speaking Christians actually read in church.

None of this was fringe. None of it was secret. It was simply what Christianity looked like before anyone had the authority, the motive, or arguably even the occasion to draw a hard line around a set of books and say: these, and only these.

---

There is a genuine scholarly argument about how chaotic this really was, and the honest version of this book has to name it rather than pretend the mess was total. The strong version of that argument, associated with the German scholar Walter Bauer, held that in many regions what later got labeled "heresy" actually came first and was simply the local Christianity, with Roman-flavored orthodoxy arriving afterward and rewriting the history of who was original and who was the deviation. Most historians today think Bauer overstated his case — the picture on the ground varied enormously by region, and plenty of communities did hold something recognizably continuous with what became orthodox teaching from very early on. But even scholars who reject Bauer's strongest claims agree on the underlying fact this chapter is built around: there was no single, centrally enforced Christian orthodoxy with an agreed textual basis in the first and much of the second century. There were overlapping, argumentative, locally rooted Christian communities, reading different combinations of texts, none of which had been declared canonical because the concept of a canon — a closed, fixed, authoritative list — had not yet been invented for Christian use.

The closest thing to evidence of that concept starting to form comes from a Christian philosopher named Justin Martyr, writing in Rome in the mid-150s. Describing an ordinary Sunday gathering, Justin says that "the memoirs of the apostles" — what he elsewhere makes clear means the gospels — were read aloud alongside the writings of the Jewish prophets, for as long as time allowed, followed by a sermon from whoever was presiding. It's a small, almost throwaway detail, and it is one of the most important sentences in this entire chapter, because it shows something new happening: certain texts acquiring the status of scripture in practice, read in worship the way the Hebrew scriptures already were, decades before anyone wrote down a list of exactly which texts qualified.

A document usually called the Muratorian Fragment, conventionally dated to around 180 AD, is often presented as the first attempt at that list — a battered, badly copied Latin manuscript naming most, though not all, of what would become the New Testament, while including a couple of books that ultimately didn't make the final cut. That conventional dating is not unanimous; at least one modern scholar has argued the fragment actually belongs to the fourth century, which would mean the "first canon list" is centuries later than the textbooks usually say, and the debate has never fully closed. Even taking the earlier date at face value, what the fragment shows is not a finished canon. It shows a snapshot of one community's opinion, in one place, still visibly in progress, still capable of disagreement about the edges.

Which is the point. For roughly a hundred and fifty years — longer than the United States existed before its Civil War — the Jesus movement operated as a genuinely plural, textually open, regionally varied set of communities, each convinced they had the truth, none of them possessing an agreed book to prove it, because the book did not exist. Nobody in the year 100 owned anything resembling what a modern reader means by "a Bible." That object — a single, portable, complete collection between two covers — was not just unavailable to ordinary Christians; it was centuries away from being technologically possible at all, since it required the codex to fully replace the scroll and a canon to actually be settled before anyone would bother binding one. What existed instead was closer to a loose, sprawling, hand-copied library, spread across a few dozen cities around the Mediterranean and beyond, no two collections quite alike, no central office anyone could write to and ask which version was correct.

None of this was experienced, by the people actually living inside it, as a crisis waiting for a solution. From the inside, this world could be extraordinary in ways the tidier, more centralized church that eventually replaced it rarely matched. Prophets spoke without a script in ordinary meetings and were taken seriously. Women hosted congregations in their own homes and are remembered, in Paul's own letters, by name and by title — Phoebe as a deacon, Prisca as a teacher, Junia as "outstanding among the apostles" — in terms later centuries would spend considerable effort explaining away. A stranger who showed up in Corinth or Philippi wasn't handed a rulebook; they were fed, at a shared meal called the agape feast, by people who had decided, with no institution telling them to, that this newcomer belonged. The plurality this chapter has mostly described as a problem was also, for the people living inside it, a kind of freedom: small, improvised, locally accountable communities, answerable to no distant office, in the brief window before anyone with real power arrived to decide that freedom was itself the problem.

This was not a system waiting to be perfected. It was a working arrangement that suited a movement without the power, the infrastructure, or frankly the need to enforce uniformity. Institutions do not consolidate authority over documents until documents start being used to make competing claims to authority — and for a hundred and fifty years, the claims mostly didn't need settling, because no one had yet found a way to make the plurality itself into a crisis.

That was about to change. Not because Rome decided the mess needed cleaning up, and not because a council convened to settle it. It changed because one man, a wealthy shipowner from a port city on the Black Sea, looked at the same sprawling, uncatalogued library every other Christian had inherited and decided almost none of it belonged. He would keep one gospel, heavily edited. He would keep ten of Paul's letters and discard the rest of the library entirely. And when the church in Rome refused to go along with him, he would not quietly disappear — he would build a rival church large enough to worry the one that expelled him, and in doing so, force the entire fractured, plural, argumentative Jesus movement to finally answer a question it had spent a century and a half avoiding: what, exactly, is ours to read.

His name was Marcion. He is where this book actually begins.
