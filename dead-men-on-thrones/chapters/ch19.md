# Chapter Nineteen

### The Dead Man on the Throne

In January of 897, inside the Lateran basilica in Rome, a pope sat on trial for his life. He had been dead for nine months.

His name was Formosus, and the men who dug him up for the occasion had dressed him back in the full papal vestments he'd been buried in, then propped the body upright on the papal throne itself, arranging the decomposed hands into something resembling the posture of a pontiff receiving a hearing. Standing beside the corpse, because even a show trial needs the appearance of due process, was a young deacon assigned to answer the charges on the dead man's behalf — to speak, in the first person, as Formosus, to a room full of living bishops asking a rotting body why it had done what it was accused of doing. The presiding pope, Stephen VI, put the first question to the corpse directly. When you were bishop of Porto, he reportedly demanded, why did you usurp the universal Roman see in such a spirit of ambition? The corpse, understandably, did not answer. The deacon, presumably in a voice doing a great deal of work to sound like justice being served, answered for it. Whoever staged the proceedings had clearly thought through the choreography with real care — the vestments, the throne, the appointed advocate — and had not, at any stage of the planning, paused to notice that no amount of correct procedure was going to make a nine-month-old corpse a plausible defendant. That gap between the ceremony's obsessive formality and the plain fact of what was actually sitting in the chair is, more or less, the whole story this chapter has to tell.

This book's own last chapter left the papacy in the early twelfth century, at the tail end of a fight over investiture two whole generations of popes and emperors had spent their lives on. This one goes backward — nearly two hundred years backward — because the argument this part of the book is actually making was never really about a clean march of dates. It's about what an unrestrained claim on that one office can produce, in any given century, once the men running it decide the normal rules don't apply to them. The ugliest early version of that argument happened here, in Rome, decades before Canossa was a word anyone needed.

---

Formosus had not had an easy road to that throne even while he was using it under his own power. Long before any of this, in 866, Pope Nicholas I sent him as a papal legate to the newly Christianizing Bulgarian court of Prince Boris — a mission Formosus handled well enough that Boris personally petitioned Rome the following year to have him installed as Bulgaria's own archbishop, a foreign ruler specifically requesting this one Roman churchman by name. Rome said no; a sitting bishop couldn't simply be reassigned to a more prestigious post whenever a prince took a liking to him, whatever the prince himself wanted. Formosus had become bishop of the Italian city of Porto around the same period, and spent years afterward under a cloud his enemies never let him forget: Pope John VIII excommunicated him at one point for allegedly still angling for that Bulgarian archbishopric, and for the papacy itself, while he was supposed to be tending his own diocese, and for fleeing Rome rather than answering the charge in person. The excommunication didn't stick — his successor, Pope Marinus, lifted it and restored Formosus to his see — but the accusation of having wanted a bigger job too openly, too early, followed him for the rest of his life. It is more or less exactly the charge that would eventually get his corpse dug back up: not that he'd been incompetent, but that he'd been good enough at his assignments that people kept wanting to promote him past the boundaries canon law said he was allowed to cross.

He became pope himself in 891, at which point Italian politics did what Italian politics kept doing to popes throughout this book's fourth part: it made him choose a side he didn't necessarily want to choose. The powerful Spoleto family, led by Duke Guy and backed by his fiercely ambitious wife Ageltrude, had spent years muscling the papacy into legitimizing their own dynasty's imperial ambitions, and in 892 they pressured Formosus into crowning Guy's son Lambert co-emperor alongside his father — a coronation extracted under duress rather than offered as an endorsement. Formosus spent the next several years looking for a way out from under Spoleto control, and in February 896 he found one: he invited Arnulf of Carinthia, the East Frankish king, to march into Italy and crowned him emperor instead, a direct and unmistakable slap at the family that had spent years treating the papacy as its own household appointment. It should have worked. Arnulf, for reasons this book can only call spectacularly bad timing, suffered a paralyzing stroke roughly two weeks after his own coronation and had to be carried back north to Bavaria, leaving Formosus without the protector he'd just risked everything to install and the Spoleto family with every reason in the world to want him gone. Formosus died on the fourth of April, 896, of what the surviving sources simply call natural causes, before anyone on either side had to find out what his gamble would have cost him while he was still alive to pay it.

What followed was not a smooth transition. Formosus's immediate successor, Boniface VI, lasted fifteen days on the throne before dying — most accounts say of gout, which is a genuinely unglamorous way for a pope to exit this book after only two weeks in office. The man who followed him, elected on the twenty-second of May, 896, was Stephen VI: a longtime critic of Formosus, closely aligned with the Spoleto faction that had just watched its careful control of the papacy get openly defied, and evidently a man who did not believe a political grievance became less actionable simply because its target had already died.

---

The formal charges Stephen's synod brought against the corpse were, on paper, specific and almost bureaucratically dull, which somehow makes the setting even stranger: that Formosus had committed perjury, that he had improperly exercised the office of bishop while technically still holding the see of Porto — canon law at the time frowned on a bishop transferring from one diocese to another, treating a bishop's bond to his original see as something close to a marriage that couldn't simply be dissolved for a better offer — and that he had coveted and seized the papacy out of raw ambition rather than legitimate election. Stephen's own synod found the defendant guilty on all counts, which was never seriously in doubt given that the prosecutor, the judges, and the political faction funding the whole proceeding were the same people. Every act of Formosus's papacy was declared retroactively null and void — every ordination, every ecclesiastical appointment, erased at a stroke, which meant that a meaningful fraction of the clergy currently serving across Western Christendom woke up to learn they were no longer, in the church's own newly revised opinion, actually clergy.

The corpse itself did not escape more lightly than its legacy did. Stripped of the papal vestments it had been re-dressed in for the trial, it had the three fingers of its right hand — the fingers used to give a blessing — hacked off, a piece of theater specific and pointed enough that nobody watching could have missed the intended message: this hand will never again confer legitimate authority on anyone. What remained was dragged out of the Lateran, paraded through the streets, and thrown into the Tiber, denied even the dignity of a marked grave. Later, more excitable chroniclers added an earthquake to the story, the Lateran basilica itself supposedly shuddering in protest at what had just happened inside it — a detail that shows up in exactly the kind of source that likes its villains punished by the heavens on schedule, and that modern historians treat with real skepticism precisely because it's the detail every dramatic retelling wants to be true. What can be said without embellishment is grim enough on its own: a nine-months-dead pope, propped up, interrogated, convicted, mutilated, and thrown in a river, by the direct order of the man currently occupying the office Formosus himself had held less than a year earlier.

---

Rome's reaction to all of this was not the quiet, orderly relief Stephen presumably hoped for. According to the accounts that survive, popular horror at the spectacle curdled fast into open unrest, and whatever political capital the Spoleto faction thought it had purchased with the trial evaporated within months. Somewhere along the way — tradition credits a monk, some versions a fisherman who simply happened to be working the right stretch of river — Formosus's body was quietly pulled back out of the Tiber and given a proper burial, out of Stephen's reach and, for the moment, out of the story. Stephen himself didn't outlast the year in office. By August 897 he had been deposed and thrown into prison, and not long afterward he was strangled to death by persons the surviving record never bothers to name — an ending that reads less like justice restored than like one faction simply losing its grip on power to the next one in line, the same cycle this book's next chapter is about to spend an entire century watching repeat.

The dead man got the better ending, for whatever a better ending is worth once you're already dead. Pope Theodore II, who held the office for barely three weeks at the end of 897, used almost all of that time to reverse everything: he formally annulled the Cadaver Synod's verdict, ordered Formosus's body retrieved and reburied with full honors inside St. Peter's Basilica itself, redressed in papal vestments one final time, and banned any future trial of a dead person as a matter of church law — a prohibition that, whatever else can be said about the medieval papacy, it has never once needed to reissue. His successor, John IX, went further still, convening his own council to formally condemn Stephen's proceedings and burn the official record of them. Even that wasn't the end of it. Sergius III, who became pope himself in 904 and had reportedly been present as one of the judges at the original Cadaver Synod, used his own papacy to reverse the reversal — reaffirming Formosus's condemnation and demanding that bishops he'd ordained be re-ordained all over again, as though the man's legitimacy were a light switch some new pope got to flip every few years depending on which faction currently held Rome. Seven popes and one antipope held the office in the eight years between Formosus's death and Sergius's eventual, grimly permanent word on the matter. A man who'd been safely, uncontroversially dead the entire time remained, for the better part of a decade, the single most contested figure in the Western church. Somewhere in the middle of all this sat an unglamorous but very real practical problem for hundreds of ordinary priests who had nothing to do with any of it: a man ordained by Formosus was canonically a real priest under Theodore and John, canonically not a priest at all under Stephen and later Sergius, and then, depending on which council's ruling happened to be currently in force in his particular corner of Italy, potentially not a priest again — expected, each time the ruling flipped, to either keep quietly saying Mass as though nothing had changed or stop entirely and wait for the next pope to sort it out. Nobody's actual vocation had changed in the slightest. Only the paperwork had, four separate times, depending entirely on which family controlled Rome that particular year.

None of what happened to Formosus was really about Formosus by the time it was happening. It was about whether the office he'd once held could be made to answer, retroactively, to whichever family controlled Rome in any given month — and the corpse on the throne, however much it looks in hindsight like the single most grotesque image this book has produced, is really just the most literal version of an argument that had been running under every other chapter in this part of the book all along: an institution built to outlast its own occupants can be turned, by the wrong hands, into a machine for punishing them even after they've stopped being able to answer back.
