# Chapter Twenty-One

### The Babylonian Captivity

On the eighth of September, 1303, an armed force of roughly two thousand men rode into the small hill town of Anagni, southeast of Rome, and cornered an old man inside his own family palace. Pope Boniface VIII was in his late sixties, and by every account still defiant — dressed, one chronicler insists, in full papal regalia, tiara on his head, as though sitting for the moment in the posture of the office itself might be the only weapon he had left. The men who'd come for him worked for Philip IV of France and an Italian noble family, the Colonna, whom Boniface had spent years grinding down. For three days they held him. He may have been struck; he was certainly humiliated, and Rome's own dumbfounded reaction did more damage than force. A month later the old pope was dead. What the Outrage of Anagni actually proved, to the next man who wanted the job, was blunt: the king of France could reach into Italy, put his hands on the pope himself, and walk away having only lost time — and Rome's other great families could all watch it happen and not lift a finger to stop it.

Two years later, in 1305, the cardinals elected a Frenchman, the Archbishop of Bordeaux, as Pope Clement V. He never set foot in Rome. He was crowned in Lyon, spent several years moving among French towns while the political weather settled, and in 1309 he did something no pope had done before: he set up permanent residence not in the city that had made the office what it was, but in Avignon, a small city on the Rhône that at the time sat just outside the kingdom of France proper, under the nominal control of the Angevin counts of Provence — close enough to Philip's reach to matter, and just far enough outside his direct borders that Clement could tell himself, and everyone else, that he hadn't simply moved the papacy into the French king's pocket. Anagni is the reason that story is even plausible. A man who had just watched what Philip IV could do to a pope who defied him did not need much persuading to build his palace somewhere the same king's soldiers were already comfortable.

---

Six more popes followed Clement into Avignon, and for close to seventy years — historians count roughly 1309 to 1377 — the bishop of Rome simply wasn't a resident of Rome at all. It is tempting, looking back, to imagine this stretch as one long uninterrupted humiliation, a papacy dragged around in chains behind the French crown. The truth is less dramatic and more institutional: the Avignon popes built, if anything, a more efficient, more centralized, more bureaucratically sophisticated papal government than Rome had ever managed on its own turf. They systematized taxation across the Catholic world, formalized how benefices were granted, and ran what was, for its century, one of the most competent chanceries in Europe. Clement VI, the fourth of the seven, bought Avignon itself outright from its Angevin ruler in 1348, which tells you plainly enough that nobody sitting in that palace in the 1340s was treating the arrangement as temporary. Whatever the popes told visiting delegations from Rome about wanting to come home eventually, the men actually running the office had built themselves a permanent capital and paid cash for the title deed.

Rome itself, left behind, did not simply wait patiently for its bishop to come home. The Lateran basilica, the pope's actual cathedral and the historic seat of the office since the fourth century, burned in a fire in May 1308 that gutted its roof and damaged the adjoining palace — a disaster Clement V ordered rebuilt from Avignon, in absentia, without ever seeing the result. Without a resident papal court to draw them, the pilgrims and prelates and litigants whose spending had been Rome's real economy for a thousand years mostly stopped coming, and a city that had once held perhaps a million people under the empire, and still tens of thousands in Boniface VIII's day, shrank further still, hollowed out by that lost income and then hit hard by the Black Death in 1348 and a second wave of plague in 1361. By the time a pope finally returned, the Lateran itself was in poor enough repair that the papacy would eventually abandon it altogether in favor of the Vatican, across the river, near St. Peter's — the seat of the Roman church quietly relocating within its own city because the old one hadn't survived the absence intact.

What Avignon cost the papacy wasn't competence. It was the thing competence can't buy back: the plain visual fact, obvious to every pilgrim and every bishop and every king in Christendom, that the successor of Peter now lived under the French crown's shadow, in a city France effectively controlled, answering — however the popes themselves might have denied it — to pressures no independent pontiff should have had to answer to at all. By the middle of the fourteenth century the arrangement had a name attached to it, and not a flattering one. The Italian poet and scholar Petrarch, writing around 1350, called Avignon "the Babylon of the west," reaching for the same image the Book of Revelation uses for a great, corrupt, exiled power — deliberately invoking the Hebrew exiles' seventy years in ancient Babylon to describe what he saw as the church's own captivity to a foreign crown. The label stuck. Two centuries later, Martin Luther would reach for it again, titling one of his own foundational Reformation treatises *On the Babylonian Captivity of the Church* — a direct inheritance of Petrarch's phrase, aimed now at an entirely different set of grievances, but proof that the image Petrarch coined about one specific seventy-year exile had enough force in it to still be doing rhetorical work two hundred years later.

---

Getting the papacy back to Rome took more than nostalgia, and the honest account of what actually moved Pope Gregory XI, the last of the seven Avignon popes, to make the trip in 1377 has two threads pulling at once. The tidier version of this story credits Catherine of Siena, a Dominican tertiary and mystic not yet thirty years old, who wrote Gregory a series of forceful letters urging him back to Rome and eventually traveled to Avignon herself to press the case in person. Catherine was a genuinely formidable figure — canonized within a century of her death and later named a doctor of the church, one of only a handful of women ever given that title — and Gregory did meet with her, and her letters do survive making exactly this argument. But historians are considerably more cautious than popular retellings about how much weight to put on her influence alone, and the caution is earned, because Gregory had a second, entirely material reason to leave that had nothing to do with anyone's spiritual counsel.

That reason was already burning through Tuscany by the time Catherine wrote to him. In 1375, Florence had organized a league of Italian cities in open revolt against the Papal States' own administration — heavy-handed, unpopular papal legates governing large stretches of central Italy had provoked exactly the kind of rebellion any absentee landlord eventually earns. Gregory excommunicated Florence, placed the city under interdict, and found himself fighting what came to be called the War of the Eight Saints, a conflict that ran from 1375 into 1378 and directly threatened the Papal States' hold on the very territory the pope needed intact if Rome was ever going to be governable again. Returning to Italy wasn't, for Gregory, a purely devotional gesture answering a young mystic's plea. It was a head of state going to secure his own collapsing territory in person, because sending legates clearly wasn't working anymore. Catherine's appeal and the war's pressure point the same direction and arrived in the same years, which is exactly why untangling them is hard — and exactly why the honest answer is that both were real, and neither alone fully explains what happened. Gregory reached Rome in January 1377. The war with Florence would not actually be settled until the following summer, at Tivoli, months after he'd already made the return trip.

---

Gregory died the next year, in March 1378, and what happened next turned a seventy-year relocation into something considerably worse. A Roman mob, determined this time to get an Italian pope after seven straight Frenchmen, surrounded the conclave and made its preference violently clear. The cardinals — mostly French themselves — elected Bartolomeo Prignano, the Archbishop of Bari, who took the name Urban VI. He was, in fact, Italian, which should have settled the mob's actual demand. It did not settle anything else.

Urban turned out to be volatile, harsh, and apparently uninterested in softening any of it once he had the office — publicly berating cardinals, threatening reform of the very body that had just elevated him, behaving, by nearly every account from the men around him, like someone whose personality had not survived contact with unlimited power. Four months after his election, the same cardinals who had chosen him announced that the election itself had been invalid, because they'd voted under duress from the Roman mob and Urban was therefore no true pope at all. The claim may well have some truth in it — a mob outside the conclave is not nothing. But four months is a long time to sit on a claim of coercion, and it is difficult, watching what actually drove the break, to avoid the plainer explanation: the cardinals hadn't found Urban's election newly illegitimate. They'd found Urban himself newly unbearable, and duress was the legal argument that let them undo a decision they'd made freely and now deeply regretted. In September 1378 they elected a second pope, Robert of Geneva, who took the name Clement VII and promptly returned to Avignon — giving Christendom, for the first time, two living men each claiming to be the one legitimate successor of Peter, each with his own cardinals, his own court, and his own claim to obedience. The Western Schism had begun.

---

It would take four decades and one especially instructive false start to end it. In 1409, a council convened at Pisa specifically to resolve the standoff, and it did something that sounded, on paper, entirely reasonable: it declared both existing claimants deposed and elected a fresh candidate, Alexander V, as the one true pope everyone could now unite behind. Neither of the deposed men, unsurprisingly, agreed that a council two hundred miles away had the authority to fire him. Both stayed exactly where they were, still claiming the office, still commanding obedience from whichever kings and bishops still backed them. Pisa hadn't ended the schism. It had done the one thing that seemed impossible going in: it made a two-way problem into a three-way one, with a third pope now sitting on top of the two the council had meant to remove. The mess would not actually resolve until the Council of Constance, 1414 to 1418, finally engineered what Pisa had only pretended to — the real, negotiated resignation or deposition of all three claims at once, clearing the field for a single new election that everyone, this time, actually accepted.

It is hard, looking at 1409, not to think of a different kind of unauthorized correction to church authority — a man two and a half centuries earlier who'd looked at the church's own inherited scripture, decided most of it didn't belong, and simply removed it by his own authority rather than waiting for anyone to agree with him first. Marcion's solution had at least been clean: keep what he judged worth keeping, discard the rest, and let the resulting rival stand or fall on its own. The cardinals at Pisa tried something structurally similar — declare the existing claims illegitimate, install a corrected version in their place — and discovered what Marcion, for all his trouble, never had to: unmaking an established claim to authority is far easier to attempt than to make actually stick. Marcion built a durable rival because he never pretended the old church would simply accept his edit. Pisa's cardinals assumed exactly that, and got a third pope instead of a solution.

None of the three Avignon-era ruptures — the move itself, the schism, Pisa's failed fix — was inevitable in the sense of being written into the office from its founding. Each was the result of particular men making particular choices under particular pressure: Clement V choosing safety over Rome, Gregory's cardinals choosing a mob's demand over their own judgment and then regretting it, a council choosing the appearance of a solution over the harder work of a real one. The papacy would survive all three, as it had survived worse. But for most of a century, the office that claimed unbroken authority back to Peter himself could not even manage to agree, among the men who held it, on how many of them there currently were.
