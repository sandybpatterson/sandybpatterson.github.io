# Chapter Fifteen

### The Monk Who Made the Papacy

Rome in 590 was burying its dead faster than anyone could count them. A plague that had already worked its way across Italy hit the city that spring with a force contemporaries described in terms usually reserved for divine punishment, and one of its victims, in February, was the sitting pope, Pelagius II. The man Rome's clergy and people turned to next did not want the job. He was a monk by then, had been for over a decade, and by his own later account spent the weeks after his election trying to avoid consecration entirely — writing to the emperor in Constantinople asking that the result be overturned, and, according to a story that started circulating not long after his death, attempting to slip out of the city in disguise before a search party dragged him back. What he did agree to do, once it was clear the office wasn't going to let him go, was lead the city in a mass litany procession through the streets, begging for the plague's end. Later tradition placed him passing the ancient tomb of the emperor Hadrian at the head of that procession and seeing, over its walls, the Archangel Michael sheathing a sword in the sky — a vision so lastingly attached to the spot that Hadrian's mausoleum is still called the Castel Sant'Angelo today, the Castle of the Holy Angel, on the strength of a story nobody wrote down until well after the fact. The plague, the procession, and the reluctant new bishop of Rome are all solidly attested. The archangel is legend layered on afterward, the kind of origin story an institution tells about its own turning points once enough time has passed that nobody's left to contradict it. What isn't legend is who this particular reluctant bishop turned out to be, and what he did with an office he'd spent real effort trying to refuse.

His name was Gregory, born around 540 into a patrician Roman family with money and connections old enough that a career in imperial administration was more or less assumed for him from birth, and by 573 he had it: prefect of the city of Rome, responsible for its food supply, its finances, its policing, its courts — precisely the civic machinery this book's last chapter watched crumble everywhere else across the former Western Empire a century earlier, still limping along in Rome itself, with Gregory personally running it. He gave it up. When his father died, Gregory converted the family's own Roman townhouse into a monastery dedicated to Saint Andrew, endowed six more on his family's estates in Sicily, and became a monk himself — trading the most powerful civil office left in the old capital for a cell and a rule of silence. It didn't last either. In 579 the pope pulled him back into service as apocrisiarius, the papacy's resident ambassador at the imperial court in Constantinople, where Gregory spent the better part of seven years lobbying — largely without success — for military help against the Lombards, and where, by his own admission, he never troubled to learn Greek, the actual working language of the court he'd been posted to for most of a decade. He came home in 585 relieved to be back among his own monks. Five years later the plague put him on the throne he'd spent his whole adult life alternately running from and being pulled back into.

---

The Rome Gregory inherited in 590 had no functioning empire behind it in any sense that mattered day to day. Constantinople still, in theory, governed Italy through an imperial representative called the exarch, seated at Ravenna — but the exarch commanded too few troops to hold his own capital securely, let alone project protection across the peninsula, and the gap between what Rome nominally still belonged to and what actually defended it had become, by Gregory's papacy, close to total. What filled that gap, the way this book's previous chapter already watched happen piecemeal in Gaul and along the Danube, was the church's own machinery — except that in Gregory's hands, it wasn't improvised city by city anymore. It was run like a state, because by any functional definition it had become one. The Patrimony of Saint Peter, the accumulated agricultural estates the Roman church had inherited or been bequeathed across Sicily, southern Italy, Sardinia, Corsica, Dalmatia, and even North Africa, was by Gregory's time an enormous landholding operation, and he administered it with a level of granular attention that reads less like a bishop's correspondence and more like a finance ministry's. Rectors and defensors — trusted clergy assigned to individual estates — reported directly to him; his surviving letters instruct them, estate by estate, on fair treatment of tenants, interest-free loans to farmers in hardship, and detailed accounting. When Rome's own grain supply came under threat, Gregory filled the city's granaries from the harvests of Sicily and Egypt and kept standing lists of the poor so relief could go out on a regular schedule — welfare administration, run out of the same office that, four hundred years earlier, was still arguing over which gospels belonged in scripture. He died, by every surviving account, having run the operation so generously that the papal treasury was essentially empty. He had been, in the words later historians settled on, the ideal landlord — which was also, whether he fully intended it or not, the job description for governing a state nobody else was left to run.

Nowhere did the gap between Rome's nominal empire and its actual defense show more clearly than in 595, when Gregory did something no pope had done before him: he negotiated directly with the enemy. The Lombards, the Germanic power that had been carving up Italy since 568, were pressing on Rome's own outskirts with the exarch at Ravenna either unwilling or unable to respond, so Gregory opened his own line to the Lombard court and began working toward a truce on his own authority. The exarch, furious at being bypassed, reported him to Constantinople as a would-be traitor conducting his own foreign policy. Emperor Maurice's reply, a letter now lost but referenced in Gregory's own surviving response, accused the pope of being both disloyal and a fool. Gregory's answer did not deny the second charge. He wrote back, in substance, that yes, he supposed he was a fool — a fool for staying at his post at all, for enduring what he called living "amid the swords of the Lombards" while the men actually paid to defend the city couldn't be bothered to show up. It is exactly the kind of exchange this book has learned to watch for: an emperor, safely a thousand miles away, questioning the competence of the one official who had actually stayed and done the job. Gregory got his truce. Rome, for the moment, got its peace. The precedent — that when the empire's own machinery failed, the bishop of Rome would simply act, treaty-making included, with or without permission — did not go away once the immediate crisis passed.

---

Gregory did not only run Rome's government during his fourteen years in office. Later centuries came to credit him with inventing the entire body of Roman liturgical chant that still carries his name — Gregorian chant, supposedly dictated to him line by line by a dove perched on his shoulder, according to an image that started circulating a couple of centuries after his death and hardened into legend from there. Modern musicologists don't buy it; the chant repertory now called Gregorian took its mature form gradually, over the century or two after Gregory died, and no single pope sat down and composed it. What Gregory did do, more prosaically and more verifiably, was reorganize how the Roman church trained and administered its singers, formalizing the schola cantorum that later generations would credit him with far more than he actually wrote. It's a small case study in exactly how this book's own recurring caution about pious legend works: the real administrative accomplishment gets remembered, a few generations later, as a miracle instead.

He wrote the manual for how to be a bishop at all, and the manual outlasted him by roughly a millennium. The Regula Pastoralis, composed in the early years of his papacy as a working guide to pastoral leadership — how to teach, how to correct, how to balance authority against humility depending on who stood in front of you — spread across Christendom with a speed and staying power almost nothing else he wrote could match. Charlemagne's own bishops, meeting in council in 813, were formally directed to know it. Archbishops a generation later handed a copy to newly consecrated bishops at the altar itself, alongside the church's book of canon law, as though the two documents were a matched set defining the job. And in the 890s, roughly three hundred years after Gregory wrote it, an English king named Alfred — rebuilding a kingdom's shattered literacy after decades of Viking devastation had wiped out most of the clergy who could still read Latin at all — chose the Regula Pastoralis as one of the very first texts worth translating into his own people's language, so that bishops who could no longer manage the original could still be handed something like it. The oldest surviving book written in English exists, in no small part, because a monk from Rome had once tried to write down what a good bishop actually does.

It's worth pausing on the England connection specifically, because the most famous story attached to it very likely isn't quite true as usually told, and the book that told it is itself the reason the story survives at all. According to the historian Bede, writing more than a century after the events he describes, Gregory — still a monk at the time, years before his own papacy — once passed through a Roman slave market and noticed a group of fair-haired boys for sale. Told they were Angles, from a pagan island most Romans barely thought about, he is supposed to have punned that they were not Angles but angels, and to have asked further questions that produced a second pun on their home kingdom of Deira — that they would be saved de ira, from wrath — and on their king's name, Ælle, which he rendered as an occasion to sing Alleluia. It is, on its face, exactly the kind of tidy, triple-pun anecdote a later biographer builds around a man everyone already agreed mattered, rather than the kind of thing that survives from an actual eyewitness. What isn't in doubt, because it's documented in Gregory's own surviving correspondence rather than reconstructed decades later, is what he actually did once he held the office: in 596, Pope Gregory dispatched a monk named Augustine, along with roughly forty companions, on a mission to the pagan Anglo-Saxon kingdoms of Britain — funded, staffed, and directed out of the same papal administration that was simultaneously running Sicilian grain shipments and negotiating with Lombard dukes. Augustine reached Kent, converted King Æthelberht, and became the first Archbishop of Canterbury. Whatever actually happened in that slave market, the English church that grew from Canterbury traces its founding, with real documentary backing, to a decision made in Gregory's own papal office.

---

What Gregory built, across all of this, wasn't a doctrine — this book's previous seven chapters had already fought and finished that argument, at real cost, before he was even born. What he built was the operating template for what the bishop of Rome would actually do with the doctrinal unity those earlier fights had produced: administer an economy, negotiate treaties, dispatch foreign missions, write the training manual for the entire chain of command underneath him, all while formally insisting, in the very title he adopted for himself — servus servorum Dei, servant of the servants of God, chosen specifically to rebuke a rival patriarch in Constantinople who'd started calling himself "ecumenical" — that none of it was really about power at all. It was, and it wasn't, in more or less equal measure, which is as honest a description of what this book keeps finding at the center of every institution it examines as any single pope's career is likely to produce. Charlemagne's coronation, two centuries later, needed a papacy with enough independent authority and administrative memory to make the claim stick. The Crusades needed a papal office capable of directing military and financial resources across a continent on its own initiative, the way Gregory had once directed grain and treaties. Neither of those later chapters happens the same way, or perhaps at all, without the reluctant monk who spent 590 to 604 quietly proving that the office could do the job when nobody else was left to.

He would not have recognized most of what came after him as anything he'd intended. That, too, is close to the whole point.
