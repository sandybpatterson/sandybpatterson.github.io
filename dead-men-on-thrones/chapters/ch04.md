# Chapter Four

### Four Accounts, One Story

Sometime around the year 177 or 178, a Greek philosopher named Celsus sat down to write the first surviving sustained attack on Christianity by an outsider, a treatise called *The True Word*. The book itself is lost. It survives only because, seventy years later, a Christian scholar named Origen decided the charges were serious enough to answer point by point, quoting long stretches of Celsus back to him inside his own rebuttal, *Contra Celsum*. Which means we know exactly what bothered a hostile, literate reader encountering the gospels for the first time, less than a century and a half after the events they describe.

What bothered him most was the resurrection. Celsus read Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John side by side and pointed out, with visible relish, that they did not tell the same story. Different numbers of women at the tomb. Different names. Different angels, or a young man, or nobody supernatural at all in the earliest version. He wrote off the witnesses as "a hysterical woman" and possibly one other person, deluded or lying. Origen's defense, notably, was not to claim the four accounts actually matched. It was to argue that the differences were exactly what you'd expect from independent, honest testimony, and that a suspiciously identical set of four accounts would be the real red flag.

That argument — that the gospels' disagreements are a feature, not a bug, evidence of authenticity rather than a problem to be explained away — has been made, in one form or another, for roughly eighteen hundred years, which tells you two things at once. First, that the contradictions are real enough that serious people have needed an argument for why they don't matter since the second century. Second, that plenty of serious people have found that argument persuasive. This chapter isn't interested in resolving that debate. It's interested in laying out, plainly and without apology, what the four gospels actually say, so the reader can decide for themselves how much weight Origen's answer can carry.

---

Start where the story starts, and it already doesn't agree with itself.

Matthew has wise men from the East following a star to Bethlehem, King Herod ordering the slaughter of every boy under two in the region to eliminate a rival, and the holy family fleeing to Egypt to escape it — a family that, in Matthew's telling, appears to live in Bethlehem already and relocates to Nazareth only afterward, as a kind of consolation prize, because they're too afraid to go home. Luke has none of this. No star, no Magi, no massacre, no flight to Egypt. Instead Luke has shepherds, a heavenly host, and a family that lives in Nazareth from the start, traveling to Bethlehem only because of an empire-wide census that requires everyone to register in their ancestral hometown, then returning to Nazareth directly afterward, an ordinary handful of weeks later. Two birth stories, sharing only the names of the parents and the town of Bethlehem, structured so differently that reading them as a single combined narrative requires inventing details neither gospel provides.

The census creates its own separate problem, and this one isn't a matter of narrative style — it's a matter of dates that don't overlap. Matthew places the birth during the reign of Herod the Great, who died in 4 BC. Luke ties the birth to a census conducted under the Roman governor Quirinius — the only census of the region Luke could plausibly mean, and the one independently attested by the historian Josephus — which took place in 6 AD, a full decade after Herod was already dead. Centuries of careful, resourceful scholarship have tried to close that gap — an earlier, otherwise unrecorded census; a mistranslation; a deliberately anachronistic literary device aimed at readers who'd catch the joke. None of the proposed fixes has become anything close to consensus, which is itself worth noticing: this isn't a minor stylistic mismatch, it's two different starting dates for the same event, a decade apart.

Then there are the genealogies, which ought to be the most boring part of either gospel and instead turn out to be one of the strangest. Both Matthew and Luke trace Jesus's lineage back through Joseph to King David — and list almost entirely different names doing it. Matthew runs the line through Solomon; Luke runs it through a different son of David entirely, named Nathan. The two lists barely overlap between David and Joseph, disagree on the total number of generations, and both are presented, without qualification, as Joseph's actual paternal ancestry. Later readers have proposed that one list is secretly Mary's genealogy in disguise, which is nowhere stated in either text and would still be theologically beside the point in a culture that traced legal descent through the father. Taken as written, on the page, in both gospels: two different family trees, for the same man, through the same father, that do not match.

---

Some of what doesn't match isn't really a contradiction so much as a passage most churches have quietly agreed never to preach on. At the moment of the crucifixion, Matthew reports that the earth shook, rocks split, and "the tombs also were opened, and many bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep were raised. And coming out of the tombs after his resurrection they went into the holy city and appeared to many." That is the entire account. Dead saints, walking around Jerusalem, seen by an unspecified number of people, mentioned nowhere else — not in Mark, not in Luke, not in John, not in any Roman or Jewish record of the period, not in a single other early Christian writing that might be expected to mention dozens of resurrected holy people touring the capital during Passover week. It is, on any plain reading, one of the most extraordinary claims in the entire New Testament, and it shows up in exactly one gospel, in two verses, and then is never referenced again by anyone, including the other three evangelists standing right next to the event.

The temple has its own timing problem. In Matthew, Mark, and Luke, Jesus drives the money-changers out of the temple in the final week of his life, an act of open confrontation that reads as the immediate spark for his arrest days later. In John, the same episode — overturned tables, a whip of cords, the same charge about turning a house of prayer into a den of thieves — happens in chapter two, at the very beginning of Jesus's public ministry, years before the events that lead to his death. Harmonizers have proposed he did it twice. The gospels themselves give no hint of that; each account presents its version as the temple incident, singular, and John in particular treats it as an early, defining confrontation rather than one of two.

And then there is Lazarus, which is less a discrepancy than a silence loud enough to be its own kind of evidence. In John's gospel, Jesus raises a man four days dead, in front of a crowd of mourners, and it is this miracle — not the temple, not any teaching, not any other act in John's account — that the religious authorities cite as the final reason they need him dead: "if we let him go on like this, everyone will believe in him." It is presented as the hinge of the entire story, the single event that turns opposition into a death sentence. Matthew, Mark, and Luke never mention it. Not the miracle, not the man, not the funeral, not the crowd, not the plot it supposedly triggered. Three separate authors, writing accounts of the same final weeks, describing in detail the argument over why Jesus had to die, and not one of them appears to have heard of the single event John says caused it.

---

The ending is where the disagreements matter most, because the ending is the whole claim.

The earliest and best manuscripts of Mark — Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus among them — stop at 16:8. The women arrive at the tomb, find it empty, are told by a young man in white that Jesus has risen and gone ahead to Galilee, and flee. "And they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid." No resurrected Jesus appears on the page. No commissioning of the disciples. No ascension. The gospel simply ends on frightened silence. A longer ending, the familiar one with appearances and a great commission and snake-handling, does exist and did circulate reasonably early — Irenaeus seems to know some version of it by the 170s — but the church's own leading scholars in the fourth century, Eusebius and Jerome, both reported that nearly every Greek manuscript they knew of stopped at verse eight, and most textual critics today agree the original gospel ended there too, with someone else adding an ending later that felt too abrupt to leave alone.

Where Mark stops short, the other three keep going, and immediately start disagreeing with each other about basic facts. Who arrives at the tomb, and how many of them: Mark says three women; Matthew says two; Luke says an unspecified larger group including at least three named women; John narrows it to Mary Magdalene alone, at least at first. Who or what they encounter: a young man, in Mark; an angel that descends during an earthquake, in Matthew; two men in dazzling clothes, in Luke; nothing at first, then two angels, then Jesus himself, in John. Where the disciples are told to go to see the risen Jesus: a mountain in Galilee, in Matthew; nothing beyond Jerusalem and its immediate outskirts, in Luke, who has Jesus ascend into heaven that same evening; both Jerusalem and, in an additional appendix chapter, a fishing trip in Galilee, in John. Luke's timeline — resurrection and ascension compressed into a single day — sits so awkwardly next to the forty-day span the same author gives in the Book of Acts that scholars still debate whether Luke changed his mind, revised his source, or simply wasn't being that literal about the clock.

Even Jesus's last words refuse to match. Mark and Matthew, the two earliest accounts, give him a single sentence from the cross, and it's a cry of apparent despair: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" Luke removes that line entirely and gives him three different last words instead, calm and resolved: forgiveness for his executioners, a promise of paradise to a criminal dying beside him, and a peaceful commendation of his spirit into his Father's hands. John removes it too, and gives him yet another three: entrusting his mother to a disciple, announcing his thirst, and a final declaration — "it is finished" — that reads less like a death and more like the closing of a case Jesus had been arguing the entire book. No single gospel contains more than three of what later tradition would combine into "the seven last words." And the trajectory isn't random: the anguished cry appears only in the two earliest, most human accounts, and quietly disappears as the gospels get later and the portrait of Jesus gets calmer, more in control, more visibly divine.

Even the walk to the execution ground can't agree on who did the walking. Mark, Matthew, and Luke all report that the Romans pulled a passerby named Simon of Cyrene out of the crowd and forced him to carry the cross. John, describing the same walk, states plainly that Jesus "went out, bearing his own cross" — no Simon, no compulsion, no assistance, a small detail that happens to fit John's larger portrait of a Jesus who is never a passive victim of anything, right up to his own execution.

---

That last pattern is the one worth carrying out of this chapter, because it's bigger than any single contradiction on this list. Move from Mark to Matthew and Luke to John, in roughly the order most scholars think they were written, and the same man gets steadily less human and steadily more overtly divine. Mark's Jesus gets angry, sighs, is unable to work many miracles in his hometown because of the people's unbelief, and dies with what reads, on the page, like a genuine cry of abandonment. John's Jesus preexisted creation, speaks almost exclusively in long theological monologues about his own divine identity, is never once confused or afraid, arranges his mother's future from the cross like a man settling his affairs, and announces his own death as a completed project rather than suffering it as a defeat. That is not four snapshots of one static figure. It's a portrait that visibly develops, decade by decade, community by community, in the direction of higher and higher claims about who this man actually was.

None of this requires concluding that the events didn't happen, and it doesn't require concluding, either, that the four writers were lying or careless. It requires only what Origen's own defense of the gospels, back in the third century, already conceded without quite meaning to: these are four separate testimonies, from four separate communities, shaped by four separate sets of theological priorities, written down decades after the fact by people who were doing something more complicated than transcription. The question this book keeps returning to isn't whether that's disqualifying. It's what happens once you actually look at where that trajectory ends — at the gospel written last, the one that pushes furthest past a human teacher and into something closer to God walking around in a body, using words no other gospel writer had been willing to put in his mouth.
