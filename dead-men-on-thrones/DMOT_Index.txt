DEAD MEN ON THRONES
INDEX — Key terms, people, and documents, in the order they first appear.
Sandy B. Patterson
================================================================================

This is the working master for the index. The published page
(book-index.html) is generated from it — edit here first, then update the
page, so the two never drift apart.

An entry earns its place if a reader might reasonably stop mid-chapter and
ask "wait, who is that again?" Names that appear once in passing and carry
no weight in the argument stay out. Glosses are one to three sentences, in
the book's voice, and say what the person or document does in the argument —
not just what an encyclopedia would say about them.

Covers Chapters 1–12. Extend as chapters go live.


================================================================================
CHAPTER ONE — BEFORE THE BOOK
================================================================================

Ignatius of Antioch
    A bishop marched out of Antioch in chains around 107 and sentenced to die
    in a Roman arena. He spent the walk writing seven letters, and those
    letters accidentally document a Christianity that was not yet one
    institution. He could not point anyone to an agreed set of Christian
    writings, because none existed.

the archives
    What Ignatius's opponents in Philadelphia demanded — written proof from
    the Jewish scriptures before they would accept a claim. His answer is the
    hinge of the chapter: his archive is Jesus Christ. Not a book. A person,
    and the memory of what that person did.

Polycarp
    Bishop of Smyrna, taught by people who had known the apostles, and the
    closest living link the mid-second century had to Christian origins.
    Ignatius writes to him as an old man worrying about a younger one.
    Returns in Chapter 2 to curse Marcion to his face.

Justin Martyr
    A Christian philosopher writing in Rome in the mid-150s. His offhand
    description of an ordinary Sunday — "the memoirs of the apostles" read
    aloud alongside the prophets — is the earliest evidence of certain texts
    becoming scripture in practice, decades before anyone listed which ones.

Muratorian Fragment
    A battered Latin manuscript, conventionally dated around 180, usually
    called the first attempt at a canon list. It names most but not all of
    what became the New Testament, and includes a couple of books that
    didn't survive the cut. Its date is disputed — some scholars place it two
    centuries later — and either way it shows an argument in progress, not a
    settled canon.

Walter Bauer
    The German scholar behind the strong claim that in many regions what
    later got labeled heresy came first, and orthodoxy arrived afterward and
    rewrote the record of who was original. Most historians think he
    overstated it. Almost all of them accept the floor underneath it.

Tatian and the Diatessaron
    Around the 170s, Tatian solved the "which gospel" problem in Syria by
    weaving all four into one continuous narrative. For generations, Syriac-
    speaking Christians read that composite — not the four separate books —
    in church.

Gospel of Thomas
    A list of sayings attributed to Jesus with almost no narrative at all,
    circulating in Egypt and Syria with real readers who took it seriously.
    Recovered complete at Nag Hammadi in 1945. Returns in Chapter 3 as
    evidence that the sayings-gospel was a real genre.

Phoebe, Prisca, and Junia
    Three women Paul names in his own letters — a deacon, a teacher, and one
    "outstanding among the apostles." Later centuries spent considerable
    effort explaining them away. They are the chapter's evidence that the
    early plurality was a kind of freedom, not only a problem.

the agape feast
    The shared meal where a stranger arriving in Corinth or Philippi was fed
    by people who had decided, with no institution instructing them, that the
    newcomer belonged.

canon
    A closed, fixed, authoritative list. The concept did not exist for
    Christian use during the period this chapter covers. That absence is the
    chapter's whole subject.

CHAPTER TWO — THE MAN WHO FORCED THE QUESTION
================================================================================

Marcion of Sinope
    A wealthy shipowner from the Black Sea coast who arrived in Rome around
    139 or 140, handed the church two hundred thousand sesterces, and was
    excommunicated in 144 for teaching that the God of the Hebrew scriptures
    and the God of Jesus were two different beings. He is the organizing
    figure of this entire book.

the Demiurge
    Marcion's name for the god who made the material world — real, powerful,
    responsible for its law and its cruelty, and emphatically not the highest
    god. Above him sat a second, purely good God, a stranger to creation,
    who sent Jesus to liberate humanity from the first one's law.

Gospel and Apostle
    The two halves of Marcion's collection: one edited gospel resembling
    Luke, and ten edited letters of Paul. The first closed Christian
    scripture anyone has record of, roughly a century before the wider church
    produced its own.

the Antitheses
    Marcion's treatise laying the contradictions between the two gods and the
    two testaments side by side in columns, so a reader couldn't miss the
    argument. Lost, like everything else he wrote.

Tertullian
    Writing against Marcion around 207, half a century after his death, and
    the fullest surviving source on what Marcion actually taught. He assumed
    Marcion took a knife to an existing Luke and set out to prove it verse by
    verse. His hostility is why the material survives at all, and why it has
    to be read carefully.

Epiphanius
    A fourth-century bishop who compiled an enormous catalogue of heresies,
    and the only source for the story that Marcion was expelled from his
    father's church for seducing a virgin — more than two hundred years after
    the fact, with no earlier writer mentioning it. Returns in Chapter 7
    claiming Origen wrote six thousand books.

"the firstborn of Satan"
    What Polycarp said when Marcion asked whether he recognized him. Not an
    argument — a curse, from the church's most direct living link to the
    apostolic generation. The behavior of an institution that knew it was
    losing ground.

Adolf von Harnack
    The German scholar whose early-twentieth-century study credited Marcion
    with inventing the idea of a closed New Testament and forcing the wider
    church to answer in kind. The strong version of this book's own thesis.

Judith Lieu
    Author of the most important modern study of Marcion (2015), and the
    direct counterweight to Harnack. Her argument: nearly everything known
    about Marcion comes from hostile writers with every incentive to make him
    look uniquely dangerous, and canon formation was slower and messier than
    one shipowner forcing the church's hand.

Matthias Klinghardt and Markus Vinzent
    Two scholars who ask whether the knife went the other direction —
    whether Marcion's gospel was an earlier text the canonical gospels drew
    on, rather than an edited Luke. A minority position, and a live one.
    Klinghardt returns in Chapter 3.

Eznik of Kolb
    An Armenian writer still arguing against Marcionite communities in the
    fifth century, roughly three hundred years after the excommunication.
    Evidence that the movement Rome expelled outlasted the western Roman
    Empire.

CHAPTER THREE — THE LOST DOCUMENT
================================================================================

Papias of Hierapolis
    A bishop in the first decades of the second century who did something
    nobody else thought to do: went looking for where the stories about Jesus
    had actually come from. His five volumes are lost. What survives is a
    handful of quotations, including the Elder's remark about Matthew
    compiling sayings "in the Hebrew dialect."

the Synoptic Problem
    The puzzle of why Matthew, Mark, and Luke overlap so closely — sometimes
    word for word in Greek — and why they diverge exactly where they do.
    "Synoptic" is Greek for "seen together," which is literally what solving
    it requires.

Markan priority
    The widely accepted conclusion that Mark was written first and that both
    Matthew and Luke had a copy in front of them. It resolves most of the
    puzzle. It does not resolve all of it.

Q (Quelle)
    German for "source." The hypothetical document behind the roughly two
    hundred verses Matthew and Luke share that appear nowhere in Mark. It has
    never been found, no ancient writer ever mentions it, and it remains the
    dominant explanation anyway — an inference, not a discovery.

sayings gospel
    A list of things Jesus reportedly said with almost no story attached. Q,
    reconstructed, would have been one. So is the Gospel of Thomas. Two
    communities preserving teaching in the same bare form, before anyone
    decided a gospel had to be a narrative ending at an empty tomb.

the Farrer hypothesis
    The serious minority solution that needs no lost document: Mark wrote
    first, Matthew used Mark, and Luke used both. On this view the shared
    source isn't lost. It's Matthew.

CHAPTER FOUR — FOUR ACCOUNTS, ONE STORY
================================================================================

Celsus
    A Greek philosopher who wrote the first surviving sustained outside
    attack on Christianity around 177. His treatise is lost; it survives only
    because Origen quoted it at length in order to answer it. What bothered
    him most was that the four resurrection accounts don't match.

Contra Celsum
    Origen's point-by-point rebuttal, written roughly seventy years later.
    His defense was not that the gospels agree. It was that four identical
    accounts would be the real red flag — an argument still in use eighteen
    hundred years later.

the census problem
    Matthew places the birth under Herod the Great, who died in 4 BC. Luke
    ties it to the census under Quirinius, independently dated by Josephus to
    6 AD. Not a stylistic mismatch — two starting dates for the same event, a
    decade apart, with no proposed fix reaching consensus.

the two genealogies
    Matthew and Luke both trace Jesus to David through Joseph and list almost
    entirely different names doing it, through different sons of David, with
    different generation counts. Presented in both gospels, without
    qualification, as Joseph's actual paternal ancestry.

the raised saints of Matthew 27
    Dead holy people walking out of opened tombs into Jerusalem and appearing
    to many. Two verses, one gospel, never referenced again by anyone —
    including the three evangelists describing the same afternoon.

the Lazarus silence
    In John, raising a man four days dead is the event that convinces the
    authorities Jesus has to die. Matthew, Mark, and Luke never mention it.
    Not the miracle, not the man, not the plot it supposedly triggered.

Mark 16:8
    Where the earliest and best manuscripts of Mark stop. The women find the
    tomb empty, are told he has risen, and flee. "And they said nothing to
    anyone, for they were afraid." No resurrected Jesus appears on the page.
    Someone added an ending later.

the seven last words
    A composite. No single gospel gives Jesus more than three sentences from
    the cross, and they don't overlap. The cry of abandonment appears only in
    the two earliest accounts and quietly disappears as the gospels get later
    and the portrait gets calmer.

Simon of Cyrene
    Pulled from the crowd and forced to carry the cross in Matthew, Mark, and
    Luke. Absent from John, where Jesus goes out "bearing his own cross" —
    a small detail fitting John's larger portrait of a Jesus who is never a
    passive victim of anything.

CHAPTER FIVE — THE COSMIC GOSPEL
================================================================================

"Before Abraham was, I am"
    The sentence that ends the argument, and the reason the crowd picks up
    stones. Not "I existed before Abraham" — the bare present-tense phrase
    God used to name himself to Moses. Nobody in that temple needed it
    explained.

the Logos
    The Word: a cosmic principle present with God before creation, through
    whom all things were made, who at one particular moment became flesh.
    John's prologue opens where Genesis opens and puts a second figure inside
    the sentence.

Philo of Alexandria
    A Jewish philosopher writing in Greek in roughly the same decades as
    Jesus's ministry, who had already fused the Stoic idea of a world-ordering
    logos with Hebrew scripture's creative "word of the Lord." Whether John
    read him, absorbed him secondhand, or arrived somewhere similar
    independently is unsettled.

ego eimi
    "I am." The formula John repeats across the whole gospel — the bread of
    life, the light of the world, the good shepherd, the resurrection and the
    life, the true vine — training the reader to hear it, until it finally
    arrives in the temple with no metaphor attached at all.

the farewell discourse
    Four chapters of monologue after the last supper, unlike anything in the
    other three gospels, in which Jesus prepares his followers for his own
    absence.

the vine and the branches
    John 15. Real intimacy — "I have called you friends" — wired directly to
    continuous monitored productivity, framed by the threat of being cut off
    and burned if the fruit doesn't come. The book returns to this passage in
    Chapter 56 on clinical rather than literary terms.

Thomas
    The one disciple who says out loud that he isn't convinced, and holds out
    for the same evidence everyone else in the room was already given. He
    gets it, and then gives the highest confession of Jesus's identity
    anywhere in the gospel. The book's conclusion returns to him.

aposynagogos
    "Put out of the synagogue." The term behind John's three references to
    believers being expelled, and the evidence that this community understood
    itself as painfully and recently separated from the Jewish world it grew
    out of.

J. Louis Martyn and Adele Reinhartz
    Martyn tied the expulsion language to a specific late-first-century
    synagogue curse and read John's community as recent refugees from a
    formal split. Reinhartz leads the pushback, reading it as the gospel's own
    rhetoric defining insider and outsider. What survives the argument is the
    rupture itself.


================================================================================
CHAPTER SIX — THE MAN WHO BUILT THE THEOLOGY
================================================================================

the Damascus road
    Told three times in Acts, differently each time — who heard the voice,
    who saw the light, who fell. One author, one book, no attempt to
    reconcile them. Whatever happened, the writer wasn't working from a
    transcript.

Acts versus the letters
    Most of what people think they know about Paul comes from Acts, written
    by someone else decades later, shaping him into the hero of its own
    narrative. What Paul said about himself survives separately, in his own
    voice. The distinction runs through the whole chapter.

the seven undisputed letters
    Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and
    Philemon. The letters nearly all scholars accept as Paul's own. See also
    Appendix C.

the pastorals
    1 and 2 Timothy and Titus. Most critical scholars think they were written
    after Paul's death by followers using his authority to settle questions
    he never lived to face. Writing in a revered teacher's name wasn't fraud
    in the ancient world — but it means two different Pauls sit inside the
    same New Testament.

E. P. Sanders
    The scholar whose 1977 study broke a five-hundred-year reading. Working
    through surviving Jewish literature from Paul's own period, he found that
    Second Temple Judaism was not a religion of earning salvation by a ledger
    of deeds at all.

covenantal nomism
    Sanders's term: a Jew was already inside the covenant by grace, born into
    it, and keeping the law was the response to belonging rather than the
    price of admission. If that's right, "works of the law" wasn't a swipe at
    moral effort — it meant the boundary markers that decided who counted as
    inside.

the New Perspective on Paul
    The movement Sanders launched, which reframed Paul's argument from
    individual guilt to Gentile inclusion. Not universally accepted. But its
    critics now have to argue with Sanders rather than around him.

"an epistle of straw"
    What Luther called the Epistle of James in his 1522 preface, because
    James says a person is justified by works "and not by faith alone" — the
    only place in the Bible that phrase appears, and it is there to be
    rejected. Luther removed the line from later editions. He left James at
    the back of his German New Testament anyway.

Galatians 3:28
    "Neither Jew nor Greek... neither slave nor free... no male and female."
    One of the most radically egalitarian sentences in the ancient world, and
    quite possibly a baptismal formula already in use before Paul quoted it.

1 Corinthians 14:34–35
    "The women should keep silent in the churches." The passage wanders to a
    different location in part of the manuscript tradition — the signature of
    a later note absorbed into the text — and sits three chapters after Paul
    gives instructions for how women should pray and prophesy aloud in the
    assembly.

the closing clock
    Paul expected the world to end within his own lifetime, and included
    himself among those who would still be alive to see it. His instructions
    were triage for a movement he thought had no next generation. Later
    centuries treated them as binding forever.

CHAPTER SEVEN — THE BRILLIANT HERETIC
================================================================================

Origen of Alexandria
    Seventeen when his father was beheaded and the family's property seized,
    running the city's catechetical school not long after, and for the next
    forty years the most productive mind the early church produced. Tortured
    at nearly seventy, condemned three centuries after his death, and
    indispensable to the tradition that condemned him.

Leonides
    Origen's father, arrested in Alexandria in 202 and executed. Origen wanted
    to die with him. His mother hid his clothes, and a teenager with nothing
    to wear stayed home.

the Hexapla
    Origen's monumental parallel Old Testament, laying the Hebrew text and
    four Greek translations side by side in columns so a reader could see
    exactly where the versions diverged. No complete copy survived antiquity.

On First Principles
    The first systematic Christian theology — God, the soul, free will, and
    the end of all things, worked through with a rigor nobody in the movement
    had attempted before.

the three levels
    Origen's method: reading scripture literally, morally, and spiritually at
    once. It is the tool that let Christian intellectuals keep the entire
    Hebrew Bible — everything Marcion wanted cut — without defending every
    verse as plain fact. Augustine used it. The medieval church built systems
    on it.

apokatastasis
    The eventual restoration of all rational beings to God. Everyone.
    Possibly including the devil. A God whose punishments corrected rather
    than excluded permanently. The most hopeful claim anyone in this book
    makes, and the one the institution spent three centuries deciding could
    not be allowed to stand.

the castration story
    Reported by Eusebius, who admired him, and doubted by scholars since the
    nineteenth century — because the man's entire life's work was a machine
    for turning literal readings into symbolic ones. A different kind of
    source problem from a hostile witness inventing something: an admiring
    source passing on something he heard rather than saw.

the Decian persecution
    The empire-wide campaign launched in 250 that put Origen, by then nearly
    seventy, under torture designed to break a prisoner without killing him.
    He didn't recant. He didn't recover either.


================================================================================
CHAPTER EIGHT — CLOSING THE BOOK
================================================================================

Athanasius
    Bishop of Alexandria, driven out of his own see five separate times by
    five different emperors, who in 367 buried inside a routine letter about
    the date of Easter the earliest surviving list of exactly the twenty-seven
    books of the New Testament, in the order still printed today.

the Thirty-Ninth Festal Letter
    That letter. Not the moment the canon was decided and not the moment the
    argument started — the first time anyone wrote the final answer down in
    one place and made a point of saying so.

the Shepherd of Hermas
    A long, strange, visionary work about repentance that had nothing to do
    with any apostle, claimed no connection to one, and was read as scripture
    in real churches for generations. Copied into Codex Sinaiticus right
    after Revelation with no visual signal that a reader had crossed a
    boundary.

the Didache
    "The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles" — a practical manual on baptism,
    fasting, the Eucharist, and how to spot a traveling apostle angling for a
    free meal. Not condemned, just no longer copied, and lost for a
    millennium until a single copy turned up in a Constantinople monastery
    library in 1873. A text read into obsolescence.

Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Alexandrinus
    Two of the great luxury Bibles of the ancient world, and evidence of what
    their scribes thought belonged: Sinaiticus carries the Shepherd and
    Barnabas, Alexandrinus carries 1 and 2 Clement, in both cases bound in
    without apology.

Dionysius of Alexandria
    A third-century bishop who picked Revelation apart on pure grounds of
    Greek style and argued its author could not possibly be the author of
    John's gospel. Modern scholars still find the argument largely
    persuasive. The book kept its traditional attribution anyway.

Hebrews
    Brilliant, clearly important, unmistakably early, and anonymous — it never
    names its author. Asked who wrote it, Origen's surviving answer is the
    closest thing to an admission of defeat anyone in this book puts in
    writing: only God knows.

2 Peter
    The book that squeaked in by the narrowest margin — last among all
    twenty-seven in early patristic citation, sitting in Eusebius's "disputed"
    pile, reading nothing like 1 Peter. Jerome noticed the problem and
    accepted it anyway, and the doubts went quiet. Nobody found new evidence.
    The most credentialed person in the room decided the argument was over.

Hippo (393) and Carthage (397)
    Regional North African synods, not meetings of the whole church, which
    ratified what individual bishops and ordinary congregations had already
    mostly agreed on. Real authority in the West, none in the East. Popular
    retellings routinely inflate them into the council that decided the Bible.

what actually decided it
    Not a single test applied consistently by a tribunal. A rough convergence
    of imperfect ones — a plausible claim to apostolic origin, broad use
    across many regions rather than passionate use in one, and consistency
    with a theological consensus that had hardened out of two centuries of
    argument.


================================================================================
CHAPTER NINE — THE EMPEROR'S CHURCH
================================================================================

the Milvian Bridge
    28 October 312. Maxentius put the river at his back, rigged a pontoon
    crossing as a fallback, and drowned in his own armor when it gave way
    under his own retreating army. Constantine rode into Rome the next day
    carrying his brother-in-law's head on a spear.

Lactantius
    A Christian writer with connections at Constantine's court, writing
    within a decade of the battle, and the earliest and most modest account
    of the vision: a dream, the night before, instructing Constantine to mark
    his soldiers' shields.

the chi-rho
    The monogram formed by overlaying the first two Greek letters of
    "Christ." What the dream told him to put on the shields.

Eusebius of Caesarea
    The church historian, and the reason the vision story can be watched
    growing. Writing while Constantine lived, he mentions divine assistance
    and no vision. Writing after Constantine died, he has the entire army
    seeing a cross of light above the sun in daylight, with words beside it.
    He says he had it from the emperor himself, under oath.

"the Edict of Milan"
    A document that does not exist. No edict by that name has ever been
    found and none was issued at Milan. What survives is a letter Licinius
    sent from Nicomedia two months later — preserved because Lactantius
    copied it out — restoring confiscated property and guaranteeing everyone
    the right to follow whatever religion they chose.

Galerius and the Edict of Serdica
    The persecutor who ended the persecution first, in 311, dying and trying
    to be less unpopular than he had lived. Constantine gets the credit
    history remembers.

pontifex maximus
    Chief priest of Rome's traditional state cults — a title Constantine held
    for his entire reign, never renounced, never quietly dropped, while
    simultaneously funding churches. He left no evidence he saw a
    contradiction worth resolving.

the Donatists
    A North African faction who refused to accept a bishop because one of the
    men who ordained him had handed scriptures over to be burned during the
    persecution. The question underneath — whether a sacrament performed by
    an unworthy man is still a real sacrament — resurfaces under different
    names for the next thousand years.

Arles, 314
    The council Constantine convened, with bishops from as far as Gaul and
    Britain, to settle the Donatist dispute. He gained nothing from it and
    pursued it anyway — and established the exact template he would use a
    decade later at Nicaea.

Crispus and Fausta
    Constantine's eldest son and his wife, dead within weeks of each other in
    326, on his orders, with no official explanation ever issued and none
    surviving. Every account of motive is later speculation, and the most
    lurid one comes from a hostile pagan historian writing more than a
    century afterward.

Constantinople
    Dedicated in 330 on the site of Byzantium, chosen for hard strategic
    reasons rather than religious ones. He called it New Rome. Everyone else
    was already calling it Constantinople within his lifetime. It outlasted
    the western empire by close to a thousand years.

Eusebius of Nicomedia
    The bishop who baptized Constantine on his deathbed in 337 — not the
    historian, a different Eusebius, and specifically a prominent defender of
    the theological position the church had gathered at Nicaea twelve years
    earlier to condemn.


================================================================================
CHAPTER TEN — ONE LETTER'S DIFFERENCE
================================================================================

Paphnutius of Thebes
    An Egyptian bishop at Nicaea whose right eye had been gouged out and
    whose hamstrings had been cut during the Great Persecution. Constantine
    reportedly summoned him to the palace to kiss the ruined socket — a
    detail sourced to a single writer a century later and worth holding a
    little loosely, unlike the underlying scene it illustrates.

Arius
    A presbyter in Alexandria who taught, with real logical elegance, that
    the Son was generated by the Father and therefore could not be
    co-eternal with him — a creature, however exalted, rather than an
    identical share of the same uncreated being. Recalled from exile by
    Constantine in 335, dead before his restoration to communion could take
    place in 336.

the Thalia
    Arius's theology set to verse, with separate songs reportedly written for
    sailors, millers, and travelers, pitched to tunes they already knew. An
    early and effective use of popular music as a doctrinal delivery
    mechanism, and part of why the church's leadership found him so hard to
    simply out-argue.

homoousios and homoiousios
    "Same substance" and "similar substance" — one Greek letter, iota, the
    entire distance between a Son who is fully God and a Son who is merely
    the highest and best approximation of one. The distinction the whole
    chapter turns on.

the Nicene Creed
    The compressed, memorizable formula the council produced, built almost
    entirely out of clauses aimed at Arius's specific claims. A later chapter
    examines an earlier creed doing the same defensive work against a
    different opponent.

Athanasius
    Returns from Chapter 8. The young deacon who argued that a created Son
    could not accomplish what Christianity claimed the crucifixion
    accomplished. Deposed and exiled by Constantine himself in 335 — the
    first of the five exiles already mentioned — not by some later, less
    sympathetic emperor.

Eusebius of Nicomedia
    Returns from Chapter 9. The bishop who spent a decade working the
    imperial court to rebuild sympathy for Arius, using connections among the
    women of Constantine's household, and who baptized the dying emperor in
    337 — undoing, from the inside, the outcome of the council Constantine
    himself had convened.

Constantius II
    Constantine's son and successor in the East. Never a strict Arian
    himself, but hostile to the Nicene settlement and to Athanasius
    personally, for reasons that outlasted any specific point of theology.
    Persecuted, within a single generation, the exact Christians his father
    had gaveled into orthodoxy.


================================================================================
CHAPTER ELEVEN — MAKING IT MANDATORY
================================================================================

the Battle of Adrianople
    9 August 378. Valens led roughly thirty thousand men against the Goths
    and lost so completely his body was never recovered. Left the eastern
    empire without an emperor, without an army, and with a Gothic force
    loose in the Balkans that nobody was left to stop.

Theodosius I
    A Spanish general recalled from retirement after his own father was
    executed two years earlier under murky court politics. Made emperor of
    the East to clean up a catastrophe he had no part in causing, and went
    on to make Nicene Christianity the law of the Roman Empire.

the Edict of Thessalonica
    27 February 380. Not toleration and not mere favor — the first Roman
    law naming one specific version of Christian belief and ordering every
    subject to hold it, branding everyone else "foolish madmen." Its actual
    target was intra-Christian, other Christians who held the wrong creed,
    not paganism, whatever the popular version of this story says.

the Serapeum
    Alexandria's great temple of Serapis, desecrated and demolished by
    Bishop Theophilus's mob in 391 under the cover of Theodosius's later,
    harder anti-pagan decrees — the ones the 380 edict itself never
    contained. A soldier struck the cult statue with an axe. The sky did
    not fall.

the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed
    The expanded creed produced at the First Council of Constantinople in
    381, adding language on the Holy Spirit that Nicaea's original text had
    left almost entirely unaddressed. The version still recited in churches
    today, under a name almost nobody reciting it has ever heard.

foederati
    The status Theodosius negotiated for the Goths in 382 — settled inside
    imperial territory, self-governing, obligated to fight for Rome when
    called on. A pragmatic solution from the same man who, in religious
    matters, had just decided pragmatism had no place at all.

Ambrose of Milan
    The bishop who denied Theodosius communion for months after the
    Massacre of Thessalonica in 390, until the emperor performed public
    penance. Proof that making Christianity mandatory had handed the church
    real leverage over the throne it depended on — leverage a letter of
    protest from an earlier exile could never have carried.


================================================================================
CHAPTER TWELVE — THE CREED AS WEAPON
================================================================================

Rufinus of Aquileia
    A scholar and translator who, writing a creed commentary around 404,
    gave later generations the legend that each of the twelve apostles
    contributed one article of the creed in turn. His own broader
    scholarship is the reason we know the story isn't literally true — the
    real document traces to Rome roughly fifty years after the last apostle
    died.

the Old Roman Creed
    The baptismal formula behind what became the Apostles' Creed, taking
    shape at Rome over the second century and reaching something close to
    its classic form around 180 — in the specific decades right after
    Marcion built a rival church around his own fixed, edited canon.

the Symbolum
    Latin for "token" — specifically the kind of broken coin or tile two
    parties would each keep half of to prove their identity later. Applied
    to the creed: not a devotional poem, a password, matched clause by
    clause against a candidate standing in the baptismal water.

"maker of heaven and earth"
    The creed's opening clause, and the whole argument of Chapter 2 undone
    in five words: one God, both Father and creator, where Marcion had
    insisted on two.

"descended into hell"
    The one clause in the creed that isn't as old as the rest — absent from
    the earliest surviving text (337), first mentioned by Rufinus himself,
    and not universal until roughly 650. Still built to fight the same
    original argument once it arrived: a Christ whose death was real enough
    to reach all the way into death's own territory.


================================================================================
CHAPTER THIRTEEN — THE GHOST IN THE MACHINE
================================================================================

Ulfilas
    Consecrated a bishop around 341 and sent north of the Danube to
    evangelize the Goths, the people his own grandparents had reportedly
    been taken captive from. Built the first Gothic alphabet and used it to
    translate most of the Bible into Gothic, deliberately skipping the
    Books of Kings on the theory that the already-warlike Goths had no
    need for more scripture about kings waging war.

the Codex Argenteus
    The one surviving substantial copy of Ulfilas's Gothic Bible — a
    deluxe sixth-century edition in silver and gold ink on purple vellum,
    made generations after his death, almost certainly for Theodoric's own
    court in Ravenna. Now held at Uppsala University in Sweden.

Eusebius of Nicomedia
    Returns from Chapters 9 and 10. The same bishop who rehabilitated Arius
    at court and baptized the dying Constantine personally ordained Ulfilas
    and sent him north — handing an anti-Nicene theology directly to a
    people the empire couldn't easily correct once it changed its mind.

Homoian Christianity
    The more historically precise name for what Ulfilas actually taught the
    Goths — not Arius's own theology exactly, but a related formula holding
    the Son merely "like" the Father, without Nicaea's homoousios or
    Arius's sharper subordinationism. The version that happened to hold
    real power under Constantius II at the moment the Goths were converted.

Constantius II
    Returns from Chapter 10. Constantine's son, whose anti-Nicene sympathies
    were still shaping imperial religious policy in the 340s, at exactly
    the moment Eusebius of Nicomedia sent Ulfilas across the Danube.

Clovis
    King of the Franks, converted directly to Nicene Catholicism —
    traditionally dated to Christmas Day, 496, though the exact year is
    disputed — skipping the Arian phase nearly every other major Germanic
    power passed through. Baptized at Reims by Bishop Remigius, giving his
    dynasty a durable head start in legitimacy with the Catholic population
    it ruled.

Huneric
    Vandal king of North Africa from 477, who ran the harshest campaign of
    anti-Catholic persecution of any Arian Germanic ruler — exiling clergy,
    seizing churches, and issuing an edict in 484 that openly recycled
    Rome's own old anti-heretical laws against the Catholics it now ruled.

Victor of Vita
    A Catholic bishop who lived through Huneric's persecution and wrote it
    down in detail, preserving the only substantial surviving account —
    written, as the book's own recurring caution about hostile sources
    requires noting, by a man with every reason to make the persecution
    look as total as he could.

Theodoric the Ostrogoth
    Arian king who ruled Catholic-majority Italy from 493 to 526, for most
    of that span protecting Catholic worship rather than suppressing it —
    the clearest counterexample to Huneric's Africa. Ended his reign by
    executing the philosopher Boethius and his father-in-law Symmachus on
    suspicion of treason historians still can't fully verify.

the Third Council of Toledo
    Convened in 589, where the Visigothic king Reccared formally renounced
    Arianism on behalf of his entire kingdom — the last major Germanic
    Arian monarchy to give the position up, two hundred and eight years
    after Theodosius first made Nicene Christianity Roman law.

CHAPTER FOURTEEN — INTO THE VACUUM
================================================================================

Romulus Augustulus
    The last Western Roman emperor by popular reckoning — sixteen years
    old, put on the throne by his own father, deposed by Odoacer on 4
    September 476 without a fight, and pensioned off to a villa near
    Naples rather than killed. Most modern historians actually count
    someone else as the last legitimate holder of the title; see Julius
    Nepos below.

Odoacer
    The Germanic officer who deposed Romulus Augustulus and declined to
    become emperor himself, sending the imperial regalia to Constantinople
    instead with word that the West no longer needed one. Received the
    rank of patrician from Zeno in exchange, and kept striking coins in
    Julius Nepos's name for years afterward.

Julius Nepos
    The emperor Romulus Augustulus's own father had deposed to make room
    for his son — driven into exile in Dalmatia in 475, still recognized
    as legitimate emperor by Constantinople even after 476, and murdered
    at his own country house in 480. The technically correct answer to
    "who was the last Western Roman emperor," and the reason the tidy
    schoolbook date is tidier than the actual history.

the cursus publicus
    Rome's imperial relay-station network for messages, officials, and
    requisitioned horses, run on a fixed guaranteed schedule for half a
    millennium. Depended on tax revenue and local compulsion that didn't
    survive the collapse of central authority, and simply stopped
    existing in the West once nobody was left to enforce it.

Sidonius Apollinaris
    A Gallo-Roman aristocrat and former urban prefect of Rome who became
    bishop of Clermont in 469 and organized the city's actual physical
    defense against Euric's Visigoths from 473 to 475 — doing the literal
    job of civil government for a city imperial Rome had already written
    off, then getting exiled by the king he'd fought once Rome ceded the
    city anyway.

Severinus of Noricum
    A monk on the upper Danube frontier who spent the decades after 453
    organizing food distribution and negotiating directly with Germanic
    warlords for the release of Roman captives, with no formal office and
    no authority the old empire would have recognized — moral standing
    functioning, in practice, as government. Died in 482; his remains were
    later carried to the same villa near Naples where Romulus Augustulus
    had been quietly pensioned off.

CHAPTER FIFTEEN — THE MONK WHO MADE THE PAPACY
================================================================================

Gregory the Great
    Prefect of Rome by 573, then a monk, then reluctant pope from 590 to
    604 after the plague that killed his predecessor. Ran the church's
    vast estates like a state, negotiated directly with the Lombards over
    the emperor's own objections, wrote the training manual future bishops
    would carry for the next thousand years, and dispatched the mission
    that founded the English church — all while adopting the title
    "servant of the servants of God."

the Patrimony of Saint Peter
    The Roman church's own accumulated agricultural estates across
    Sicily, southern Italy, Sardinia, Corsica, Dalmatia, and North Africa.
    Gregory administered it with the granular attention of a finance
    ministry, filling Rome's granaries and running standing relief lists
    for the poor — welfare administration run out of the same office that,
    four centuries earlier, was still arguing over the biblical canon.

the "Fool" letter
    Emperor Maurice's lost 595 letter accusing Gregory of treason and
    folly for negotiating peace with the Lombards without imperial
    permission. Gregory's own surviving reply didn't deny the charge,
    calling himself a fool for staying at his post at all while Rome's
    actual defenders stayed safely away.

the Regula Pastoralis
    Gregory's handbook for bishops, written in the early 590s. Recommended
    to Charlemagne's own bishops by council in 813, handed to newly
    consecrated bishops at the altar a generation later, and translated
    into Old English by King Alfred in the 890s as part of rebuilding
    England's shattered clerical literacy after Viking raids.

the Anglii/angeli story
    Bede's century-later account of Gregory, still a monk, punning on
    enslaved English boys in a Roman market — "not Angles, but angels."
    A triple-pun anecdote almost certainly embellished in the retelling;
    what's independently documented is the actual outcome, Gregory's 596
    dispatch of Augustine's mission to England.

servus servorum Dei
    "Servant of the servants of God" — the title Gregory adopted
    specifically to rebuke the patriarch of Constantinople for calling
    himself "ecumenical." Still used by every pope since, a permanent
    monument to Gregory building unprecedented practical power while
    insisting, in his own chosen words, that it wasn't really about power
    at all.

CHAPTER SIXTEEN — THE PROPHET AND THE POWER
================================================================================

Heraclius
    Byzantine emperor who reversed total defeat by Persia, recovering the
    True Cross and restoring it to Jerusalem in a personal triumphal
    procession in 630 — six years before an entirely new power broke
    Byzantine authority in the same territory for good.

the Battle of Yarmouk
    Fought in 636 in Syria, the battle that broke Byzantine military power
    in the Levant so completely it never recovered there. Opened the way
    to the fall of Jerusalem, Antioch, and Alexandria within the following
    six years.

the Hijra
    Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina in 622, marking the moment
    his movement became a governing political community rather than a
    persecuted minority. Chosen as year one of the Islamic calendar over
    his birth or first revelation for exactly that reason.

the Pentarchy
    The five ancient patriarchal sees — Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria,
    Antioch, and Jerusalem — traditionally ranked as Christianity's great
    apostolic centers. Three of the five passed permanently out of
    Christian political control within a decade of Heraclius's own
    triumph in the same region.

the Covenant of Umar
    The terms Caliph Umar set for Jerusalem's surrender in 637–638,
    guaranteeing the city's churches and Christian residents protection in
    exchange for a special tax and subordinate legal status. Umar himself
    declined to pray inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, reportedly
    to avoid giving later generations of his own followers a precedent for
    claiming it.

the Battle of Tours
    Fought in 732 near Poitiers in central Gaul, where a Frankish force
    under Charles Martel turned back an Umayyad raiding expedition. Later
    inflated by European historians into the battle that saved Christian
    civilization; modern historians read the actual engagement as more
    modest than its reputation.

CHAPTER SEVENTEEN — THE CROWN AND THE KEYS
================================================================================

Charlemagne
    King of the Franks, crowned Emperor of the Romans by Pope Leo III on
    Christmas Day, 800 — a title reviving the office this book's
    fourteenth chapter last saw stripped from a sixteen-year-old and
    pensioned off to a villa near Naples. Had already been styling
    himself Rome's heir in stone, at his own palace chapel in Aachen,
    years before any pope made it official.

Pope Leo III
    Survived an assassination attempt by Roman nobles in 799, fled to
    Charlemagne for protection, cleared himself of the charges against
    him by personal oath rather than trial, and two days later crowned
    his protector emperor — cementing the precedent that the imperial
    title now passed through the pope's own hands.

prima sedes a nemine iudicatur
    "The first see is judged by no one" — the doctrine Leo's 800 synod
    leaned on to avoid trying him, first established three centuries
    earlier when Pope Symmachus faced almost the same situation under
    Theodoric the Ostrogoth, the same king this book's thirteenth chapter
    already met ruling Catholic Italy from Ravenna.

Empress Irene
    Sole ruler of the Byzantine Empire from 797, having arranged the
    blinding of her own son and co-emperor, Constantine VI, to keep power
    for herself. Her sex, rather than the blinding, was what Western
    writers cited as disqualifying her from the imperial title — the
    pretext that left Rome's throne "vacant" enough to fill with
    Charlemagne. A proposed 802 marriage between the two of them
    collapsed when she was deposed that October; she died in exile on
    Lesbos less than a year later.

renovatio Romani imperii
    "The renewal of the Roman empire" — the motto on Charlemagne's own
    official seal, and the political ambition his court had already been
    building toward, in stone and in ceremony, before Leo III's
    coronation made the claim official.

the 813 Aachen coronation
    Charlemagne's own late-reign attempt to crown his son Louis co-emperor
    without any pope involved, an explicit bid to keep the title inside
    his own family's gift rather than the papacy's. Louis was crowned a
    second time anyway, by Pope Stephen IV in 816 — a sign of how quickly
    the Roman ceremony reasserted itself as the version that actually
    counted.
