RFK Jr. Cast Doubt on Confirmed Pennsylvania Measles Deaths, Then Walked It Back After the County Coroner Spoke

RFK Jr. suggested Pennsylvania's confirmed measles deaths might be fabricated, then reversed course after the county coroner went on the record, plus COVID wastewater hits its highest level since January 2024, Florida's vaccine mandate rollback reaches public hearings, tadalafil is linked to higher glaucoma risk, and a new pill cuts sleep apnea severity nearly in half.

Sandy B. Patterson · September 8, 2026

Biggest Story

Pennsylvania's Lancaster County reported the state's first measles deaths in 35 years in late August: a six-week-old infant, born with a condition that already left the child medically fragile, and one other unvaccinated resident, both confirmed through lab testing and epidemiologic investigation by the state health department. HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. publicly cast doubt on the finding anyway, suggesting the deaths may have been fabricated by staff working for Pennsylvania's governor, and directed CDC Director Erica Schwartz to strike the Pennsylvania cases from the national measles death count. On September 4th, Lancaster County Coroner Stephen Diamantoni went public with his own findings, confirming that measles was a contributing cause of the infant's death, with the immediate cause listed as a ruptured spleen the virus had left the child vulnerable to. Kennedy reversed course within a day, acknowledging the coroner's statement in a social media post, though he continued to accuse Governor Josh Shapiro of politicizing the child's death. The underlying outbreak in Lancaster County has now sickened 577 people and hospitalized 98. A sitting HHS Secretary publicly disputing a state's own confirmed death data, then backing down only after a county coroner spoke on the record, is its own kind of public health story, regardless of where anyone stands on vaccine policy.

Biology

Men taking tadalafil, sold as Cialis, long term for an enlarged prostate may be at meaningfully higher risk of glaucoma, according to a study of nearly 74,000 men published in the British Journal of Ophthalmology. Comparing roughly 37,000 men on the drug against a matched group who weren't, researchers found tadalafil users were 22 percent more likely to develop glaucoma, 32 percent more likely to develop high eye pressure, and 33 percent more likely to be diagnosed with the most common form of glaucoma over five years of follow up. The study can't prove the drug is causing the damage rather than just correlating with it, and the researchers are not telling anyone to stop taking it. But it's a large enough signal that anyone on long term tadalafil for prostate symptoms may want to mention it to their eye doctor.

Misinformation

A separate measles claim got a harder fact check this week. Representative Byron Donalds of Florida told CBS's Face the Nation in August that illegal immigration is driving the country's measles outbreak, and PolitiFact rated the claim false on August 27th. No data ties unauthorized immigrants to this year's outbreak, and the experts PolitiFact consulted point to a different, well documented cause: falling MMR vaccination rates among U.S. born children. FactCheck.org reached the same conclusion in a separate debunk published this month. It's a claim that keeps resurfacing anyway, in part because it offers an explanation for the outbreak that doesn't require confronting declining vaccine uptake.

Public Health Data

COVID's late summer surge, which this brief has been tracking all week, just got a clearer picture. CDC wastewater data for the week ending September 7th show SARS-CoV-2 levels at high or very high in 37 states, the highest national reading since January 2024. The West is running hottest at a regional level, followed by the Midwest and South, with the Northeast still comparatively low. CDC's separate transmission modeling backs this up: as of September 2nd, COVID infections were growing or likely growing in 47 states, declining in none, and holding steady in only 3. The dominant variant nationally, KP.3.1.1, an Omicron lineage, now accounts for about a third of sequenced wastewater samples.

Politics

Florida's push to drop four school vaccine requirements, hepatitis B, chickenpox, Hib, and pneumococcal, moved from paperwork to public testimony this week, with hearings drawing hours of comment from both sides. Parents citing medical freedom and officials aligned with Surgeon General Joseph Ladapo argued for the rollback, while physicians and pediatric groups warned it risks a resurgence of diseases the state hasn't seen in years. Public comment on the formal rule stays open through September 14th. No other state has followed Florida's lead yet, though health policy researchers say several Republican led states are watching how this plays out before deciding whether to try something similar.

Good News

A once nightly pill called AD109 cut the severity of obstructive sleep apnea nearly in half in a Phase 3 trial, reducing the apnea hypopnea index by about 44 percent compared with 18 percent for placebo. More than 40 percent of patients on the drug moved into a less severe category of the disease, and 18 percent saw their sleep apnea controlled completely. The trial, called SynAIRgy, combines two existing drugs, aroxybutynin and atomoxetine, to relax the airway muscles that collapse during sleep rather than relying on a CPAP machine to force air through. It's not an approved treatment yet, but a well powered trial showing a real oral alternative to CPAP is a genuinely good result for the millions of people who have sleep apnea and can't tolerate wearing a mask all night.
