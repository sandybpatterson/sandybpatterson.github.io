COVID Wastewater in Parts of Texas Reaches Levels Last Seen During the Omicron Peak

A presumed 13th COVID wave now shows in at least 29 states, plus long-term melatonin use is linked to a sharply higher heart failure risk, the largest study yet finds no autism link in MMR-vaccinated kids, foodborne Cyclospora cases pass 17,000, expiring ACA subsidies roughly double premiums for millions, and a Troy, New York community saves its last birthing center.

Sandy B. Patterson · September 2, 2026

Biggest Story

The Pandemic Mitigation Collaborative pushed its weekly COVID-19 report out two days early on August 31st, citing what it's now calling a "presumed 13th wave" of the pandemic. The group's model shows active outbreak signals in at least 29 states and territories, stretching from Hawai'i and Alaska across the South and into New England. The clearest evidence is in Texas: CDC's own wastewater surveillance, updated August 20th, placed Texas at the highest viral-activity reading of any state in the country for the week ending August 15th, drawing on 40 monitoring sites statewide, with Mississippi close behind on a thinner two-site sample. Some individual Texas counties are now registering wastewater concentrations on par with the national peak of the BA.1 Omicron wave — the highest COVID signal the country has ever recorded — high enough that modelers are using Texas's dense monitoring network to estimate conditions in neighboring states that don't run wastewater surveillance of their own. None of this fits the seasonal pattern COVID was expected to settle into: this is a late-summer surge arriving months before the traditional winter respiratory season even starts, the latest sign that the virus still isn't behaving like flu or RSV.

Biology

New data drawing fresh attention this week ties long-term melatonin use to a substantially higher risk of heart failure. Researchers tracked electronic health records for more than 130,000 adults diagnosed with chronic insomnia, comparing those who'd used melatonin for 12 months or longer against matched non-users. Over five years of follow-up, long-term melatonin users developed heart failure at nearly twice the rate — 4.6 percent versus 2.7 percent — were almost three and a half times more likely to be hospitalized for it, and died of any cause at close to double the rate of non-users. The findings are observational, not proof that melatonin itself causes heart failure, and the underlying data hasn't yet cleared full peer review as a published manuscript — but the size of the gap, on a supplement tens of millions of Americans take nightly without a prescription or a doctor's involvement, is enough that cardiologists are taking it seriously rather than waving it off.

Misinformation

The largest study yet on the MMR vaccine and autism reached the same conclusion research has been reaching for more than two decades — it just did it at a scale no earlier study had. Published July 28th in the Pediatric Infectious Disease Journal, the study followed more than 2.5 million children and found no elevated autism risk among those who received their first MMR dose between 11.5 and 24 months, compared with children who hadn't yet been vaccinated. A commentary in Genetic Literacy Project on August 19th made the timing explicit: both the CDC's own current messaging and the executive order pushing to split the MMR vaccine into three separate shots lean on an autism link that this newest, largest study still doesn't find. This isn't an open question research keeps failing to resolve. It's the same answer, arrived at again, at a scale that makes it harder than ever to wave away.

Public Health Data

The CDC's 2026 Cyclospora count climbed again this week, adding more than 3,000 newly confirmed infections to bring the year's total to roughly 17,180 cases — compared with just 1,180 cases logged through the same point in 2025, a roughly fifteen-fold increase. Most of the outbreak traces to Taylor Farms iceberg lettuce grown in central Mexico and recalled in July; at least two people have died and 922 have been hospitalized so far this season. The FDA says every lot of the specific recalled lettuce is now off the market, but the case count is still rising as infections from earlier in the summer continue to get diagnosed and reported.

Politics

The enhanced ACA premium tax credits enacted during the pandemic have now expired, and the bill is landing on the 21.8 million Americans who buy Marketplace coverage with a subsidy. KFF's analysis of the newly released 2026 numbers puts the average annual premium payment for subsidized enrollees at roughly $1,904, up from about $888 — a 114 percent increase in what people actually pay out of pocket. The Congressional Budget Office projects up to 4 million people could drop coverage entirely once the higher bills arrive, split between people just above the income cutoffs who lose financial help altogether and lower-income enrollees who keep some subsidy but can no longer cover what's left. Congress let the enhanced credits lapse without an extension, and there's no active legislative fix moving as this fall's open enrollment period approaches.

Good News

In Troy, New York, Democrats and Republicans did something neither party manages very often in American health care right now: they won, together. When Trinity Health — the multibillion-dollar Catholic system that owns Samaritan Hospital — announced it would close the Burdett Birth Center, the last birthing center left in Rensselaer County, a genuinely bipartisan coalition formed to stop it. Democratic state Assemblyman John T. McDonald III worked alongside Republican county and state officials to secure $5 million in state funding, while Democratic Attorney General Letitia James opened a formal investigation into the closure and held a public hearing in Troy. Planned Parenthood and the local Catholic diocese, rarely aligned on anything, both backed the campaign. Nearly a year after first announcing the closure, Trinity reversed course this week: Burdett stays open, and Troy keeps delivering its own babies.
