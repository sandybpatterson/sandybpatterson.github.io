Coming up on Wetwear: Biology Corrected.

Cyclospora Outbreak Tied to Recalled Lettuce Grows to Nearly 11,000 Cases
Plus: CMS chief Dr. Oz publicly contradicts Trump's false MMR claim, a nearly 200-year-old museum specimen reveals a hidden pangolin species, early-life stress leaves a lasting "scar" on the brain, and a pancreatic cancer drug nearly doubles survival.
I am Sandy B. Patterson, and today is August 27, 2026.

=========================================================================

Here is the BIGGEST STORY from the CDC and FDA
-------------

The CDC and FDA's investigation into a multistate Cyclospora outbreak linked to recalled iceberg lettuce has grown to 10,930 confirmed illnesses across 17 states, with at least 454 hospitalizations and two deaths in Michigan — one of the largest foodborne outbreaks the country has tracked this year, and one that expanded to four new states in a CDC newsroom update this week. The source is iceberg lettuce processed by Taylor Farms de Mexico, which recalled all iceberg lettuce sourced from its central Mexico operations on July 17; people got sick both from lettuce purchased directly and from lettuce served at restaurants, including reported cases tied to eating at Taco Bell. Illness onset dates span an unusually long window, from June 14 through August 11, reflecting both the scale of distribution and the parasite's incubation period, which can run one to two weeks and cause watery diarrhea, cramping, and fatigue that can persist for weeks without treatment. Cyclospora is caused by a microscopic parasite typically spread through contaminated produce rather than person-to-person contact, which is part of why a single contaminated growing operation can seed illnesses across so many states at once. The two deaths, both in Michigan, are a reminder that even a foodborne illness widely thought of as unpleasant rather than dangerous can turn fatal, particularly in people who are older or immunocompromised. With cases still being added to the CDC's count as recently as this week, health officials are continuing to urge anyone who bought iceberg lettuce from the recalled lots — or who ate at a restaurant using Taylor Farms de Mexico lettuce during the exposure window — to watch for prolonged, watery diarrhea and seek care if symptoms don't resolve on their own.

Here is the latest in BIOLOGY
-------

Scientists have confirmed that the Himalayan pangolin, long assumed to be a subspecies of a more common relative, is in fact its own distinct species — and the proof came from DNA extracted out of a museum specimen collected in 1836, nearly 190 years ago. Researchers sequenced full genomes from 55 living pangolins and partial genomic material from 70 more, then compared them against the historic type specimen held at the Natural History Museum, confirming the Himalayan population found across Nepal and northern India as Manis aurita — physically distinguished from the Chinese pangolin by a larger body, longer tail, and smaller ears, but subtle enough that it had gone unrecognized for nearly two centuries. Pangolins are the world's most trafficked mammal, prized for scales used in traditional medicine, and the findings, published in Communications Biology, give conservationists a genetic reference point they didn't have before: a way to trace seized, trafficked pangolin scales back to the specific species and region they were poached from.

A study published August 7 in Neuron, from researchers at Princeton and Washington University in St. Louis, has identified a specific molecular mechanism by which childhood stress leaves a lasting physical mark on the brain. In mice, early-life stress increased an enzyme called SETD7 in dopamine-producing neurons in the brain's reward circuitry, which in turn added a chemical tag to the DNA-packaging material in those cells — loosening it just enough to make nearby stress-response genes easier to switch on later in life. The result was a kind of primed hypersensitivity: mice that had experienced early stress showed heightened anxiety-like behavior and stronger neural stress responses as adults, and — critically — reducing the SETD7 enzyme after the early stress had already occurred prevented that hypersensitivity from developing, suggesting the "scar" may be reversible rather than permanent.

New findings presented this summer at NUTRITION 2026, drawn from the long-running DIETFITS trial, help explain why a low-carb diet raises some people's cholesterol and not others'. Researchers built a polygenic score combining thousands of genetic variants tied to LDL cholesterol and found that people with a higher genetic predisposition toward high cholesterol saw substantially bigger LDL increases on a low-carb diet than people with a lower genetic score — an effect driven specifically by those individuals' greater sensitivity to saturated fat. The takeaway isn't that low-carb diets are broadly risky, researchers say, but that the same diet can have meaningfully different cardiovascular effects depending on a person's underlying genetics, particularly for people who lean heavily on saturated-fat-heavy foods like butter and fatty meat.

Here is the latest on MISINFORMATION
--------------

In a notable break from the president he serves, CMS Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz stated flatly on CBS's Face the Nation this week that "the MMR vaccine is not a lethal vaccine" — directly contradicting President Trump, who has repeatedly claimed the combined measles-mumps-rubella shot could be "quite lethal." It took host Margaret Brennan three separate questions to get Oz to say so plainly; he opened by emphasizing "parental autonomy" before conceding the underlying safety claim outright. The exchange lands in the middle of an executive order Trump signed this month directing HHS to explore splitting the MMR into three separate shots and narrowing the recommended childhood vaccine schedule — meaning one of the administration's own senior health officials is now on record rejecting the factual premise the policy was built on.

Separately, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has continued to claim that the MMR vaccine contains "millions of particles ... created from aborted fetal tissue," a claim CIDRAP and other fact-checkers describe as false — the vaccine's rubella component is grown using a fetal cell line established decades ago, but the finished product does not contain fetal tissue or its particles. The claim has real reach: surveys cited by CIDRAP this week found that roughly a third of U.S. adults have now encountered the false claim that the MMR vaccine is more dangerous than a measles infection itself, and some patients report believing vitamin A can prevent the disease outright — neither of which is supported by evidence.

Here is today's PUBLIC HEALTH DATA
------------------

The national measles count continues to climb: the CDC's most recent tally puts the U.S. at 2,777 confirmed cases as of August 20, spread across 36 outbreaks in 45 states, with 93 to 94 percent of patients unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status. CIDRAP's own retrospective this week put a number on just how long this has been going: the outbreak has now stretched across 20 consecutive months of active measles transmission, the longest sustained run since before the U.S. eliminated the disease in 2000 — the grim backdrop against which this brief reported Pennsylvania's first measles deaths of the year earlier this week.

Here is the latest in POLITICS
--------

Late last month, the Trump administration reversed course and unfroze $600 million in congressionally appropriated funding for Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, which delivers vaccines against roughly 20 diseases to children in more than 50 low-income countries — money HHS Secretary Kennedy had halted last year over unsubstantiated vaccine-safety concerns. The State Department said the funding was released after Gavi agreed to a set of conditions: eliminating mercury-based preservatives from its vaccine programs, cutting back its Geneva staffing, and ceasing the "promotion and procurement" of COVID-19 vaccines for routine childhood immunization — meaning one of the administration's most consequential recent vaccine-policy wins for global public health came bundled with concessions to some of the same anti-vaccine talking points this brief has spent weeks documenting.

Meanwhile, the self-imposed deadline thirty Senate Democrats set for Secretary Kennedy to respond to their questions about HHS's mass cancellation of roughly 150 Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality grants arrived and passed this week, on August 25, with no public response from HHS found as of this writing. The senators are seeking a detailed accounting — written criteria, funding plans, rankings, or any documentation — behind a decision that abruptly terminated close to 200 active research programs on patient safety and care quality across nearly 30 states.

And finally, some GOOD NEWS
---------

A pancreatic cancer drug that first made headlines at May's ASCO meeting got fresh attention this week in a Bloomberg feature on patients living longer than they expected: in the Phase 3 RASolute 302 trial, the oral drug daraxonrasib nearly doubled median overall survival for patients with previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer, extending it to 13.2 months compared with 6.7 months on standard chemotherapy — a 60 percent reduction in the risk of death, published simultaneously in the New England Journal of Medicine. Pancreatic cancer remains one of the deadliest common cancers, with survival gains historically measured in weeks rather than months, which is part of why oncologists are treating this result as a genuine turning point rather than an incremental improvement.
