The FDA Ignored Its Own Scientists to Give RFK Jr. the Peptides He Asked For

Six wellness peptides — most backed by animal studies, gym forums, and little else — are headed toward legal compounding after Kennedy pushed for access on a podcast and an advisory panel overruled its own agency's safety review. Here's the evidence that got overruled, and why the "reasonable middle" being offered still doesn't hold up.

Sandy B. Patterson · August 28, 2026

What Changed

Six months ago, a cluster of trendy wellness peptides — among them a pair marketed together for tissue repair — sat in a category the FDA reserves for substances it considers to carry "significant safety risks" — a label that, in practice, kept licensed compounding pharmacies from legally making them into prescriptions at all. That changed not because new safety data came in, but because HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said he wanted it to. On February 27, Kennedy told podcast host Joe Rogan he was "a big fan" of peptides, disclosed he takes them himself, and pledged to restore legal access to roughly 14 of them — waving away the FDA's prior restrictions as having merely "created the gray market" rather than addressed any real risk. It was, by any normal standard, an unusual way for a federal drug-safety agency to receive its marching orders: not a peer-reviewed finding, not a citizen petition backed by new clinical data, but a cabinet secretary's personal enthusiasm, aired on a podcast.

The FDA responded with a speed that had nothing to do with new science. Seven weeks later, on April 15, it removed a dozen peptides — sold for uses ranging from tissue repair, gut inflammation, and sleep to cognitive focus, skin healing, athletic performance, tanning, and anti-aging — from its "significant safety risk" category, effective April 22. Nothing about the underlying evidence had changed between February and April; what had changed was who was asking. The agency then scheduled a formal hearing of its Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee for July 23–24, to vote on seven of them — the closest thing this process has to independent scientific review, and the one part of it that, at least on paper, didn't go the administration's way.

What FDA's Own Scientists Said

Before the committee voted, FDA staff scientists — the actual reviewers, not the political appointees pushing this along — filed briefing documents recommending against adding all seven to the list. Every single one. And their objections weren't hedged or bureaucratic; they were the kind of findings that would sink a drug application in any other context. One of the two tissue-repair peptides had five human studies behind it, and even those were short, small, and tested doses staff called "exploratory in nature" — a polite way of saying the studies weren't designed to prove the drug works, only to see what happens if you try it. Its regular pairing fared worse: staff said they could not locate a single human study for it at all — everything known about how it behaves in a human body is extrapolated from animal data, which is to say nobody actually knows. And it isn't only that the evidence is thin: real people using compounded versions of these drugs have already been harmed. The agency logged adverse-event reports including a patient who developed shortness of breath and another who developed diffuse skin and gum darkening after using a compounded blend of the two tissue-repair peptides together. The longevity peptide activates telomerase, an enzyme the agency flagged for "uncertain carcinogenicity" when manipulated long-term — a cancer-risk unknown, not a hypothetical one. The cognitive-focus peptide carries anticoagulant and amphetamine-potentiation signals. Two of the seven — the metabolic-performance peptide and the harder-hit tissue-repair peptide with no human data at all — are banned outright for competitive athletes by the World Anti-Doping Agency, which says plainly where most of the actual demand for these compounds is coming from: not oncology wards or physical therapy clinics, but gyms and message boards.

What The Vote Actually Means — And Doesn't

The committee voted for six of the seven anyway. Both tissue-repair peptides and the anti-inflammatory one each passed 8-6. The metabolic-performance peptide passed 7-5. The cognitive-focus peptide passed 8-5. The longevity peptide passed 7-5. Every one of those is a coin flip dressed up as a scientific consensus — margins that, from a body reviewing the exact briefing documents that said the evidence didn't support approval, should read as a warning sign, not a mandate. Only the sleep peptide was voted down, 6-7 — close enough that a single vote flipping the other way would have made it seven for seven, over the unanimous, on-the-record objection of the agency's own scientific staff.

It's worth being precise about what that vote did and didn't do, because most of the coverage since has blurred the line in a way that flatters the outcome. The advisory committee's recommendation isn't binding. The FDA still has to run a full rulemaking process — likely months — before any of these six peptides is actually legal for a compounding pharmacy to make. As of today, nothing has changed in practice: the tissue-repair peptides are politically closer to being legal than they were in June, but they remain exactly as unapproved and unproven, right now, as they were before a single vote was cast.

What The Peptides Actually Are

Strip away the marketing language and here is what's actually being pushed toward a legal compounding pipeline. Two of the six are marketed together for accelerated tissue repair — "the Wolverine peptide" pairing, in gym-forum shorthand — built almost entirely on rodent studies and anecdote, not controlled human trials; one of the two has never been tested in a human study at all. The anti-inflammatory one is a fragment studied mostly in the context of gut disease, its intended use now stretched well past what that research actually covers. The metabolic-performance one is marketed to exactly the population — competitive athletes — for whom it's already a banned substance. The cognitive-focus one is a Soviet-era compound with essentially no American regulatory track record at all. The longevity one is sold almost entirely on anti-aging claims resting on a mechanism the FDA itself flagged as carrying uncertain cancer risk. Not one of the six has been through the large, controlled human trial the FDA would ordinarily require before letting a drug anywhere near a patient. What's being normalized here isn't a treatment with a known risk profile — it's six compounds whose risk profile is, by the reviewing agency's own admission, still unknown.

What More Reasonable People Are Saying

Strip out the two loudest and least informative reactions to this story — wellness influencers treating the vote as vindication, and reflexive alarmists treating any peptide as poison — and what's left is a real disagreement between professional organizations that actually read the evidence. It is not, on inspection, a close call.

The American Pharmacists Association read the same briefing documents the committee did and reached the same conclusion FDA staff did: not enough evidence, for any of the seven. In a July 9 comment letter, APhA said plainly that without peer-reviewed clinical data, pharmacists have no real basis to counsel patients on dosing, interactions, or side effects — which is another way of saying that legalizing this compounding means legalizing guesswork. APhA's letter also surfaced a detail that should have gotten far more attention than it did: reports of Chinese manufacturers previously known for producing fentanyl precursors pivoting into peptide sales. The "gray market" Kennedy waved off on a podcast is, according to the industry's own trade association, already intersecting with the supply chains behind the country's other drug crisis. The Partnership for Safe Medicines was no less direct — its executive director, Shabbir Imber Safdar, called the outcome "a serious setback for patient safety," and pointed out, accurately, that the committee voted to advance substances its own agency's scientists had already flagged as inadequately supported.

The counterargument, from the Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding's CEO, Scott Brunner, is that a legal pathway with mandatory reporting requirements would put these drugs under more oversight than the black market currently provides. It's coherent in theory. It falls apart on the specifics: an August 4 Health Affairs Forefront analysis found that the exact legal pathway on offer — 503A compounding — doesn't require FDA registration, doesn't require routine inspection, and specifically exempts compounders from the adverse-event reporting Brunner is citing as the whole point of going legal. The "more oversight" argument describes a regulatory regime that doesn't actually exist under the rule being proposed. That isn't a minor technicality. It's the entire premise of the pro-access case collapsing on contact with the actual text of what's being voted on.

Even the more careful access-expansion voices don't hold up much better under scrutiny. Robert Pearl, writing in Forbes, and STAT's editorial board both proposed pairing expanded access with mandatory data collection — access now, evidence later. It sounds moderate. It isn't: it's a proposal to let patients generate the safety data that's supposed to come before approval, not after it, on substances the reviewing agency has already flagged for real signals of harm. That's caution's language wrapped around the opposite of a cautious policy.

None of this is actually close, once you weigh it honestly. Every organization that reviewed the underlying evidence on its own merits — FDA's staff scientists, APhA, the Partnership for Safe Medicines — reached the same conclusion: not enough. The only voices arguing otherwise are the trade group whose members stand to profit from expanded compounding, and commentators proposing to run the safety trial on the public after the fact instead of before it. If reasonable means what's actually safe, and proven to be safe, this isn't a story with two reasonable sides. It's a story where the evidence-based position lost a political vote.

What Happens Next

The FDA has not announced a timeline for the rulemaking that would make July's recommendations binding, and Kennedy's public statements suggest the administration wants this done quickly regardless. Its own scientific staff have now said, twice, in writing — in the Category 2 review and again in the PCAC briefing documents — that the evidence doesn't support that. Whichever way the rulemaking goes, it will not have been decided by science; the science already spoke, and it said no. It will have been decided by how much a political timeline can outrun an evidence review it was never given the chance to catch up to.
