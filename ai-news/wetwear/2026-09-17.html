Pennsylvania's Fourth Measles Death Is the Worst US Toll in Decades, and the CDC Still Won't Count It

Plus a four-week diet change appears to lower older adults' biological age, TrumpRx's drug savings claims don't hold up, COVID cases are climbing in 35 states, the FDA holds a landmark hearing on psychedelic therapy, and rattlesnake blood proteins point toward a far more potent antivenom.

Sandy B. Patterson · September 17, 2026

Biggest Story

Pennsylvania has confirmed its fourth measles related death of the year, a grim milestone the state has not seen in 35 years and the country has not recorded in a single year since 1992. The victim was an 18-year-old from Mifflin County who was not vaccinated and died of acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, a rare and severe neurological complication of measles infection, according to the county coroner. Pennsylvania has now reported nearly 700 measles cases this year, with 133 people hospitalized, as the outbreak that began earlier this year continues to grow rather than subside. What makes this story harder to look away from is not just the outbreak itself but the fact that the federal government is refusing to formally acknowledge any of it. Since the first two Pennsylvania deaths were reported to the CDC in late August, the agency, now under new director Doctor Erica Schwartz, has declined to include any of the state's measles deaths in its national weekly tally, saying available information does not establish whether measles caused or contributed to the deaths. That explanation has grown harder to sustain with each subsequent death, and the CDC has historically accepted state health department death reports like Pennsylvania's without this kind of extended review. The result is a genuinely strange split screen: a state coroner's office attributing a documented death to a measles complication with a name and a diagnosis, and the nation's leading public health agency's official case count still sitting wherever it stood before the first Pennsylvania death was reported. National measles cases were already approaching 3,300 as of the CDC's own most recent count, more than 1,000 above all of last year's total. The discrepancy over Pennsylvania's dead means the true national toll from this year's outbreak is very likely being undercounted in real time, on the record, by the one agency whose job is to count it.

Biology

A new clinical study out of the University of Sydney, published in the journal Aging Cell, found that older adults who changed the balance of fat, carbohydrate, and animal protein in their diet showed measurable signs of a younger biological age after just four weeks. Researchers followed adults between 65 and 75 and found the strongest effect in those who shifted toward an omnivorous diet lower in fat and higher in carbohydrates, with reduced intake of animal based protein and dietary fat linked to improvements in cholesterol, insulin, and blood pressure, all of which feed into biomarker based estimates of biological aging. The findings add to a growing body of research suggesting that at least some hallmarks of aging can respond to relatively short, achievable dietary changes rather than requiring years of sustained lifestyle overhaul.

Study leader Caitlin Andrews cautioned that the results are preliminary and reflect a short intervention window, saying it is too soon to say definitively that specific dietary changes will extend anyone's life. Still, a four week window producing measurable biomarker shifts in a population as old as 65 to 75 is a meaningfully fast result by the standards of aging research, and the study gives researchers a clear, testable starting point for larger and longer trials.

Misinformation

President Trump and administration officials have spent much of the past month taking credit for falling prescription drug prices, pointing to the new TrumpRx purchasing portal and a series of negotiated deals with pharmaceutical manufacturers as the cause. Independent fact checkers and drug pricing experts who actually examined the numbers found a considerably messier picture. TrumpRx currently lists around 80 brand name drugs as part of its presidential deals, a small fraction of the medications on the market, and the negotiated prices do not touch what the majority of Americans who rely on private insurance actually pay at the pharmacy counter. A House Democratic analysis of 43 drugs initially listed on the platform found that for nearly half of them, TrumpRx's savings claims were misleading or outright false, in some cases because a cheaper generic version was already available and simply not disclosed alongside the brand name price. Even the platform's marquee claim of offering the world's lowest prices does not hold up consistently on inspection: for Novo Nordisk's Wegovy, the TrumpRx price beats Canada's but still runs higher than prices in seven other countries, including Germany, Britain, and Japan. Experts who study drug pricing say the broader national decline likely owes far more to ordinary market forces and existing manufacturer discount programs than to either the portal or the administration's deals, meaning a real policy story is being told as a bigger and simpler one than the evidence actually supports.

Public Health Data

CDC surveillance data released this month shows COVID-19 infections growing or likely growing in 35 states, with the sharpest increases concentrated in the South and West and California, Nevada, and Texas posting the highest levels of the virus detected in wastewater of any states in the country. National COVID activity is still officially classified as low, but the trend represents a genuine late summer wave arriving after a prolonged seasonal lull, and it is showing up broadly enough that the CDC says infections are rising in nearly three quarters of the country at once. The timing lines up with the rollout of updated vaccines: the FDA approved monovalent 2026 to 2027 COVID vaccines from Moderna, Pfizer BioNTech, and Novavax Sanofi in late August, and the new shots are reaching pharmacies, clinics, and hospitals this month just as case counts climb, giving people a real window to get an updated shot ahead of whatever the fall and winter respiratory season brings.

Politics

The FDA held a landmark public hearing on September 14th on the potential future therapeutic use of psychedelic drugs, drawing more than 18,000 registered attendees for a single afternoon hearing, a scale that reflects both the scientific and political weight the issue now carries. The hearing implements Executive Order 14401, which directs federal agencies to speed up research, review, and approval of psychedelic treatments for serious mental health conditions. It comes after the FDA already issued national priority vouchers earlier this year for companies studying psilocybin for treatment resistant depression and major depressive disorder, and methylone for post traumatic stress disorder. FDA officials were careful to define what the hearing was not: it did not cover any individual drug application, the scheduling status of psychedelics as controlled substances, or their broader legalization or decriminalization, only the regulatory framework for supervised, clinical therapeutic use. Written public comments remain open through October 5th, meaning the hearing itself is an opening move in a much longer regulatory process rather than a decision point, but the size and speed of federal interest in this specific area of medicine is a real and fairly sudden shift from where psychedelic therapy sat in federal policy just a few years ago.

Good News

Researchers at the University of Maryland have identified a combination of proteins found naturally in rattlesnake blood that neutralizes venom from a wide range of pit vipers and true vipers, and in laboratory testing the combination proved roughly ten times more potent than the sheep derived antivenom currently used in hospitals. The work, led by biologist Sean Carroll and published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, builds on the lab's earlier discovery of a single protein called FETUA-3 that blocks a major class of venom toxins in western diamondback rattlesnakes. The new study shows that combinations of related FETUA proteins work even better together, and that the same combinations remain effective against venom from snake species evolutionarily distant from the diamondback itself, a property researchers call polyvalency. Current antivenom production still relies on the decades old method of injecting large animals like sheep or horses with venom and harvesting their antibodies, an expensive, slow, and supply limited process. An antivenom built instead from a defined set of recombinant, nature inspired proteins could eventually be manufactured more consistently, at greater scale, and with broader protection against multiple snake species from a single product, a real practical upgrade for a category of medicine that has not changed much in generations.
