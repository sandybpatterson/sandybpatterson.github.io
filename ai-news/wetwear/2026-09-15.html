Congo's Ebola Outbreak Killed Thirty-Five People in a Single Day

Plus scientists directly watched two DNA strands zip together for the first time, a Florida congressman's claim that illegal immigration is driving measles outbreaks falls apart under scrutiny, and a new study finds a kind of brain wiring that may help protect thinking skills as we age.

Sandy B. Patterson · September 15, 2026

Biggest Story

The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo added 58 confirmed cases and 35 deaths in a single day, according to figures covering September 13th to 14th, bringing the total to 7,258 confirmed cases and 3,510 deaths since the World Health Organization declared the outbreak a public health emergency of international concern on May 16th. Ituri province remains the hardest hit, with 5,659 cases and 2,583 deaths reported across 28 of its 36 health zones. North Kivu has recorded 1,281 cases and 797 deaths across 16 of its 34 health zones. Two additional deaths have been confirmed just across the border in Uganda. As of this week, 905 patients remain hospitalized in isolation. This is now unambiguously the deadliest Ebola outbreak on record, and the single day increase of 35 deaths shows the epidemic is not slowing, months after international emergency status was declared. Health authorities are contending with the same structural problems that have defined the response since the spring: a vast, difficult to access outbreak zone, community mistrust built up over previous Ebola responses in the same region, and a caseload large enough that isolation and contact tracing capacity is stretched thin even with international support. There is no approved vaccine specifically matched to this Ebola species circulating at the scale this outbreak would need, which is part of why case counts have continued climbing steadily rather than peaking and falling the way some past outbreaks have.

Biology

Scientists have directly watched two DNA double helices pair up and lock together for the first time, resolving a question that has puzzled researchers for two decades. Using high powered atomic force microscopy, a team at the University of Sheffield and the University of York imaged two DNA molecules zipping together despite the fact that DNA backbones carry a negative charge and should, in principle, repel each other. The answer turns out to be positively charged metal ions, including nickel, magnesium, and calcium, acting as molecular bridges. These divalent ions lodge in the gap between two DNA helices and partially neutralize the repulsion between their negatively charged backbones, allowing matching strands to align groove by groove like a zipper. The finding confirms a theory first proposed twenty years ago, but confirming it required actually watching the process happen at the molecular level, which had never been done before. This pairing behavior matters well beyond a laboratory curiosity, since it plays a role in genetic recombination, gene silencing, and how chromosomes get packaged, all processes with direct relevance to cancer development and other cellular malfunctions.

Misinformation

Florida congressman Byron Donalds, who is running for governor, has repeatedly claimed that rampant illegal immigration during the Biden administration is responsible for this year's measles outbreaks. FactCheck.org and PolitiFact both examined the claim and found no supporting evidence. When asked to justify the claim, Donalds specifically pointed to an outbreak in Southwest Florida, including a 107 case outbreak centered on Ave Maria University in Collier County. But Florida's health department has said that outbreak most likely began with a student's holiday travel from another U.S. state, not with an unauthorized immigrant. Infectious disease experts told FactCheck.org the actual driver of this year's measles resurgence is straightforward and already well documented: falling MMR vaccination rates among American kindergartners, which have slid from 95.2 percent in the 2019 to 2020 school year to 92.4 percent more recently, well below the threshold needed for herd immunity. Blaming immigration lets the actual, domestic, and far less politically convenient cause go unmentioned. That gap, between a real and boring explanation and a more politically useful invented one, is a pattern worth naming every time it shows up, not just this once.

Good News

A new study offers a genuinely hopeful wrinkle in how scientists understand aging and memory. Researchers at USC analyzed brain scans and cognitive testing from 459 adults age 60 and older, focusing on a thin layer of short, curved nerve fibers that sit just beneath the brain's outer gray matter, acting like local roads carrying signals between nearby regions of the cortex. Gray matter loss remains the single strongest predictor of cognitive decline with age, that part of the picture has not changed. But the study found that when this shallow layer of wiring stayed healthy, the usual link between gray matter loss and worse language performance weakened substantially. In people whose local wiring had already deteriorated, the same amount of gray matter loss corresponded to noticeably worse cognitive outcomes. In other words, this local wiring appears to offer some real, measurable resilience against the brain changes that come with age, even when the brain's overall volume is already shrinking. It is not a cure and it does not reverse gray matter loss, but it does suggest a specific, physical target for future research into why some older adults stay sharp while others decline faster, and that is a more concrete lead than aging research usually gets to work with.
