Pennsylvania Reports the First U.S. Measles Deaths of 2026

Plus: AI decodes a hidden switch inside 60 percent of human genes, a resurgent "turbo cancer" myth threatens a real cancer-vaccine breakthrough, a growing alfalfa sprout recall, and a chemo-free regimen showing real promise against breast cancer.

Sandy B. Patterson · August 26, 2026

Biggest Story

Pennsylvania's Department of Health confirmed this week that two unvaccinated Lancaster County residents have died of measles — the first measles-related deaths recorded anywhere in the United States in 2026, and the first recorded in Pennsylvania in 35 years. Officials are withholding further identifying details out of respect for the families, but confirmed both were unvaccinated and both lived in Lancaster County, the epicenter of the state's outbreak. Pennsylvania has now confirmed 393 measles cases this year, 86 of them diagnosed in just the past week, and Lancaster County alone accounts for roughly half the state's total — one of the largest single-county measles outbreaks anywhere in the country in 2026. The deaths land against a grim national backdrop: the CDC's most recent count puts the United States at 2,777 confirmed measles cases as of August 20, spread across 36 active outbreaks in 45 states, with 94 percent of patients unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status — already the country's worst measles year in more than three decades, a total this brief has watched climb for weeks. Pennsylvania's last measles death predates this outbreak by 35 years, near the tail end of the country's last major resurgence, before widespread vaccination drove sustained domestic transmission to zero. There is no specific treatment for measles once it's contracted; the MMR vaccine is the only real defense, which is part of why public health officials are describing these deaths not as a freak event but as the direct, foreseeable cost of declining vaccination rates and the same vaccine misinformation this brief has tracked spreading alongside them all month. Pennsylvania health officials say they are working with local providers to identify anyone who may have been exposed and are urging unvaccinated residents in the affected area to get the MMR vaccine immediately.

Biology

A team at UC San Diego, led by professor James Kadonaga and graduate researcher Torrey Rhyne-Carrigg, has decoded the DNA signature of the "initiator" — a fundamental genetic on-switch present at the start of more than half of all human genes that had defied precise characterization for decades. The team used high-throughput sequencing to measure the activity of roughly 500,000 different versions of the initiator sequence, then trained a machine-learning model on the results, producing the first AI system able to reliably predict whether a given stretch of DNA is or isn't an initiator. Because the initiator sits at the exact site where a gene's instructions first get switched on, the new model gives researchers a way to predict how disease-linked mutations there might disrupt gene activity — with direct relevance to cancer and other disorders tied to faulty gene regulation — and could also help engineers design synthetic "promoter" sequences with custom-tuned activity. The findings were published July 31 in the journal Genes & Development.

A study published August 21 in Nature Medicine, led by researchers at Columbia University, has found direct evidence that adult neurogenesis — the brain's ability to grow new neurons — stalls in people with major depressive disorder. Examining nearly half a million individual brain cells from the hippocampus of deceased donors, some with depression and some without, the team identified specific molecular programs that appear to shut down the birth of new neurons in the depressed brain, rather than finding a simple deficit in mood-regulating chemicals as older models of depression assumed. Because the hippocampus is one of the only regions of the adult brain that keeps generating new neurons throughout life, and because it plays a central role in memory and emotional processing, the researchers say restarting that process could become a genuinely new therapeutic target for depression.

Researchers at the University of Vienna, publishing August 19 in Science Advances, analyzed more than 5,800 chromosome-scale genomes spanning 4,454 species across 19 animal phyla — the largest comparison of its kind ever assembled — and found that animal chromosomes don't reshuffle randomly over evolutionary time. Instead, they follow a limited set of what the researchers call irreversible "evolutionary highways," preserving traceable structural patterns that stretch back more than 600 million years. Most lineages travel similar paths at different speeds, while a handful of outliers — including mosquitoes, glass sponges, and earthworms — occupy genome-structure territory with no close parallel anywhere else on the map.

Misinformation

Public health communicators are watching a familiar myth resurface at an inconvenient moment: "turbo cancer" claims — the false assertion that COVID-19 mRNA vaccines cause unusually aggressive cancers — are recirculating online just as Moderna and Merck's genuinely successful mRNA melanoma vaccine, covered in this brief earlier this week, moves toward wider use. The claim has no basis in evidence; large population studies have found no increased cancer risk following COVID-19 vaccination, and Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center has dismissed it outright as myth. But the posts driving it — built on misinterpreted animal studies, misused adverse-event reports, and recycled claims that mRNA vaccines alter human DNA — have been circulating steadily since 2023, and researchers tracking the trend worry the same rhetoric could suppress interest in an entirely different, genuinely effective class of mRNA vaccine before it reaches many of the patients who could benefit.

The vaccine executive order President Trump signed August 10 turns out to have a concrete number attached to it that hasn't gotten much attention: FactCheck.org reports the order's new "Gold Standard Childhood Vaccine Recommendations" would shrink the standard pediatric schedule from targeting 17 diseases down to 11, alongside directing that combination shots like the MMR be split into separate doses given on separate visits. Both President Trump and HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. defended the changes with claims fact-checkers say aren't supported by evidence — including RFK Jr.'s assertion that an unnamed "recent" study backs splitting the MMR vaccine, a study that, as far as anyone has been able to find, doesn't exist.

Public Health Data

The CDC's investigation into a multistate E. coli and Salmonella outbreak linked to alfalfa sprouts has grown to 55 confirmed illnesses across 15 states as of this week — 46 involving E. coli, 7 involving Salmonella, and 2 involving both. The source has been narrowed to alfalfa sprouts distributed by Everything Sprouts, LLC of Minneapolis, which recalled affected lots — sold under the Calco and Everything Sprouts brands, plus two sprout-mix cup products made with the same sprouts — on August 22. Illness onset dates range from May 31 through August 8, four people have been hospitalized, and no deaths have been reported.

The CDC's latest respiratory-virus snapshot, updated August 21, shows overall acute respiratory illness activity holding very low nationally, but COVID-19 is trending the other direction, with both national and regional activity increasing; RSV remains very low nearly everywhere, while rhinovirus and enterovirus activity is on the rise heading into the fall respiratory season.

Politics

In the early hours of August 8, the Senate passed a stopgap government funding bill, 90 to 6, that includes a provision blocking the White House Office of Management and Budget from finalizing or enforcing its contested rewrite of federal grantmaking rules — a plan that would have required political appointees to sign off on federal research grants, including scientific ones, to ensure they "advance the President's policy priorities." The provision, championed by Senate Democrats with the backing of Appropriations Chair Susan Collins, holds the rule off only until December 11, and only if it survives reconciliation with the House, which has not yet passed its own version; government funding itself lapses October 1, leaving the underlying fight over political control of federal science funding very much unresolved.

Illinois' House narrowly advanced a bill in May that remains one of more than 30 similar measures moving through over 20 state legislatures this year — it would let an independent board set the maximum price a prescription drug can be sold for in the state, automatically adopting whatever prices Medicare's own negotiation program lands on nationally, plus any future federally negotiated maximum prices, and applying them statewide. It's part of a broader wave of state-level action responding to a Medicare drug-negotiation program that's already estimated to be saving beneficiaries $1.5 billion a year in out-of-pocket costs.

Good News

A small but genuinely encouraging trial reported this week offers real hope for avoiding chemotherapy in some breast cancer patients: researchers tested a four-drug, chemo-free regimen — combining hormone therapy with targeted medicines that block two separate tumor-growth pathways — in 29 patients with HR-positive, HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer, and found that 97 percent showed clinical benefit within the first six months. It's a small, early-stage study, published in the Journal of the National Cancer Institute, but it points toward a future where some of the most treatable forms of metastatic breast cancer could be managed without the toxicity of standard chemotherapy at all.
