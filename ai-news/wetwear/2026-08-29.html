New Documents Contradict RFK Jr.'s Sworn Testimony About His Samoa Trip

A 2019 letter shows Kennedy asked to study Samoa's MMR vaccine program months before he told the Senate the trip had "nothing to do with vaccines" — testimony given before a measles outbreak that killed 83 people, mostly children. Plus: scientists organize to counter government vaccine misinformation, a light-activated eye drop restores sight in blind mice, and what chimps might be saying when they throw rocks at the same tree for a decade.

Sandy B. Patterson · August 29, 2026

Biggest Story

Newly obtained documents directly contradict sworn testimony HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. gave the Senate about a 2019 trip to Samoa, according to reporting published this week by the Associated Press and The Guardian. At his January 2025 confirmation hearing, Kennedy told Senator Ron Wyden: "I went there, nothing to do with vaccines. I went there to introduce a medical informatics system that would digitalize records in Samoa and make health delivery much more efficient." But a January 2019 letter Kennedy signed and sent to Samoa's then-prime minister — obtained by the AP and Guardian — explicitly proposed a vaccine-focused assessment of the country's measles, mumps, and rubella immunization program, following the deaths of two infants who had received the vaccine. The letter uses the word "vaccine" eight times. While in Samoa, Kennedy, then a prominent anti-vaccine activist, met with local anti-vaccine campaigners; months later, with vaccination rates still dangerously low, a measles outbreak swept the islands, sickening thousands and killing 83 people, most of them children under five — the deadliest measles outbreak the Pacific had seen in decades. Samoan officials have said Kennedy's visit lent credibility to the anti-vaccine activists whose messaging preceded the outbreak. Senator Ed Markey, who also questioned Kennedy at the hearing, said the new documents raise serious concerns that the man now running U.S. health policy misled Congress under oath, and at least one lawmaker has called for a formal investigation. HHS has not yet issued a substantive response to the documents.

Biology

Researchers studying wild chimpanzees in West Africa have documented a strange, ritual-like behavior that has now been observed at four separate sites: adult males repeatedly throwing rocks at specific trees over periods stretching more than a decade, building up piles of accumulated stones at the base. The chimps typically pant-hoot — a loud, long-distance vocal call — while throwing, and sometimes drum on the tree's buttress roots with their hands and feet. The behavior hasn't been seen anywhere else, including at long-term chimpanzee research sites where scientists have observed the same populations for decades, which is part of why researchers suspect it's a learned cultural practice rather than an instinctive one — and, given the pant-hooting and drumming that accompanies it, possibly communicative or even symbolic, though what exactly it communicates remains an open question.

A large tracking study, newly resurfaced in this week's science coverage, analyzed GPS data from 4,581 individual mammals and birds across the continental U.S., comparing their movement in 2019 against the same weeks in 2020, when COVID-19 lockdowns sharply reduced human activity in many areas. Human presence alone — not just permanent infrastructure like roads and buildings — reshaped how most of the 37 species studied used their surroundings, and different species responded in opposite ways: coyotes shrank their home ranges by roughly 11 square kilometers a week under high human activity, while gray wolves expanded their range, apparently traveling farther to avoid people altogether. Because the reactions varied so much by species, the researchers say conservation planning built around a single "give animals space" rule may be less effective than approaches tailored to how each species actually responds to people.

Misinformation

A coalition of vaccine scientists and medical leaders announced this week that they're launching a coordinated effort to counter vaccine misinformation coming from the U.S. government itself — an unusual move for a group more accustomed to fighting misinformation from outside official channels. The initiative comes against a backdrop its organizers say has gotten genuinely strange: a bill introduced in the Minnesota legislature this year sought to formally designate mRNA vaccines as "weapons of mass destruction" and criminalize their manufacture and distribution (it stalled and is now listed as dead), and a Texas group affiliated with Kennedy has claimed, without evidence, that this year's measles outbreak was started by a bioweapon. The coalition's stated goal is more basic than any of that sounds: making sure the public and healthcare providers have accurate information on vaccine safety, effectiveness, and availability, in an environment where a lot of what circulates now no longer reliably comes from the federal health agencies whose job that used to be.

Public Health Data

The CDC's investigation into a multistate Salmonella outbreak linked to jalapeños has identified 28 separate illness clusters tied to Chipotle and QDOBA locations across 8 states, part of an outbreak this brief has tracked developing over the past several weeks. Health officials continue to advise anyone who ate at an affected location and developed symptoms — fever, diarrhea, stomach cramps — within a week or two afterward to contact their healthcare provider, particularly given how widely the affected restaurant chains operate.

Politics

New CDC Director Erica Schwartz, confirmed by the Senate on August 5 as the agency's 22nd director and its first Black woman to hold the job, has spent her first weeks in office trying to reassure a rattled workforce. In an internal meeting with CDC staff around August 19, Schwartz said the agency is prepared to handle a range of disease outbreaks despite ongoing concerns about staffing and program cuts, and she has publicly pledged to "speak truth" about the science even where it's politically inconvenient. Her confirmation matters beyond personnel: it restores the CDC director's own authority to accept or reject vaccine-schedule recommendations from the agency's advisory committee, a power that becomes directly relevant given that a federal judge blocked Secretary Kennedy's January rewrite of the childhood immunization schedule back in March. Whether Schwartz uses that restored authority to hold the line against further changes, or to ratify them, is likely to be one of the more consequential vaccine-policy questions still unresolved heading into the fall.

Good News

A consortium led by the Institute for Bioengineering of Catalonia has developed light-activated eye drops that restored vision in completely blind mice within hours, without gene therapy, retinal implants, or any specialized lighting equipment. The compounds, called prosthe6, work by targeting receptors on cells that survive even after the eye's actual photoreceptors have degenerated — the underlying damage in blinding diseases like age-related macular degeneration and retinitis pigmentosa — effectively substituting for the lost cells' function using nothing more than the light already entering the eye. Treated mice regained their natural preference for dark environments, a reliable sign of restored light perception, within hours of a single eye-drop dose or injection. The approach, published in the Journal of the American Chemical Society, is still in the safety-evaluation stage, but the simplicity of eye-drop administration — as opposed to surgery or gene therapy — is exactly what's drawing interest from the company now working with the consortium toward eventual human trials.
