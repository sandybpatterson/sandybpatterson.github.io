Autism Has No Cure. The Market for One Is Thriving.

A wellness page is circulating a 2002 study of ten children as evidence that a vitamin B1 derivative treats autism. The numbers in the post are real. Almost everything they are being used to suggest is not — and the study turns out to be a nearly perfect specimen of how the autism cure business has always worked.

Sandy B. Patterson · November 23, 2026

The Post Going Around

A Facebook page called Genuinely Healthy recently shared a post about TTFD, a fat-soluble derivative of vitamin B1, and a study reporting that 8 out of 10 autistic children improved on it. The post is better sourced than most of what circulates in this space. The study exists. The journal exists. The numbers it quotes — ten children, 50 milligrams twice a day for two months, 8 of 10 improved — are the numbers in the paper.

That is exactly what makes it worth slowing down for. Most autism cure claims collapse the moment you check whether the study is real. This one survives that check and fails every check after it, which is the more common and more instructive pattern. The paper is real, and it proves almost nothing, and in the twenty-four years since it was published, no one has ever shown otherwise in a proper trial. To see why a post like this works, you have to look at the study itself, and then at the much older market it belongs to.

What the Study Actually Did

The paper is by Derrick Lonsdale, Raymond Shamberger, and Tapan Audhya, published in Neuro Endocrinology Letters in 2002 under the title “Treatment of autism spectrum children with thiamine tetrahydrofurfuryl disulfide: A pilot study.” Ten children with autism received TTFD in the form of rectal suppositories, 50 milligrams twice daily, for two months. Improvement was measured with the Autism Treatment Evaluation Checklist, the ATEC — a questionnaire filled out by parents.

The paper reports that 8 of the 10 children improved clinically. It also reports, in the same results section, that only 3 of the 10 were actually thiamine-deficient when the study began, measured by a blood test of thiamine-dependent enzyme activity. The urine testing found arsenic above control levels in 6 of the children and traces of mercury in all of them, and the authors framed the result partly as a heavy metals story: a vitamin that helps the body handle metals appeared to help autistic children.

The Study at a Glance

10children in the entire study
0children in a control or placebo group
3of the 10 were actually low on thiamine
8reported improved — as rated by their parents
0proper controlled replications in 24 years

Two more facts belong next to those numbers. The study had no control group, no placebo, and no blinding — every parent knew their child was receiving the treatment they were then asked to score. And in March 2002, while the paper was under review, Lonsdale filed a U.S. patent application on using lipid-soluble thiamine derivatives to treat autism. The patent, US 6,585,996, was granted in July 2003 and assigned to Westlake Laboratories. The lead author of the study stood to make money if the treatment caught on.

Why Ten Children Can't Prove a Treatment Works

None of this makes Lonsdale a villain or the paper a fabrication. Pilot studies are supposed to be small and preliminary; their job is to justify a real trial, not to settle a question. The problem is what a design like this cannot rule out, and the list is long.

Young children develop. Over any two-month window, autistic children gain skills, and a checklist scored before and after will often register that ordinary development as improvement. Parents who have sought out a treatment, administered it twice a day for two months, and believe in it will tend to see improvement — researchers call this the placebo effect by proxy, and it is strongest precisely when the outcome is a parent-completed questionnaire and nobody is blinded. Families also tend to start new treatments when things are at their worst, and the worst tends to ease on its own. A controlled trial exists to strip all of that away. This study had none of the stripping equipment.

The internal numbers argue against the paper's own theory. If TTFD helped by correcting a thiamine deficiency, the 3 deficient children should have been the story. Instead 8 improved. A treatment that “works” on five children who did not have the condition it treats is telling you the measurement is picking up something else — hope, development, and the passage of time are the usual suspects. The honest conclusion from this paper is one sentence: this might be worth testing properly. In twenty-four years, that test has never been run. The silence since 2002 is itself a result, and it points the other way.

The Older, Bigger Market

The TTFD paper did not appear in a vacuum. It was published in 2002, in the middle of the most fertile period the autism cure market has ever had, and its heavy-metals framing was aimed squarely at that market's central fear.

The modern era starts in 1998, when Andrew Wakefield published a paper in The Lancet suggesting a link between the MMR vaccine and autism. The paper was later shown to be fraudulent — data manipulated, undisclosed payments from lawyers suing vaccine makers — and was retracted in 2010, the same year Wakefield was struck off the medical register in Britain. The same year as Wakefield's paper, a hormone called secretin produced a wave of headlines after a small report suggested it improved language in autistic children; parents paid heavily for infusions before controlled trials found it did nothing beyond placebo. The mercury theory followed: the preservative thimerosal was blamed, and chelation therapy — drugs that bind metals so the body excretes them, approved for genuine heavy metal poisoning — was repackaged as an autism treatment under the banner of movements like Defeat Autism Now!

Chelation is where the market's cost stops being only financial. In August 2005, a five-year-old autistic boy, Tariq Nadama, died of cardiac arrest caused by dangerously low blood calcium during chelation treatment in Pennsylvania. The CDC documented three deaths from chelation-related hypocalcemia between 2003 and 2005. A Cochrane review later found no trial evidence that chelation helps autism at all, and concluded the risks outweigh any proven benefit — of which there is none.

The catalog since then has rotated without changing character. Chlorine dioxide, sold as Miracle Mineral Solution or MMS, is an industrial bleach when mixed as directed; the FDA has received reports of severe vomiting, life-threatening dehydration, and acute liver failure, and its acting commissioner said in 2019 that ingesting the products “is the same as drinking bleach.” The FDA's consumer warning on autism treatments also named hyperbaric oxygen chambers, detoxifying clay baths, raw camel milk, and essential oils marketed with cure claims. Unregulated stem cell clinics sell infusions for thousands of dollars. The products change. The sales pitch — a hidden cause, a detox, a breakthrough the establishment is ignoring — does not.

“Products or treatments claiming to cure autism are deceptive and misleading, because there is no cure for autism.”

U.S. Food and Drug Administration, consumer warning

One recent development deserves noting, because it removes a guardrail rather than adding one. The FDA's consumer page carrying that warning stood from 2017 until late December of last year, when the agency quietly retired it as part of what a spokesperson called a routine cleanup of dated content. Autism advocates objected, on the record, that chelation and hyperbaric oxygen have already killed children and that the warning was the page parents found first. The products are all still for sale.

Why Good Parents Buy It

It is easy to write this market off as gullible people buying nonsense, and it is wrong. The customers are mostly parents doing arithmetic that medicine has left them to do alone. Autism has no cure and no medication for its core features. What real medicine offers is support — speech therapy, occupational therapy, developmental and behavioral help, treatment for the conditions that often travel with autism like epilepsy, sleep disorders, and severe irritability — delivered slowly, expensively, and with honest uncertainty about how much it will help any individual child.

Against that, the cure market offers a cause, a mechanism, a product, and a testimonial, usually in one post. It offers them at the exact moment parents are most exposed: the months after diagnosis, when they have been told that early intervention matters and that the window is now. A parent who spends money on a useless supplement has usually not failed at reasoning. They have been outbid for hope by people willing to promise certainty that honest clinicians will not. Some of what is sold is merely useless. Some of it — bleach, chelation, restrictive diets pushed to extremes — takes children's health as the price of the lesson.

The “natural vitamin” framing deserves its own sentence, because it is doing quiet work in the TTFD post. A B vitamin sounds like food, not medicine, and food sounds safe. TTFD is a synthetic, fat-soluble derivative engineered to push thiamine into tissues at levels diet never reaches, given in the study as twice-daily suppositories to small children. Calling that “just a vitamin” is a marketing decision, not a description.

How the TTFD Study Fits the Pattern

Set the 2002 paper beside the market's history and the match is close to complete. A very small pilot study, run by an author with a patent on the treatment. A plausible-sounding nutrient story that dissolves on its own numbers, since most of the children were not deficient. A heavy-metals hook, in the urine arsenic and mercury findings, tying it to the chelation-era theory that was then at its peak. No control group, outcomes scored by unblinded parents, and then — the most telling part — no follow-up. No larger trial, no replication, no research program. A finding that was going somewhere does not sit untouched for twenty-four years.

What a study like this does have is an afterlife. It costs nothing to recirculate. Wellness pages can quote its abstract forever, because the abstract's conclusion — “8 of the 10 children improved clinically” — is a true sentence about a study that cannot support the weight placed on it. TTFD and related thiamine derivatives are sold online as supplements today, and every recirculation of the 2002 paper functions as advertising copy with a citation attached. The study's role in the market was never to be the last word. It is to be the footnote that makes a product look researched.

None of this rules out that thiamine matters for some children. A child who is genuinely thiamine-deficient should be treated for thiamine deficiency — that is ordinary medicine, and a blood test settles it. What the evidence does not support is the leap from “3 of these 10 children were deficient” to “this derivative treats autism,” and twenty-four years of non-replication is what that leap looks like when science declines to follow it.

How to Spot the Next One

The next version of this post is already written somewhere, with a different vitamin, hormone, or device in the lead role. The warning signs travel with it:

  • It promises a cure, recovery, or reversal — of a condition for which no cure exists.
  • The evidence is one small, old study — especially a pilot with no control group that was never followed by a larger trial.
  • Parents rate the outcome — testimonials and parent checklists, in place of blinded measurement.
  • It blames toxins and sells a detox — heavy metals, “chemicals,” or parasites, with a product that removes them.
  • Someone profits from the claim itself — a patent, a supplement line, a clinic, or a page that sells what the study praises.
  • The treatment cures everything — the same product marketed for autism, cancer, and chronic illness alike.
  • Mainstream medicine is the villain — “doctors don't want you to know” doing the work that data should do.

A claim does not need all seven. The TTFD post manages five of them while quoting a real paper accurately, which is the whole lesson: checking that a study exists is the beginning of checking a claim, not the end of it.

The Bottom Line

Autism cure scams persist because they sell the one thing legitimate medicine honestly cannot: certainty. The honest position is less satisfying and has not changed — there is no cure for autism, there are supports that help many children and adults live better, and any product claiming more than that is asking to be checked the hard way.

The TTFD study is a fair test of how to do that checking. It is a real paper, and it is also ten children, no controls, parent-rated outcomes, a result that contradicts its own deficiency theory, an author patenting the treatment as the paper went to press, and twenty-four years of silence afterward. A wellness page can quote “8 out of 10 improved” and be accurate in every word while misleading in every implication. That is not a loophole in how science communication works. For the autism cure market, it is the business model — and it has been running, with rotating products and a body count at its worst edges, for more than twenty-five years.
