Autism Has No Cure. The Market for One Is Thriving.

Sandy B. Patterson · November 23, 2026

The Post Going Around

A Facebook page called Genuinely Healthy recently shared a post about TTFD, a derivative of vitamin B1, and a study reporting that 8 out of 10 autistic children improved on it. The post is better sourced than most: the study exists, the journal exists, and the numbers it quotes — ten children, 50 milligrams twice a day for two months, 8 of 10 improved — are the numbers in the paper.

That is what makes it worth slowing down for. Most autism cure claims collapse when you check whether the study is real. This one survives that check and fails every check after it. The paper is real, it proves almost nothing, and in twenty-four years no one has shown otherwise in a proper trial.

What the Study Actually Did

The paper is by Derrick Lonsdale, Raymond Shamberger, and Tapan Audhya, published in Neuro Endocrinology Letters in 2002. Ten children with autism received TTFD as rectal suppositories, 50 milligrams twice daily, for two months. Improvement was measured with the Autism Treatment Evaluation Checklist — a questionnaire filled out by parents.

The paper reports 8 of the 10 children improved. It also reports that only 3 of the 10 were actually thiamine-deficient when the study began. Urine testing found arsenic above control levels in 6 children and traces of mercury in all of them, and the authors framed the result partly as a heavy metals story.

The Study at a Glance

10children in the entire study
0children in a control or placebo group
3of the 10 were actually low on thiamine
8reported improved — as rated by their parents
0proper controlled replications in 24 years

Two more facts belong next to those numbers. The study had no control group, no placebo, and no blinding — every parent knew their child was receiving the treatment they were then asked to score. And in March 2002, while the paper was under review, Lonsdale filed a U.S. patent application on using lipid-soluble thiamine derivatives to treat autism. The patent, US 6,585,996, was granted in July 2003. The lead author stood to make money if the treatment caught on.

Why Ten Children Can't Prove a Treatment Works

None of this makes the paper a fabrication. Pilot studies are supposed to be small; their job is to justify a real trial, not settle a question. The problem is what this design cannot rule out.

Young children develop. Over any two-month window, autistic children gain skills, and a before-and-after checklist will often register that ordinary development as improvement. Parents who administered a treatment twice a day for two months will tend to see improvement — the placebo effect by proxy, strongest when outcomes are parent questionnaires and nobody is blinded. Families also tend to start treatments when things are at their worst, and the worst tends to ease on its own. A controlled trial exists to strip all of that away. This study had none of the stripping equipment.

The numbers also argue against the paper's own theory. If TTFD helped by correcting a thiamine deficiency, the 3 deficient children should have been the story. Instead 8 improved. A treatment that “works” on five children who did not have the condition it treats is telling you the measurement picked up something else. The honest conclusion is one sentence: this might be worth testing properly. In twenty-four years, that test has never been run.

The Older, Bigger Market

The TTFD paper did not appear in a vacuum. It arrived in 2002, in the middle of the most fertile period the autism cure market has ever had, aimed at that market's central fear: heavy metals.

The modern era starts in 1998, when Andrew Wakefield published a paper in The Lancet linking the MMR vaccine to autism — later shown to be fraudulent, retracted in 2010, the year Wakefield was struck off the medical register in Britain. The same year, a hormone called secretin produced headlines after a small report suggested it improved language; controlled trials later found it did nothing beyond placebo. Then came the mercury theory: the preservative thimerosal was blamed, and chelation therapy — drugs that bind metals so the body excretes them, approved for genuine heavy metal poisoning — was repackaged as an autism treatment.

Chelation is where the cost stops being only financial. In August 2005, a five-year-old autistic boy, Tariq Nadama, died of cardiac arrest from dangerously low blood calcium during chelation in Pennsylvania — one of three chelation deaths the CDC documented between 2003 and 2005. A Cochrane review later found no trial evidence that chelation helps autism at all.

The catalog since has rotated without changing character. Chlorine dioxide, sold as Miracle Mineral Solution or MMS, is an industrial bleach when mixed as directed; the FDA has received reports of severe vomiting, life-threatening dehydration, and acute liver failure, and its acting commissioner said in 2019 that ingesting it “is the same as drinking bleach.” The FDA's consumer warning also named hyperbaric oxygen chambers, detoxifying clay baths, raw camel milk, and essential oils with cure claims. Unregulated stem cell clinics sell infusions for thousands of dollars. The products change. The sales pitch — a hidden cause, a detox, a breakthrough the establishment is ignoring — does not.

“Products or treatments claiming to cure autism are deceptive and misleading, because there is no cure for autism.”

U.S. Food and Drug Administration, consumer warning

The FDA's consumer page carrying that warning stood from 2017 until late December of last year, when the agency quietly retired it as a routine cleanup. The products are all still for sale.

Why Good Parents Buy It

It is easy to write this market off as gullible people buying nonsense, and it is wrong. The customers are mostly parents doing arithmetic that medicine has left them to do alone. Autism has no cure and no medication for its core features. What real medicine offers is support — speech and occupational therapy, developmental help, treatment for conditions that often travel with autism like epilepsy and sleep disorders — delivered slowly, expensively, and with honest uncertainty.

Against that, the cure market offers a cause, a mechanism, a product, and a testimonial, usually in one post — at the exact moment parents are most exposed: the months after diagnosis, when they have been told early intervention matters and the window is now. A parent who spends money on a useless supplement has usually not failed at reasoning. They have been outbid for hope by people willing to promise certainty that honest clinicians will not.

The “natural vitamin” framing deserves its own sentence. A B vitamin sounds like food, and food sounds safe. TTFD is a synthetic derivative engineered to push thiamine into tissues at levels diet never reaches, given in the study as twice-daily suppositories to small children. “Just a vitamin” is a marketing decision, not a description.

How the TTFD Study Fits the Pattern

Set the 2002 paper beside the market's history and the match is close to complete: a very small pilot study by an author with a patent on the treatment; a nutrient story that dissolves on its own numbers, since most of the children were not deficient; a heavy-metals hook tying it to the chelation-era theory then at its peak; no control group, outcomes scored by unblinded parents; and then — the most telling part — no follow-up. No larger trial, no replication. A finding that was going somewhere does not sit untouched for twenty-four years.

What a study like this does have is an afterlife. Wellness pages can quote its abstract forever, because “8 of the 10 children improved clinically” is a true sentence about a study that cannot support the weight placed on it. TTFD derivatives are sold online as supplements today, and every recirculation of the paper functions as advertising copy with a citation attached.

None of this rules out that thiamine matters for some children — a genuinely deficient child should be treated, and a blood test settles it. What the evidence does not support is the leap from “3 of these 10 children were deficient” to “this derivative treats autism.”

How to Spot the Next One

The next version of this post is already written somewhere, with a different vitamin, hormone, or device in the lead role. The warning signs travel with it:

  • It promises a cure, recovery, or reversal — of a condition for which no cure exists.
  • The evidence is one small, old study — especially a pilot with no control group that was never followed by a larger trial.
  • Parents rate the outcome — testimonials and parent checklists, in place of blinded measurement.
  • It blames toxins and sells a detox — heavy metals, “chemicals,” or parasites, with a product that removes them.
  • Someone profits from the claim itself — a patent, a supplement line, a clinic, or a page that sells what the study praises.
  • The treatment cures everything — the same product marketed for autism, cancer, and chronic illness alike.
  • Mainstream medicine is the villain — “doctors don't want you to know” doing the work that data should do.

A claim does not need all seven. The TTFD post manages five of them while quoting a real paper accurately, which is the whole lesson: checking that a study exists is the beginning of checking a claim, not the end of it.

The Bottom Line

Autism cure scams persist because they sell the one thing legitimate medicine honestly cannot: certainty. The honest position is less satisfying and has not changed — there is no cure for autism, there are supports that help many children and adults live better, and any product claiming more than that is asking to be checked the hard way.

The TTFD study is a fair test of how to do that checking. It is a real paper, and it is also ten children, no controls, parent-rated outcomes, a result that contradicts its own theory, an author patenting the treatment as the paper went to press, and twenty-four years of silence afterward. A wellness page can quote “8 out of 10 improved” and be accurate in every word while misleading in every implication. For the autism cure market, that is not a loophole. It is the business model — running, with rotating products and a body count at its worst edges, for more than twenty-five years.
