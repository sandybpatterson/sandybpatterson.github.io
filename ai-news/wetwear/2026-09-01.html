The U.S. Is on Track to Lose Measles Elimination Status This November

Confirmed cases have already surpassed all of 2025, and researchers now call losing the twenty-five-year-old elimination designation highly likely. Plus: a 324-million-year-old fossil rewrites how insects colonized land, a new poll finds most Americans are persuadable on vaccine myths rather than committed either way, a nationwide cyclosporiasis count tops 17,000, NIH reopens South African research funding with a catch, and a new standard of care arrives for HER2-positive gastroesophageal cancer.

Sandy B. Patterson · September 1, 2026

Biggest Story

Confirmed U.S. measles cases have climbed to 2,903 as of August 27, already surpassing 2025's full-year total of roughly 2,289 and putting the country on track for its worst measles year since before the disease was declared eliminated in 2000. Ninety-four percent of this year's cases are tied to one of 37 active outbreaks, and 93 percent of patients are unvaccinated or have unknown vaccination status. Elimination status itself is a specific, technical designation — twelve consecutive months without a continuous domestic transmission chain, verified against a robust surveillance system — and the U.S. has never actually lost it since 2000, even during smaller past outbreaks. The wider Region of the Americas, which includes the U.S. and Canada, already lost that same regional status in November 2025 after Canada's own outbreak ran past the twelve-month mark; the U.S. kept its individual country-level status at that review, but faces its own next assessment this coming November, and public health researchers now describe losing it as highly likely rather than merely possible. Losing elimination status wouldn't change how sick any individual patient gets, but it would be a formal acknowledgment that the virus has re-established itself here on an ongoing basis rather than arriving only in imported flare-ups — undoing, on paper, one of the more durable public health achievements of the last quarter-century.

Biology

A newly described 324-million-year-old fossil from Texas is reshaping the story of how insects first moved onto land. The specimen, named Chosha praecursor and published in Nature by a team led by Cai Chenyang of the Nanjing Institute of Geology and Palaeontology and Cambridge's Erik Tihelka, still carried paddle-like limbs on its abdomen — a holdover from aquatic ancestors — even as it showed clear adaptations for life out of water. Rather than insects making one clean jump from water to land, the fossil suggests early forms spent an extended stretch living amphibiously, keeping aquatic features while gradually acquiring terrestrial ones, much the way early tetrapods took their time leaving the water millions of years earlier.

Misinformation

A new KFF/CIDRAP survey of 2,480 U.S. adults finds that most people don't fall cleanly into "believes vaccine myths" or "doesn't" — a large, persuadable middle sits in between. Presented with four common false claims, including that MMR vaccines cause autism and that mRNA vaccines can alter DNA, only about 8 percent of respondents consistently endorsed the myths and only about 55 percent consistently rejected all four; nearly everyone else expressed real uncertainty about at least one. The researchers frame this "malleable middle" as the actual audience public health messaging needs to reach — not the small core of committed believers, who are unlikely to be moved by more information, but the much larger group whose uncertainty means the next thing they read could plausibly move them in either direction.

Public Health Data

The CDC's national cyclosporiasis count has climbed to 17,180 laboratory-confirmed cases as of August 24, up 1,464 in a single week, with 922 hospitalizations and two deaths across 48 states and Washington, D.C. Within that total, the cluster tied to recalled Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg lettuce — the outbreak this brief has followed for the past week — now accounts for at least 6,358 cases across 15 states, and investigators have identified a new specific exposure point: Taco Bell locations in nine states, including Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, and Ohio, where diners ate the recalled lettuce.

Politics

An internal NIH memo dated August 17 and signed by director Jay Bhattacharya lifted an 18-month ban on funding research in South Africa, clearing the way for new grants supporting HIV and tuberculosis research that had been frozen since a January 2025 executive order halting foreign aid. The memo argues NIH research grants are "distinct from the objectives traditionally associated with foreign aid," which is why they're exempt from the broader freeze. But the reversal comes with a new restriction attached: South African scientists will no longer be eligible for training grants from NIH's Fogarty International Center, on the grounds that South Africa's G20 membership and upper-middle-income status put it outside the program's intended reach — a partial reopening that trades one kind of funding access for another.

Good News

The FDA approved zanidatamab (Ziihera) in combination with the immunotherapy drug tislelizumab and chemotherapy on August 25, establishing a new first-line standard of care for HER2-positive advanced gastric, gastroesophageal junction, and esophageal cancers. In the pivotal HERIZON-GEA-01 trial, patients on the new combination lived a median of 26.4 months, compared with 19.2 months on trastuzumab, the prior standard treatment, for a patient population that hasn't seen a meaningfully better first-line option in years. Zanidatamab is now the first bispecific HER2-targeted antibody approved alongside a PD-1 inhibitor for this cancer type, regardless of a patient's PD-L1 status — meaning more patients qualify for the improved regimen than could access similarly targeted treatment before.
