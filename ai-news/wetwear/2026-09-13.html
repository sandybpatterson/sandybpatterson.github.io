Major Medical Societies Say Their Independent Vaccine Review Found No New Safety Concerns

Plus scientists turn plastic and crop waste into astronaut food, a specific false claim about the health of American children gets fact-checked, New York's measles count breaks down by city and state, the FDA's flu vaccine strain selection process gets back on its normal calendar, and a pancreatic cancer treatment strategy advances toward a real clinical trial.

Sandy B. Patterson · September 13, 2026

Biggest Story

The Vaccine Integrity Project and the American Medical Association have completed and published the independent evidence review they first announced back on September 2nd, and the headline finding is reassuring rather than alarming. Reviewing hundreds of peer reviewed studies published since August 2025, the review found no new safety concerns in the influenza, COVID-19, and RSV vaccines currently recommended for the 2026-27 respiratory season, building on a similar review the group conducted the year before. The point of the project is to fill a gap this brief has tracked for months: with federal vaccine guidance from ACIP and the CDC in flux or delayed, medical societies decided they could not wait for Washington to tell doctors what to recommend this fall. The completed review helped inform unified 2026-27 season guidance jointly issued by the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Academy of Family Physicians, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and the Infectious Diseases Society of America, four of the country's largest medical specialty groups agreeing on the same recommendations at the same time. The full results were published in JAMA, and the project also launched a public, searchable database letting clinicians, researchers, or anyone else look up exactly which studies were reviewed for which vaccine and which population. None of this changes what vaccines are available or how they are administered, but it does mean that, for the first time in months, a large and credible slice of the medical establishment has said clearly and in one voice what the evidence currently shows. A public webinar on the review is scheduled for September 23rd for anyone who wants the details straight from the people who did the work.

Biology

Scientists working on NASA's Deep Space Food Challenge have engineered yeast that can turn PET plastic and agricultural waste into ingredients for a protein rich, 3D printed food product they are calling microbial bites, or muBites for short. The process uses engineered yeast to break down and convert otherwise unusable waste, plastic packaging and crop leftovers among it, into proteins, fats, and other food grade ingredients, which are then 3D printed into a dense, nutritionally complete bite sized product. The original motivation is genuinely practical: a multi-year deep space mission cannot resupply food from Earth, and every kilogram of packaging or inedible plant matter a crew produces has to go somewhere, so a system that turns that waste back into usable calories solves two problems at once. Whether astronauts will actually want to eat something made from recycled plastic and crop stalks is a separate question from whether the chemistry works, and the researchers are candid that taste and texture are still active areas of development. Still, the underlying idea, using engineered microbes to close a waste loop that would otherwise just accumulate, has obvious applications well beyond spaceflight, including disaster relief and other settings where resupply is difficult or impossible.

Misinformation

Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s prepared remarks at the Republican midterm convention on September 10th keep generating individual fact checks days later, and PolitiFact's latest addresses a claim distinct from the ones already covered here: Kennedy's assertion that the United States now has, in his words, the sickest children in world history. PolitiFact rated the claim false. Childhood mortality and infectious disease rates have fallen dramatically since the 19th century, when diseases like diphtheria, measles, and whooping cough routinely killed children by the thousands before vaccines and modern medicine existed at all. Measured against any point in actual history rather than against an idealized recent past, American children today are, by nearly every hard metric, healthier than children have ever been, even accounting for real and worsening problems like childhood obesity, chronic disease rates, and mental health. The claim works rhetorically because it borrows the shape of a real concern, since chronic disease among children has genuinely risen in recent decades, and grafts that concern onto a historical comparison that falls apart the moment it is checked against basic mortality data. That gap, between a real underlying trend and a sweeping historical claim built on top of it, is the same pattern this brief keeps finding in health misinformation generally.

Public Health Data

New York's measles numbers this year break down in a way worth separating out from the national total already covered here. As of September 11th, the New York City Health Department has confirmed six measles cases within the five boroughs in 2026, while the New York State Department of Health has confirmed 45 more cases outside the city, for 51 total statewide. That split matters because it shows this year's activity in New York is concentrated well outside the five boroughs rather than being a New York City story specifically, a different shape than some of the state's past outbreaks. Nationally, the CDC's confirmed count sat at 3,294 cases as of September 10th, so New York's 51 cases represent a small share of the national total even though the state gets outsized attention given its size and its history with the disease. Neither figure has moved dramatically week to week, which is itself part of the story: measles transmission this year has been a slow, sustained burn in multiple pockets around the country rather than a single explosive event, and that is a harder pattern for both public health messaging and news coverage to keep pace with.

Politics

The FDA's vaccine advisory committee is back to something resembling its normal calendar, at least for one narrow but important task. A Federal Register notice published September 9th confirmed the Vaccines and Related Biological Products Advisory Committee will meet October 1st to recommend which flu strains should go into 2027 Southern Hemisphere influenza vaccines, the annual process that tells manufacturers what to formulate for countries where flu season runs on the opposite half year schedule from the United States. This is a smaller, more technical process than the Northern Hemisphere strain selection that shapes the flu shot Americans get each fall, but it runs through the same committee and the same basic process, and this brief has covered real disruptions to that process before, including outright cancellations of scheduled meetings with no explanation given. A meeting notice going out on a normal timeline, for a normal reason, is not itself a headline in ordinary years. In a year where this committee's basic functioning has been a live question, it is worth noting when the process runs the way it is supposed to.

Good News

A pancreatic cancer research strategy that has been building for a couple of years took a real step forward this month, advancing toward what researchers are calling a first-of-its-kind clinical trial. The approach, developed by researchers at the Sylvester Comprehensive Cancer Center at the University of Miami, targets a receptor called IL1RAP that sits at a control point linking pancreatic tumor cells, immune cells, and the scar-like tissue that surrounds and protects tumors, all three of which cooperate to keep the tumor hidden from the immune system and resistant to treatment. In laboratory experiments, blocking IL1RAP reduced that protective tissue and increased the activity of cancer fighting T cells, which in principle should make chemotherapy and immunotherapy more effective against a cancer that is notoriously hard to treat. Pancreatic cancer has one of the lowest survival rates of any major cancer precisely because it is so good at building defenses like this one, so a strategy that specifically targets the defense mechanism, rather than just the tumor cells themselves, is a meaningfully different approach. The planned trial would combine IL1RAP targeted therapy with existing chemoimmunotherapy in patients with operable pancreatic cancer before surgery, and while it has not started enrolling patients yet, getting a genuinely new mechanism this far is itself real progress against a cancer where progress has been slow.
