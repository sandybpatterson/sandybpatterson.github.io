More Than 1,000 California Hospices Have Been Removed From Medicare in a $3.5 Billion Fraud Crackdown

Federal investigators say ghost patients and fake hospice companies have drained billions from Medicare in Los Angeles County alone, plus twin antibody-gene systems may explain how bats dodge deadly viruses, a viral post badly mischaracterizes what Kennedy actually said about the fraud, new CDC data finds most American adults deal with fatigue, Congress returns to avert a government shutdown, and a nightly pill cuts sleep apnea events by nearly half.

Sandy B. Patterson · September 4, 2026

Biggest Story

Federal and state investigators say hospice fraud in Los Angeles County alone accounts for an estimated $3.5 billion in fraudulent Medicare claims, built largely on "ghost patients" — people paid, tricked, or enrolled using stolen identities so a hospice company can bill Medicare for care no one is actually receiving. The scheme concentrates geographically in strange ways: state auditors found 210 separate hospice agencies licensed within a single square mile of Van Nuys, with one commercial building alone holding licenses for 112 of them, and of the roughly 2,000 hospices operating in LA County, authorities believe close to half may be fraudulent. CMS imposed a six-month nationwide moratorium on new hospice and home health Medicare enrollments in May, one of the largest fraud-prevention measures in the agency's history, meant to stop fraudulent operators from entering the system or hopping state lines to dodge detection. California has pulled more than 1,000 hospices from Medicare since the crackdown intensified in early 2025, revoked nearly 500 state licenses since a 2021 moratorium on new hospice openings, and adopted emergency regulations in June tightening the criteria for approving new ones. The fraud has real victims beyond taxpayers: because hospice enrollment is meant for terminally ill patients and cuts off Medicare's regular coverage for other treatment, people fraudulently signed up — sometimes without fully understanding what they'd agreed to — have found themselves denied care and appointments they actually needed. CMS Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz has pointed to organized crime, describing "Russian, Armenian gangs, mafia" as leading much of the scheme; California officials have pushed back on federal characterizations of the state's own enforcement record, turning the cleanup into a genuine federal-state fight even as both sides agree the underlying fraud is real and enormous.

Biology

More than 500 species of vesper bats — the largest bat family, found on every continent except Antarctica — carry two separate, complete heavy-chain antibody gene systems, an immune arrangement researchers say has never been documented in any other mammal. Every other known mammal, humans included, builds antibodies from a single genetic toolkit; vesper bats appear to have duplicated the whole system at some point in their evolutionary history, giving them an entirely new way to generate antibody diversity. The research, led by scientists at Tulane University with collaborators at Stanford and the CDC and published in Science Advances, suggests this redundancy may help explain one of the more unsettling facts about bats: they carry an unusually large number of viruses, including some that cause severe disease in humans, without getting sick themselves. It's early work, but if the extra antibody system really does explain bats' viral tolerance, it could eventually point toward new approaches to human immune therapies.

Misinformation

A viral summary of Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s comments on Fox News's "Saturday in America" on August 29th claimed he'd said hospice patients cost taxpayers too much money because they weren't dying fast enough. Snopes and other fact-checkers found that isn't what he said. Kennedy was specifically describing the Los Angeles hospice fraud scheme detailed above, in which fraudulent operators enroll people as hospice patients — using stolen identities or coercion — who were never actually terminally ill in the first place, then bill Medicare for care they don't provide; because those patients aren't really dying, the fraudulent hospices built on the scam simply never discharge or lose them, which is one of the red flags investigators use to spot the fraud in the first place. The real story is arguably more damning than the misquote made it sound. It just isn't damning in the way the viral post claimed — a useful reminder that even a scandal this real can attract its own layer of misinformation stacked on top of facts that were already bad enough on their own.

Public Health Data

A new CDC data brief, published September 3rd from the 2024 National Health Interview Survey, finds that 71.5% of American adults report at least some fatigue in a typical three-month period, and 16.6% report fatigue on most or all days — what researchers classify as frequent fatigue. Frequent fatigue was notably more common in women (20.0%) than men (13.0%), and more common among younger adults than older ones. It varied sharply by race and ethnicity too: American Indian and Alaska Native adults reported the highest rate of frequent fatigue at 25.5%, more than double the rate among Asian adults, the lowest group at 9.1%. The report doesn't get into why those disparities exist, but it's the kind of baseline data public health researchers use to start asking that next question.

Politics

The House returned to Washington on August 31st for what's expected to be a one-week session focused on passing a continuing resolution to keep the federal government funded past the current September 30th deadline, this time through December 11th. It's the same deadline already putting pressure on NIH's grantmaking pace this fiscal year, and a shutdown would compound that problem directly — federal science and health agencies, including the CDC and NIH, would furlough much of their staff and pause new grant and contract activity until funding resumed. Speaker Mike Johnson is expected to bring the resolution up under a process requiring a two-thirds vote, which will need real bipartisan support to pass. Nothing about this week's fight is new or dramatic by Washington standards, but a lapse would have very concrete effects on public health infrastructure already stretched by a year of funding uncertainty.

Good News

A once-nightly pill for obstructive sleep apnea posted genuinely strong results in a large Phase 3 trial presented at this year's American Thoracic Society conference. The drug, AD109 — a combination of two existing medications, aroxybutynin and atomoxetine, designed to keep throat muscles from collapsing during sleep — cut patients' rate of breathing interruptions by about 44%, compared with 18% in the placebo group. More than 40% of patients improved enough to move into a less severe disease category altogether, and 18% reached complete disease control. It's not a replacement for CPAP for everyone, and it still needs FDA review, but for the large share of sleep apnea patients who can't or won't tolerate a CPAP machine, a nightly pill with results like this would be a genuinely significant option.
