The Lettuce-Linked Parasite Outbreak That Sickened Nearly 13,000 People Is Finally Over

Plus a newly discovered class of neurons that helps put the human brain to sleep, the U.S. measles count keeps climbing past a 25 year high, fact-checkers picked apart Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s convention speech, the Supreme Court declined for a second time to hear a New York vaccine mandate case, and human heart muscle turns out to regenerate after a heart attack.

Sandy B. Patterson · September 12, 2026

Biggest Story

The multistate Cyclospora outbreak tied to Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg lettuce is officially over, the CDC announced on September 11th, closing out one of the longest running public health stories this brief has followed this year. The final count: 12,883 confirmed illnesses across 21 states, more than 570 hospitalizations, and two deaths, both in Michigan. Illness onset dates ran from June 14th through August 17th, and the CDC says the contaminated lettuce is no longer in stores or restaurants, since Taylor Farms recalled all iceberg lettuce sourced from central Mexico back on July 17th. This brief covered the outbreak twice while it was still growing, once when the case count crossed 11,000 and again when reporting surfaced that the CDC's own parasitic disease surveillance team had shrunk from 11 people to 3 in the middle of the response. The FDA says it is still investigating exactly how the lettuce became contaminated in the first place, so the underlying question, how a parasite normally associated with imported fresh produce got into a major supplier's iceberg lettuce, remains open even though the outbreak itself has ended. Cyclosporiasis is caused by a microscopic parasite spread through contaminated food or water, not from person to person, and it typically causes watery diarrhea, cramping, and fatigue that can last for weeks without treatment. Closing the case with two deaths and hundreds of hospitalizations is a reminder that even a foodborne illness most people recover from can be genuinely dangerous at scale.

Biology

A newly discovered class of brain cells may force a rewrite of a century old assumption about how sleep actually works. Researchers led by Renata Batista-Brito at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai identified an unusually rare population of cortical neurons, called Sst-Chodl neurons, that can synchronize activity across large areas of the brain and appear to actively help put the brain to sleep. The long standing model held that sleep is controlled by deep brain structures, with the cerebral cortex, the outer layer responsible for thought and sensory processing, playing a passive role and simply following along. This new work, published in Nature on September 11th, suggests the opposite: the cortex is not just put to sleep, it helps do the putting. Because Sst-Chodl neurons are so rare and hard to study, the finding took specialized tools to even detect, and the researchers still need to work out exactly how these neurons interact with the deeper sleep centers the field has focused on for decades. If the model holds up, it could reshape how scientists think about sleep disorders that do not fit neatly into the old top down picture.

Misinformation

Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s speech at the Republican Party's midterm convention on September 9th drew one of the more thorough fact checking takedowns of his tenure as Health Secretary. CNN's Daniel Dale, reviewing the speech alongside remarks from other convention speakers, called it the most falsehood filled address of the night. Kennedy repeated a version of a long debunked claim that the government had lost track of 360,000 migrant children and turned itself into, in his words, the biggest child trafficking operation in world history, a claim fact checkers have knocked down repeatedly since it first circulated. He also cited preliminary CDC figures to suggest the nation's health was in decline, but the actual CDC trend line runs the other way: U.S. life expectancy fell during the COVID-19 pandemic, rebounded in 2022, reached a record high in 2024, and preliminary data suggest it likely set a new record again in 2025. Both claims follow a pattern this brief has tracked before, where a real institutional data point gets cited, then described in a way the data itself does not support. The venue matters here too: this was not an offhand remark, it was a prepared speech from a sitting Cabinet secretary at his own party's convention, watched by a national audience.

Public Health Data

The United States' measles count keeps climbing toward territory the country has not seen since before it eliminated the disease. As of September 10th, the CDC had confirmed 3,294 measles cases nationally in 2026, spread across 38 separate outbreaks, with 95 percent of cases, 3,123 of the total, linked to one of those outbreaks rather than occurring in isolation. That is now more than double the 1,274 cases the country saw in 2019, which had stood as the worst year since measles was declared eliminated in 2000. The Pan American Health Organization has scheduled a formal review of the country's elimination status for November, and public health officials have said for months that losing it is now the likely outcome rather than a remote risk. The overwhelming majority of this year's cases are in people who were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status is unknown, and national kindergarten MMR coverage has continued sliding, down from 95.2 percent in the 2019 to 2020 school year to a little over 92 percent most recently. None of these numbers move quickly week to week, but the trend line has been consistent in one direction all year.

Politics

The Supreme Court has now twice declined to get involved in a case brought by New York healthcare workers who were fired for refusing the state's COVID-19 vaccine mandate on religious grounds. In Doe v. Hochul, the workers argued their terminations violated Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which requires employers to accommodate sincere religious beliefs unless doing so would cause undue hardship. The Court denied the initial petition earlier this year, and on September 4th, as part of its final scheduled summer order list, it denied a petition for rehearing as well, leaving the lower court's ruling against the workers in place. The justices did not explain either decision, which is standard practice for a denial of certiorari, so it is not possible to say how individual justices came down or whether any dissented. New York's mandate itself, and its removal of the religious exemption that used to exist for healthcare workers, has been law for several years now, so this ruling closes out a legal challenge rather than changing the underlying policy. It is one more instance of a mandate era employment dispute reaching its final legal stop without the Court weighing in on the merits.

Good News

For the first time, researchers have directly shown that human heart muscle can regenerate after a heart attack. A team from the University of Sydney, the Baird Institute, and Royal Prince Alfred Hospital found evidence of heart muscle cells dividing and multiplying in damaged human heart tissue, a process called mitosis that had previously only been documented in the hearts of mice. The study, published in Circulation Research, overturns the long standing assumption that the human heart has no meaningful capacity to repair the muscle it loses after a heart attack. The researchers are careful to note that this natural repair response is far too weak on its own to replace all the cells a heart attack destroys, so nobody should read this as evidence that damaged hearts fix themselves. What it does open up is a real, human tissue confirmed target: if scientists can figure out how to safely amplify this existing repair mechanism, it could eventually lead to treatments for the heart failure that often follows a serious heart attack. It is a genuinely new piece of biology, not just an incremental confirmation of something already assumed.
