The Aging Brain Undergoes a Major Rewiring Between Ages 50 and 75

Plus a 125 million year old crocodile fossil reveals what may be the oldest known camouflage pattern ever preserved. Two Republican senators revive a debunked claim that COVID vaccines caused an 82 percent miscarriage rate. Gavi commits fresh funding to the Ebola response in Central Africa. And cannabis root derived nanoparticles show real promise against tumors in mice.

Sandy B. Patterson · September 16, 2026

Biggest Story

A major study published in the journal Science has identified a specific window, roughly between ages 50 and 75, when the human brain undergoes sweeping changes to how it organizes and regulates its own genome, offering a possible explanation for why Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative disease risk climbs so sharply in exactly that stretch of life. Researchers used cutting edge single cell technology to profile gene regulation and three dimensional genome architecture in individual cells taken from the human hippocampus, the brain region most critical to learning and memory. The biggest single shift involves microglia, the brain's resident immune cells. Microglia that originate during embryonic development and persist for decades decline substantially during this window, and get replaced by a new population of cells whose molecular signatures look more like immune cells recruited from the blood, cells generally associated with a more inflammatory profile. Alongside that shift, the researchers found weakening in the cells that help maintain the blood brain barrier, plus widespread deterioration in how the genome folds and organizes itself in three dimensions inside brain cells generally. None of this proves that these changes cause Alzheimer's on their own, but it gives researchers a specific, measurable, age anchored process to investigate as a possible mechanism, rather than just the vague observation that risk rises with age. Understanding what physically changes inside brain cells during this window is a meaningfully different kind of lead than researchers usually get to work with.

Biology

A remarkably preserved fossil from the Pyrenees is giving paleontologists a rare direct look at what an ancient crocodile relative actually looked like, 125 million years after it died. The specimen, a Lower Cretaceous crocodylomorph called Montsecosuchus depereti discovered at a site in Lleida, Catalonia, preserves fossilized skin, cartilage, claw sheaths, and what researchers describe as one of the most complete soft tissue records ever found in an early crocodile relative. The genuinely striking part came when researchers examined the fossil under ultraviolet light. The UV glow revealed alternating light and dark bands across the tail that scientists believe may represent a disruptive camouflage pattern, the kind that breaks up an animal's outline to make it harder for predators or prey to distinguish from its surroundings. If the interpretation holds, this would be among the oldest documented examples of color patterning ever preserved in a crocodile relative. Researchers are careful to note they cannot determine the actual colors involved, only that the underlying pattern of pigment concentration survived in a way clear enough to detect under UV light more than a hundred million years later.

Misinformation

Republican Senators Rand Paul and Ron Johnson have revived a specific, previously debunked statistic claiming that COVID-19 vaccination caused an 82 percent miscarriage rate among women vaccinated in their first or second trimester. Johnson made the claim in a Fox News Digital video, citing a distorted reading of a 2021 pregnancy study, and paired it with newly released text messages from Dr. Anthony Fauci's government issued phone that the senators characterized as evidence of a cover up. Fact checkers at PolitiFact and FactCheck.org both reexamined the underlying claim and found it does not hold up. The most comprehensive analysis of CDC pregnancy monitoring data, published in February of this year, found an overall miscarriage rate of 10.79 percent among the population studied and identified no increased week by week miscarriage risk associated with vaccination before 20 weeks of pregnancy. The 82 percent figure comes from selectively reading a much smaller, methodologically limited early dataset in a way that ignores how that data was actually structured. Two sitting United States senators repeating a number this far removed from the actual research, using a doctor's private communications as supporting evidence for a conclusion that same research does not support, is the kind of misinformation that carries more institutional weight than the usual social media version precisely because of who is saying it.

Politics

Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, has committed fresh funding toward the ongoing Ebola response in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda, on top of the earlier $50 million commitment made when the United States announced it would re-engage with the alliance back in June. The new package includes $7 million to provide 70,000 doses of the Ervebo vaccine through the global Ebola vaccine stockpile, plus an additional $6 million from Gavi's First Response Fund. Of that $6 million, roughly $4 million is earmarked to keep routine immunization services running for more than 1.17 million children under age two across five provinces, including Ituri and North Kivu, the two provinces hit hardest by the outbreak this brief has been tracking. Another $1.5 million supports technical assistance from Africa CDC, the World Health Organization, and UNICEF, with the remaining $500,000 going toward strengthening Uganda's routine immunization and data systems. With the outbreak now the deadliest Ebola epidemic on record and still adding dozens of deaths some days, keeping ordinary childhood vaccination programs running in the same provinces fighting Ebola is its own quiet, unglamorous fight against a second wave of entirely preventable childhood disease.

Good News

Researchers in South Korea have developed a plant based nanoparticle, derived from cultivated Cannabis sativa root tissue, that shows real promise as an oral immunotherapy platform in early animal testing. The nanovesicles, each about 113 nanometers across, remained structurally stable under simulated digestive conditions, suggesting they could realistically be taken by mouth rather than requiring injection, a genuine practical advantage if the approach ever advances toward people. In laboratory testing, the particles activated dendritic cells, a key type of immune cell, without causing cell damage, and in tumor bearing mice, oral treatment significantly slowed tumor growth. The effect was tied to a shift in the immune system toward tumor fighting T-cell activity and away from the regulatory immune responses that let tumors evade attack. The treatment also appeared to protect the liver and restore certain antioxidant activity, hinting at a possible protective effect against chemotherapy related damage on top of the direct anti-tumor effect. This is mouse data from a novel delivery platform, not a treatment ready for anyone, and plenty of promising preclinical immunotherapy work never makes it to human trials. But a stable, orally deliverable plant based platform that does this much in early testing is a genuinely interesting new direction worth watching.
