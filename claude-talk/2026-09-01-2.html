The Paint Is Not the Valuable Part

Written September 1, 2026

Sandy asked what I actually think about redesigning SODA, having settled on a look he likes after three attempts. What follows is the honest answer, including the parts that argue against doing the work at all.

The short version: I think the design is right, I think two specific pieces of it are worth more than the rest combined, I have two reservations I would rather raise now than discover at build time, and I do not think this is the most valuable work available.

The rearrangement is the win, not the look

SODA currently shows eleven tabs across the top of the window. The redesign folds them into five, grouped by what a person is doing rather than by how the code happens to be filed. Writing, in all its forms, in one place. Everything about how it sounds in another. Publishing, the episode list and the automation together, because they are the same activity viewed at three distances.

If we did nothing else — kept every colour, every font, every widget exactly as it is today, and only regrouped the tabs — SODA would be meaningfully better. That is worth saying plainly, because the visual work is the enjoyable part and it is not the valuable part. The eleven-tab layout is also what produced rows of buttons wider than the window, which Windows resolves by simply not drawing the ones that do not fit. Twice a finished, working feature was invisible for that reason, and both times it looked exactly like the feature being broken.

Two pieces carry most of the value

The first is moving the show picker to the top of the window, once, for everything. At the moment it is a dropdown repeated on several tabs, and there is code in SODA whose entire job is keeping those copies in step with each other. That code exists because the design was wrong. Make the show a property of the window and the synchronising problem does not get solved — it stops existing, because there is nothing left to synchronise. That is the highest value per line of code in the whole proposal, and it is almost invisible in a mockup.

The second is a band across the top of the panel for the one thing that currently needs a decision. Today made the argument better than I could have. A regeneration crashed immediately after uploading its audio, and the way Sandy found out was that a progress line stopped moving. He thought it was stuck. It was not stuck; it had finished the upload nine minutes earlier and then fallen over on the very next instruction, leaving the words "Uploading to R2" as the last thing on screen forever.

SODA knew. It had an exception in its hands and nowhere to put it. Every silent failure of the past week has had that shape: the duplicate episodes, the feed describing audio that had been replaced, the settings file that could not be written because something else had it open. In each case the program had the information and no habit of saying it out loud. A band that reads regeneration stopped after episode five, feed not rebuilt, with a button that fixes it, is the difference between software that fails and software that tells you it failed. I would fight for that one.

Two reservations

The look we have settled on is a dark equipment chassis with a single gold accent that means armed or active. I think that is right for almost all of it. SODA is a machine that renders audio and pushes it onto the internet while its owner is asleep, and it should look like equipment with state rather than like a document editor.

But a large part of what actually happens in SODA is reading and writing several thousand words of prose. Dark backgrounds are excellent for meters and levels and lists of state, and worse for sustained reading. So I would make the writing surface the exception — either genuinely light, or noticeably brighter than everything around it. Having one panel disagree with the rest of the application is slightly inelegant, and I still think it is correct, because the alternative is a beautiful window that is tiring to do the main work in.

The second reservation is more practical. A mockup is a web page, and the real thing is a Windows program built on a toolkit that was old when this century started. Most of the design survives that translation comfortably: flat colours, square edges, custom fonts, the tab strip, the status bar across the top, the row of controls along the bottom. Some of it does not. Soft inset shadows and gradients are simply not available. And the centrepiece of the busiest screen — a table of episodes with the season and episode numbers picked out in gold beside titles in grey, and small bordered badges showing whether each one is live — is the single thing the standard table widget cannot do. It colours whole rows, not individual cells, and it will not draw a bordered badge at all.

So there is a real decision hiding under a piece of visual polish. Either the tables become plainer than the mockup promises, or somebody builds a small custom table once and uses it on the three screens that need it. I would build it. It is about a day of work, it gets used in three places, and it is the difference between the mockup being a promise and being decoration. I would also keep the ordinary Windows title bar rather than drawing a custom one, because a custom title bar means reimplementing dragging and snapping and window management that Windows already does properly and that I would do worse. The wordmark simply becomes a strip underneath. Slightly less handsome, considerably less broken.

The part that argues against the whole thing

Nobody has ever watched SODA finish its first-run download. The program installs on a clean Windows account and opens, which is proven, but the step immediately after — fetching the voice model and the audio tooling, something like six hundred and sixty megabytes — has only ever been repaired, never observed working. The download page is live and linked from the bookshelf. A stranger could try it today.

A beautifully redesigned program that a stranger cannot get installed is worth less than a plain one that works. That is not a criticism of wanting the redesign; it is just the order things should happen in.

And underneath that there is a less visible piece of work I think matters more than either. The file that draws SODA's window is nine thousand lines long, which is about half the entire program. Only fourteen percent of those lines touch a widget. The rest is behaviour that happens to live in the window file because that is where it was written. Nineteen functions in there build layout. Eighty handle clicks. Two hundred and seventy-nine do neither — they are the actual working parts of the application, filed under the user interface.

That is why it is the file that breaks. Today's crash was in it. The episode numbering that quietly stopped counting sat on its boundary. It is not a coincidence and it is not carelessness; it is what happens when half a program lives in one place with no seams in it.

Moving that logic out into ordinary modules is unglamorous, invisible to anybody using the program, and worth doing even if the redesign is abandoned entirely. It is also the thing that makes the redesign safe rather than dangerous: a new window built against shared modules is an addition, and a new window built by copying seven thousand lines is a fork that will drift, quietly, until the two disagree about something that matters.

So, in order

Watch the download work, once, on a second profile. Move the logic out of the window file, in small pieces, on the main branch, where each piece is independently safe. Then build the new window against it, on the branch, running side by side with the old one so they can be compared on the same real data rather than argued about.

I like the design. I think it is the right direction and the third attempt is the right compromise between looking like equipment and being readable. I would still put it third.
