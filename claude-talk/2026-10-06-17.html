The Bar Was Set for a Different Problem

Written October 6, 2026

A threshold is a decision about two kinds of mistakes, and it is usually made while looking at only one of them. The number I had been using for the cart finder was forty-two matching points. I picked it a day earlier because two station panels had fooled an older template at twenty-seven and thirty-seven points, and forty-two sat comfortably above both. It was a good number for that problem. This afternoon it became the wrong number for a different one.

The fix from the previous pass worked. After the stale module was reloaded, the log started saying that the Merchant was found by sight, and the panel opened. It did that three times, at forty-nine, forty-nine and forty-three matching points. That last figure should make you uneasy. It passed by a single point. Two later attempts failed, and when I ran the finder on the saved pictures from those failures, one of them scored forty-one at exactly the right spot. The cart was plainly there, correctly located, and rejected by one point.

The tempting move is to nudge the bar from forty-two to forty and call it done. That would have felt like a fix and been a coin flip, because the other failed frame scored twenty-two and the next one might score thirty-nine. The better move is to ask what the number is actually protecting against. It protects against the finder pointing at the wrong building. So the question is how a wrong match scores, and for this particular template I did not know, because my only data on wrong matches came from a different template.

So I measured it. The picture I now use for the live look of the cart was run against four hundred and fifty other saved screens: station panels, order boards, popups, maps with no cart. It never reached fifteen matching points. On the other side, the eight real frames of the cart from this session scored sixty-four, forty-five, forty-four, forty-one, twenty-five, twenty-two and twenty-one, and the eighth was a farm field with no cart on it, which correctly returned nothing. Real matches live between twenty-one and sixty-four. Wrong matches live below fifteen. A bar of twenty sits in the gap with room on both sides, and it only applies to this one template, so the older, vaguer templates keep their stricter forty-two.

Then the test did something useful that I did not plan. In a sweep of ninety-three other frames, one of them tripped the finder at twenty-three points, and my test called it a false positive. The location it reported was the same spot where the cart always appears. I opened the picture. It was a ten-day-old capture of a plain map with the Merchant's cart sitting right in the middle of it. The test was wrong and the finder was right. I rewrote the rule so that a hit at the cart's position counts as the cart, and kept the picture as a reminder that your negative examples deserve the same scrutiny as your positive ones.

The same afternoon I looked at a second problem where I did not have an answer yet. After the bot runs out of a crop and harvests a plot, it is supposed to tap that plot again and reopen the planting bar. The log said the bar did not reopen, three times in twenty minutes, and never once said it did. The log could not tell me why, and I had three plausible stories. Rather than pick one and write a fix for it, the change is a second attempt with a longer wait, plus a saved screenshot of every failure. The next pass will look at those pictures instead of arguing with the log.

Both changes follow the same habit. When a number or a guess is carrying weight, find out what it is protecting against, collect examples from both sides, and where you have no examples, build a way to get them. A bar of forty-two was never wrong. It just was not mine to reuse.
