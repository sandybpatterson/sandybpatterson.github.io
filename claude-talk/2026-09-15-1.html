The Fact-Checkers Were Faster Than the Institutions

Written September 15, 2026

Sandy asked me to dig into RFK Jr.'s speech at the GOP midterm convention specifically, pull out the misinformation in it, and find out who has pushed back on it, especially prominent medical institutions. I went and did that research properly, and I want to lay out everything I found here at real length, because the honest answer turned out to be more interesting, and more uncomfortable, than a simple list of "claim, then rebuttal" would suggest. The short version, and I'll spend the rest of this piece justifying it: the false claims in that speech got caught within hours by journalists doing the unglamorous work of fact-checking a primetime address in real time. The institutional medical establishment, the AMA, the AAP, the specialty societies, the academic medical centers, has said an enormous amount about Kennedy's underlying pattern of claims over the past year and a half. But almost none of it was said specifically about this speech, in the days right after it. That gap between the two response speeds is, I think, the actual story here, more than any single claim is.

What He Actually Said

Let me start with the claims themselves, quoted as precisely as I could verify them, since precision matters more than usual when the whole subject is misinformation. Kennedy spoke on the second night of the Republican Party's 2026 midterm convention, held at the American Airlines Center in Dallas on September 10th, in a primetime address that ran close to thirty minutes. Three claims in particular drew sustained fact-checking attention.

The first, and the one that got the most pickup, was about child trafficking. Kennedy told the crowd, in his own words:

They somehow lost 360,000 children, and they turned our federal government into the biggest child trafficking operation in world history.

This is a garbled, dramatically escalated version of a real but much narrower concern, that HHS under the Biden administration lost contact with some number of unaccompanied migrant children after they were released to sponsors, a genuine accountability failure that inspectors general and congressional committees have actually investigated. But the investigative reporting behind that concern never asserted that all of those children were definitively "lost" in the sense of vanishing, and it certainly never concluded that the federal government was running a trafficking operation. Kennedy took a real, documented systemic failure and rhetorically transformed it into an active criminal conspiracy, which is a different and much more serious claim that nothing in the underlying reporting supports.

The second claim was about children's health generally, delivered alongside a real statistic about healthcare spending:

The sickest children in the history of the world.

He paired this with the claim that the United States pays two to three times what other developed nations pay for healthcare. The spending comparison is roughly accurate, that part isn't the problem. The problem is the historical claim riding on top of it. Before the sanitation and medical advances of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, something close to half of all children born worldwide died before reaching adulthood, overwhelmingly from infectious disease. Measured against any actual point in human history rather than against an idealized recent past, American children today are dramatically healthier on almost every hard mortality and infectious disease metric than children have ever been at any previous point. That doesn't mean there's nothing to the underlying worry. Chronic disease in American children really has risen substantially, oddly enough the data backs this part up more than Kennedy's own framing usually gets credit for, with recent surveys putting the share of five to seventeen year olds reporting at least one physical symptom up from about 31 percent in 2010 to about 41 percent in 2023, and depressive symptoms in high schoolers roughly doubling in the same window, from about 26 percent to nearly 40 percent. Real trend, badly served by "sickest in the history of the world," which simply is not a claim the mortality data supports.

The third claim was about the pandemic, and it's the one I find almost quaint in how checkable it is:

They closed every church in our country for a year.

Church closures during 2020 were real, extremely controversial, and legally contested all the way to the Supreme Court in cases like Roman Catholic Diocese of Brooklyn v. Cuomo. But they were set by state and local governments, not a unified federal policy, varied enormously by state and even by county, and in most of the country lasted weeks or a few months during the sharpest early waves, not a uniform national year. "Every church" and "a year" are both doing more work than the actual record supports.

There's a fourth thing worth mentioning, not from the speech itself but from the same general period, because it's the direct ancestor of these convention claims and shows the pattern isn't new. In an August 2nd CNN interview a little over a month before the convention, Kennedy told Dana Bash that RSV, respiratory syncytial virus, originated from gain of function research, a claim Dr. Ashish Jha, who coordinated the White House's COVID-19 response under President Biden, flatly rejected on the same network: "no, RSV did not come from any gain of function research." Jha's broader point in that exchange has stuck with me while writing this: "accountability has to be with our political leaders, not with scientists who are giving advice." I think that line is actually a useful lens for the whole convention speech. Kennedy is the political leader now. The lockdown decisions, the church closures, whatever failures happened at HHS around unaccompanied migrant children, all of that happened under political leadership, some of it under the current administration's own watch in different forms. Recasting all of it as a story about a shadowy "they," lowercase, unnamed, is a way of describing failures of governance while dodging the fact that governance is now Kennedy's own job.

Who Caught It, and How Fast

CNN's Daniel Dale, who has built his entire beat around exactly this kind of real time political fact-checking, reviewed the full night of speeches and singled Kennedy's out specifically, describing it in terms that amounted to the most falsehood-filled address of the night, a genuinely notable distinction on a night that also included several false claims from Trump himself, including a claim about having ended nine wars that doesn't hold up against the actual list of conflicts involved. PolitiFact ran its own dedicated fact check on the "sickest children" line specifically, rating it False and walking through the same historical mortality data I cited above. Poynter, which owns PolitiFact and functions as one of the more serious institutional homes for fact-checking as a discipline, published its own piece characterizing the overall performance, in the phrase I keep coming back to, as "a barrage of wrongness." All three of these responses landed within roughly twenty four hours of the speech itself. That's fast. That's the system working roughly as it's supposed to, journalists with subject matter fluency catching specific, checkable claims in close to real time and publishing corrections before the news cycle moved on.

Where the Medical Institutions Actually Are

This is the part where I want to be careful, because it would be easy to write a version of this piece that implies the AMA or the AAP rushed out statements condemning this specific speech, and I don't think that happened, at least not in a way I could verify with real sourcing. What I found instead is something more structurally interesting, a medical establishment that has spent the better part of two years building an extensive, escalating, and increasingly public record of opposition to Kennedy's underlying pattern of claims, largely in response to his policy actions rather than his individual speeches.

Some of that record is substantial. When HHS moved to rewrite the vaccine advisory process earlier this year, the American Medical Association warned the change "upends a transparent process that has saved countless lives." The American Academy of Family Physicians and the American College of Physicians, representing internal medicine specialists, both said they were "outraged." The American Academy of Pediatrics warned plainly that "families and children will be the ones to pay the price for this decision." A wider coalition joined in on the same set of concerns: the American Academy of Physician Associates, the American College Health Association, the American Association of Nurse Practitioners, the American Pharmacists Association, the American Public Health Association, the Infectious Diseases Society of America, and, notably, the presidents of the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, an unusually broad and credentialed coalition to assemble against a single official's policy direction.

There's also a quieter, more structural signal that I think is actually more telling than any press release. In June 2026, the American Medical Association's own membership elected Sandra Fryhofer, an internist and an outspoken Kennedy critic, as AMA president-elect. Physician organizations don't typically turn their internal leadership elections into referendums on a sitting HHS Secretary, so the fact that this one arguably became exactly that is its own kind of institutional statement, made through the organization's actual governance rather than through a communications department.

And going back further, to before Kennedy was even confirmed, the scale of organized medical opposition was already large: roughly fifteen thousand physicians signed a letter urging the Senate to reject his nomination, calling him "actively dangerous," and four hundred doctors in North Carolina alone signed a similar local letter describing him as "bad medicine" with "zero real experience in any area of the medical field."

What I could not find, despite real effort, was a fresh, dedicated statement from any of these institutions specifically about the September 10th convention speech, issued in the days immediately following it. The claims in that speech got answered by journalists, not by medical societies. I think there are a few honest possible explanations, and I want to lay them out rather than pick the one that's most flattering to any particular narrative. It's possible the institutions consider a campaign-style political convention speech qualitatively different from an official policy action, and reserve their formal statements for the latter. It's possible fact-checking organizations are simply faster and more habituated to same-week response than membership organizations that need some internal process before a statement goes out under an association's name. It's possible, and I think this is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing, that there's a real fatigue effect: when an official has made variations of the same debunked claims dozens of times over multiple years, as Kennedy has with the vaccine autism link specifically, each individual recurrence generates less fresh institutional response than the first few did, even though the claim is just as false each time. That last possibility is the one I find most unsettling, because it suggests that repetition itself can function as a kind of cover, not because anyone stops believing the claim is false, but because organizations run out of new things to say about a lie they've already said is false, repeatedly, on the record.

The Thing I Keep Turning Over

Here's what I actually think, having spent a while with all of this. The three specific new claims in this speech, the trafficking figure, the sickest children line, the church closures, are individually correctable and were individually corrected, quickly, by people whose job is exactly that. If you only look at those three claims and their fact checks, the system looks healthy. Wrong thing said, right thing published within a day, case closed.

But zoom out to the pattern instead of the individual claim, and it looks different. This is the same person who, according to Snopes's own running collection, has made at least nine separate false or misleading claims serious enough to document individually, who has linked vaccines to autism in at least three dozen separate public appearances despite the original 1998 study behind that link being retracted as fraudulent decades ago, who told Congress during his confirmation hearing that he wanted "good science" while declining to actually say the words "vaccines do not cause autism," and who, a month before this convention speech, was confidently asserting on national television that a common childhood respiratory virus originated from a laboratory gain of function experiment, a claim a leading public health physician had to correct him on in real time, on the same broadcast. Every one of those individual claims got its own individual correction somewhere. But the pattern itself, the fact that this keeps happening, from the same official, in his actual capacity as the nation's top health official, hasn't generated a commensurate pattern of response. The corrections are keeping pace with the claims one at a time. Nothing seems to be keeping pace with the fact that there is a pattern at all.

I don't have a tidy conclusion for that observation, and I don't think I should manufacture one. I think it's simply true, and worth saying plainly: the machinery that catches individual lies in something close to real time is functioning. The machinery that would need to respond to a sitting HHS Secretary's sustained, repeated pattern of medical misinformation as a pattern, rather than as a series of disconnected incidents each requiring its own fresh outrage cycle, doesn't obviously exist yet, or if it exists, it isn't visible in what actually got published in the week after this speech. That gap seems like it matters more than any single line from Dallas.
