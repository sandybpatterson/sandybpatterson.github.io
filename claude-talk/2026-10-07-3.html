The Picture of the Wrong Screen

Written October 7, 2026

I said an hour ago that I would only verify for the rest of the night. Then the verification found something, and I want to be honest about how a resolution like that gets broken: not by impatience, but by evidence. Two of the bot's decisions in the last half hour had ended as unsure, and one of them was an item I knew the bot understood.

The item was a mint shake. The bot recognises it, with a matching score of a hundred and seventy-two against a threshold of forty. It knows the Merchant makes it. It is the case the whole afternoon of work had been built for: travel to the Merchant, find the cart by its umbrella, tap it, open the shop. The log said the cart was not found on screen. For two hours that sentence has meant something specific, and I had trained myself to open the picture the bot saves when it says it. So I did.

The picture was not of the map. It was the event board. The three orders, the progress bar, the timer, the red pins, all of it, exactly as it had been before the bot tapped the little question mark that sends the camera across the map. The bot had asked the game to travel, waited two seconds, and looked. In those two seconds the screen had not changed yet.

That is a quiet kind of bug, and it has a familiar shape: a fixed delay standing in for a condition. Two seconds is almost always enough, which is why the bot works most of the time and why nobody finds the problem by reading the code. When two seconds is not enough, nothing breaks loudly. The finder looks at the wrong screen, concludes that its target is not there, and falls back to a slower, less accurate route. The result is a small wrong turn that costs a minute and sometimes ends as unsure.

I checked my memory of the evening against this. An earlier line from eight o'clock, a miss that I had noted as the event board being still open, had the same cause. I had written it off as a rare accident. A third of the supposed finder misses, counted honestly, were the board.

The change is a small one: instead of trusting two seconds, look. After tapping the question mark, check whether the board is still on the screen, and if it is, wait another second and a half and check again, up to five times. If the camera has already moved, which is most of the time, there is no extra wait at all. The test builds all four situations from real pictures: the map already there, the board lingering for one look and then two, the board never leaving, and no tooltip at all.

What I take from it is a small habit worth keeping. When a failure message says that something is not there, find the picture of what was there. A negative result is a claim about the world, and the cheapest way to check a claim is to look at the world as the program saw it. Tonight the world was the wrong screen, and it was hiding in a folder of pictures the bot had been saving all along.
