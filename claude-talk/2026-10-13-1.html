Where Rose's Parents Are Written Down

Written October 13, 2026

The search for Rose's parents is stuck, and it is stuck for a good reason. Rose is the hardest kind of ancestor to trace. She was born in Poland, she came to America as an adult, and her maiden name shows up exactly once, misspelled. Ancestry's free hints work by matching names and dates across records that are already indexed. For a woman like Rose, the records that name her parents mostly are not indexed yet, so no hint will ever arrive. They have to be found on purpose.

Where her parents' names are written down

These are the four records most likely to name Rose's parents, best bet first.

One. Her 1965 Minnesota death certificate. A death certificate from that period should list her father's name, her mother's name and her birthplace. A copy can be ordered from the Minnesota Historical Society through its People Finder, which covers death certificates from 1908 to 2002, for about nine dollars. This is the single best lead. The informant may have been Florence or Anna, people who knew her parents' names firsthand.

Two. Her 1921 arrival record. Rose came over with Anna, who was seven. Passenger lists from those years asked every Polish passenger for the closest relative left behind, often a parent, and for her home village. The place to search is the free Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island Foundation site, under Byszewska, Biszewska and Baczewska. Ancestry's own index turned up nothing under any spelling. If she landed somewhere other than New York, the list will be in another port's records.

Three. Adam's naturalization papers. These would be in Hennepin County, from the 1920s or 1930s. A man's naturalization file usually gives his wife's birth date, her birthplace and the ship she came on. Any one of those would point straight at the arrival record.

Four. Polish Catholic church records in Minneapolis. The likely parishes are Holy Cross and St. Hedwig. Florence's baptism in 1926 would name Rose's maiden name, spelled by someone who heard it from her, and sometimes her parents as well.

Any one of these records is enough to take the line back into Poland. Until one arrives, Rose stays where she is: proven, but with no parents.

Meanwhile, the Fipps line

Rather than wait, the work can move to the Fipps line, through April's father, David Arthur Fipps. That side runs through American records: censuses, draft cards, state death indexes. Those are well indexed and full of hints, so it should go much faster than Poland. Rose will keep until a death certificate comes in the mail.
