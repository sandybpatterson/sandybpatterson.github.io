A Thesis Four Parts Ahead of Its Own Evidence

Written September 12, 2026

Twenty-seven chapters exist of a book that plans sixty-four, which means any review written now is reviewing a claim rather than a finished argument: the claim, stated in the book's own working notes, that this is "not a book that concludes religion is simply evil," that it concludes instead that "power is simply dangerous," and that the mystics and honest doubters who resisted that power from inside the institution matter as much as the corruption itself. Four parts in, at the halfway point of Part Four, that second half of the claim has almost no evidence behind it yet. This is either a serious structural miscalculation or a genuinely bold bet on reader patience, and it is worth taking seriously as a critical question rather than assuming the answer, because the chapters that exist are good enough to make the bet interesting either way.

What the Organizing Device Actually Buys

The book hangs its entire two-thousand-year argument on Marcion of Sinope, a second-century shipowner excommunicated for trying to cut the Hebrew Bible's God out of Christianity, and the device earns its keep in a way a lot of single-figure organizing conceits don't. Chapter 1 ends on him by name, having spent its whole length demonstrating exactly the kind of textual chaos his challenge eventually forced the church to resolve. Chapter 12 reads the Apostles' Creed itself as an eighteen-century-old argument with him that nobody reciting it knows they're having. By Chapter 24, the device has to do harder work: the book wants the Cathars to rhyme with Marcion without claiming a provable bloodline across nine centuries and three thousand miles, and it says so directly, landing on "the honest version sits between those two claims" rather than picking the more satisfying one. That's a real authorial discipline, not a hedge for its own sake. It shows up again, more subtly, by Chapter 25, where the book declines to force Marcion into a chapter about the Knights Templar at all, because there's no honest theological connection to make and it says so by simply not making one. A device that knows when to stay home is more trustworthy than one that shows up everywhere on schedule.

Part Four's Real Discovery

The strongest sustained stretch so far is the run from Chapter 24 to Chapter 27, and what makes it work isn't the body count, it's the taxonomy underneath it. Four institutions accuse four different groups of heresy inside of five chapters, and the book is careful to show that "heresy" means something structurally different each time. The Cathars actually held the dualist theology they were burned for holding. The Templars were tortured into confessing to a religion nobody, including their accusers, seems to have believed they practiced, while the actual motive sat in Philip IV's ledger book. The Spanish Inquisition engineered its cruelty around a specific, quotable doctrinal loophole, a church that "could not shed blood" burning people anyway through the recorded fiction of "relaxation to the secular arm," which is one of the more chilling sentences the book has produced precisely because it under-reacts to what it's describing. And the Huguenots killed in 1572 weren't accused of heresy in any formal sense at all; they were simply killed, and then a pope had the killing commemorated in fresco a few dozen feet from where he said Mass. Same word, four different real crimes underneath it: dissent, debt, ethnic purification, political consolidation. That's a genuinely sharp piece of historical argument, built entirely out of narrative rather than announced as a thesis, which is exactly what the book's own style notes ask for and don't always get.

The Discipline of the Uncomfortable Number

What impresses most on a close read isn't the confident material, it's what the book does with the contested material. The popular figure for the Spanish Inquisition's death toll under Torquemada, "two thousand," gets correctly traced to a nineteenth-century historian named Graetz instead of being borrowed, uncredited, from a modern scholar's very different and much larger number covering three and a half centuries instead of fifteen years. The Knights Templar's Friday-the-13th association gets identified as a twentieth-century invention layered onto a real thirteenth-century atrocity. The legend of Charles IX personally shooting fleeing Huguenots from a Louvre balcony gets checked against the historical record, found to rest on a balcony that didn't exist yet, and left out entirely rather than kept for its narrative punch. A popular history book that routinely declines its own best anecdotes because they don't hold up is rarer than it should be, and it's the single strongest evidence the book has, so far, that its stated commitment to fairness is a working method and not a mission statement.

The Cost of the Register

The reservation is not about quality, it's about balance, and it's worth stating plainly rather than softening it into a caveat. Twenty-seven chapters have now covered, in order: gospel chaos, an excommunication, doctrinal committee politics, a fallen empire run by an accidental theocracy, a corpse propped on a throne and put on trial, twenty-four popes in ninety-three years of open corruption, and then, without a break, five straight chapters of massacre, torture, fabricated heresy, and a pope celebrating the murder of thousands in oil paint. Chapter 1 planted real counterweight early, Phoebe and Prisca and Junia named and credited, an agape feast where a stranger got fed without a rulebook telling anyone to feed him, Ignatius walking toward the arena writing that he was God's wheat rather than raging at his captors. That counterweight has not had much company since. The mystics the book has already promised, Julian of Norwich, Teresa of Avila, the honest doubters who make the case that institutions and faith are not the same thing, don't arrive until Part Six, which on the current outline is more than thirty chapters away. A reader who stops at Chapter 27, which is where the book currently ends, has been given a great deal of evidence that power corrupts religion and almost none yet that anything survived it. The book knows this. Its own notes describe the mystics section as the deliberate counter-narrative the corruption chapters are building toward. Knowing it and having written it are different things, and right now the book is still asking for a great deal of trust on the strength of a promise rather than a payoff.

None of that is a reason to doubt the payoff arrives. It's a reason to notice that this is currently a book making a very serious bet on how long a reader will sit with unrelieved institutional violence before the other half of its own thesis shows up to earn its keep. Twenty-seven chapters is long enough to have proven the writing can carry real historical weight without cheapening it. It is not yet long enough to have proven the whole architecture holds.
