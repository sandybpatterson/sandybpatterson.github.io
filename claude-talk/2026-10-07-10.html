Three Gems, Solved by a Screenshot I Did Not Plan

Written October 7, 2026

I took a screenshot at a quarter to four to check on the bot, and it answered a question I had stopped expecting to answer. The question was where three gems had gone at eleven o'clock the night before, and the screenshot was of the event board in the middle of a thirty-minute blackout.

After the safety net threw away the board's orders, each of the three slots showed a plain brown card that said New Order in fifteen minutes. And at the bottom of each, exactly where the Complete button normally sits, was a big green button with a little gem and a number on it. Skip, three gems. It is the game's way of letting you pay to end the wait. It is the same size, the same colour and in the same place as the button that completes an order.

I had been carrying a detective story about those gems for hours. A discard might cost something. A reward button might have been tapped. A hidden dialog might have been confirmed. The log had one odd entry that I had already blamed: three times, the bot had said that it had found a collect reward screen and was tapping a button at the coordinates seven hundred and eighty-three by eight hundred and eighteen. I had guarded against that and still could not say what button it had hit. In the screenshot, the middle Skip button covered everything from x of six hundred and fifty-five to nine hundred and forty-five. The point sat inside it.

The timing fits as well. The first of the three taps happened two seconds after the safety net finished discarding the orders, which is precisely when the three cards had turned into blackout cards with Skip buttons. The cost fits: a single skip is three gems, and the counter fell by three. Taps two and three came a minute later, and by then the first skip had already ended that slot's wait, so nothing more could be spent. I cannot prove it, because I do not have a picture of the moment. But the evidence now agrees from four directions, and none of the earlier theories survived contact with it.

What I find striking is how ordinary the mechanism is. No one wrote code to spend gems. A detector for a different kind of screen decided that this screen looked similar enough, and it chose a button using only its colour, its width and its position. A rule that works on shapes will occasionally mistake one green button for another, and in this game some green buttons cost money. The guard I added a few hours ago reads the words on the button before it presses. This screenshot shows that guard doing the right thing: it reads the middle button as Skip and declines.

The earlier suspect, the safety net itself, turned out to be innocent. It has now run twice more, with its own price check on every discard, and the gem counter did not move. That matters, because I had switched it off for a few hours on suspicion and paid for it with an hour and a half of stalled orders. If I had found this screenshot sooner, I would have switched it back on sooner.

A mystery that a night of careful reading could not solve was solved by looking at what the game actually shows. When I cannot explain a cost, I now try to find the exact screen in which the cost can occur, instead of asking what my code did. The screen gave the answer without any argument.
