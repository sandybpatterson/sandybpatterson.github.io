The Rule That Was Right for One and Wrong for Ten

Written October 6, 2026

Ten minutes after the planner went live, it found its first real test. The middle order card on the event board wanted ten gold bottles for sixty points. The old bot would have asked for one. The new planner read the ten correctly, two digits and all, and logged that the card was short ten and was asking for all ten. Then it hit a rule I wrote a day earlier, and the rule undid the whole point.

The earlier rule was about collected batches. Sometimes the bot arrives at a station and a finished batch is sitting in the way, so it has to tap the screen to collect it before the panel opens. The danger is that the collected batch might be exactly what the order needed, and cooking another would waste ingredients. So the rule says: after collecting, look at the card's stock, and if it is anything above zero, stop and let the next pass re-read the board. That is a sensible guard when you want one item. When you want ten, a stock of one proves nothing except that you still need nine.

This is the kind of bug that only shows up when two good features meet. The planner changed what the bot wants, and the guard still assumed the old answer. Neither was wrong alone. So the fix is small: if the stock is above zero but below what is wanted, keep going and ask for the difference. If the stock reaches the wanted amount, or the screen only says two or more and the want is bigger than that, stop exactly as before. I tested it on a real saved station panel whose card showed a stock of one. Wanting one stops, wanting ten continues for nine, wanting two continues for one.

While the planner was running I also looked at the board itself, and it gave me a few ideas worth writing down. The event bar showed six hundred and three out of eight hundred points with about twenty-one hours left. Each order card pays around sixty points, so the real question is which cards give the most points for the least waiting. The bot cannot answer that yet, because it does not read the reward numbers. Training the reward digits is a bounded job, and it would let the bot work the best cards first.

A second idea is to stop paying for visits that learn nothing. About one visit in eight finds a station still cooking, which costs half a minute of travel to see a busy queue. The bot knows the cook time when it queues something, so it could remember when the batch is due and skip the trip until then. A third is smaller: at startup the bot waited sixty-five seconds because it did not recognize the open event board as the game being ready. And the biggest idea is for the next event in two weeks, a recipe learner that reads cook times and matches ingredient pictures by itself, so a new event needs a button press instead of a day of training.

None of those ideas is built yet, and the collected batch fix is staged and tested but waiting for the one-click file to apply it. What I like about this stretch is how fast the live log paid for itself. A rule that looked finished on a quiet day was wrong within ten minutes of a busier one.
