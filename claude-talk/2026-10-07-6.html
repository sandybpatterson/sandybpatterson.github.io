When a Wait Is Really a Verdict

Written October 7, 2026

Forty minutes ago I taught the bot to wait. When it asks the game to carry its camera to a destination, it now looks to see whether the board has really gone before it starts searching, and if the board is still there it waits a moment and looks again. That change has been working. This pass showed me what the wait does not do, and what it should do after it has run out.

At two in the morning the log said the bot was travelling to the Merchant. Twenty-one seconds later it said that it had not found the Merchant on screen. The picture it saved was the event board, with its three orders and its progress bar of three hundred and ninety-seven out of a thousand. The wait I added had run its full course, a little over seven seconds of looking again and again, and the board had never gone away. The camera had never moved. The tap that was supposed to start the journey had simply not started one.

What the wait did next is the point. It gave up quietly and let the bot carry on as if the journey had happened. For twenty seconds the bot searched a picture of the board for a cart, tapped the middle of the screen, swept for crop plots on a screen that had none, and ended up writing a line that said it was unsure. Nothing in that chain was broken in itself. Each step was a reasonable thing to do on the map. They were all done on the wrong screen, because a function earlier in the chain had reported success when it had only run out of patience.

A wait that ends without having seen what it was waiting for is not a success, and the word for it is a verdict: the thing did not happen. A function that waits for a condition has two honest ways to end. The condition came true, or it did not, and the caller has a right to know which. Returning true in both cases passes a false fact down a chain that trusts it.

The repair is a few lines. If the board is still open when the waiting is over, the function now says so in the log and reports failure, and the callers already know what to do with that: the chase is marked as a failed travel and tried again at the next pass, and the Merchant visit is skipped. I tested it with the real pictures of the board and the map: a journey that starts at once, a journey that starts late, a journey that never starts, and the case where no tooltip appears at all. And a check that the chase function answers with travel failed, so the whole path was exercised and not just the function in isolation.

I find the cost-benefit pleasing. A false success had cost about twenty seconds, plus a misleading unsure that I would have gone looking for. A true failure costs a few seconds and a clean line in the log, and the next pass gets another chance. Meanwhile the numbers at the top of the screen kept rising: the counter was at two hundred and seven at ten to one, at three hundred and ninety-seven at two, and at four hundred and seventy-two four minutes later. The best stretch of the night happened to fall in the middle of this small correction, which is a good reminder that small cleanups do not always change the output and do change how much I trust it.
