The Triple Structure of Chapter Four, and Where It Bends

Written August 31, 2026

Chapter Four's real argument isn't stated once — it's performed three times. Anchoring, availability, and representativeness get introduced in human terms across the first two sections, mapped onto how a language model behaves in the third, and shown killing people, wasting hundreds of millions of dollars, and biasing courtrooms in the fourth. That's not decoration; it's the book's whole thesis — the machine is a mirror — enacted structurally rather than just argued for. The same three shortcuts really do show up three times, in three registers, and the chapter's shape recreates that recurrence instead of merely describing it. It's the strongest thing about the chapter, and worth naming before getting into what could be tightened, because everything below is in service of that structure, not against it.

Where the rhythm slips

Sections 4.1 through 4.3 introduce and revisit the three shortcuts in the same order every time: anchoring, then availability, then representativeness. By the third pass — Section 4.3, mapping them onto AI — a reader has absorbed that order as the chapter's rhythm, almost a reflex. Section 4.4 breaks it: Case One is availability, Case Two is anchoring, Case Three is representativeness. There's a plausible reason for the new order — the cases escalate by stakes, a spacecraft, then a single patient, then a whole justice system, which is its own defensible logic — but nothing in the text signals that the reordering is deliberate, so it just reads as a break in a pattern the chapter spent two sections training the reader to expect. Either reordering 4.4 to match, or adding one clause that acknowledges the shift, would resolve it without losing the escalation.

The opening hook has a smaller version of the same issue — a fidelity gap rather than an ordering one. The real 1974 experiment anchors participants to a number they did not choose, generated by a wheel rigged to stop on ten or sixty-five. The chapter's version asks the reader to pick their own number, freely, between one and a hundred. That's a different, and slightly weaker, demonstration of the same effect: a number you picked yourself isn't as clean a stand-in for "an irrelevant input imposed from outside" as a number a rigged wheel handed you. A reader who notices the substitution — and the kind of reader this book is for is exactly the kind who'd notice — might feel the demo doesn't quite prove what it claims to. An easy fix that keeps the interactivity: anchor the reader's number to something already outside their control, like the last two digits of their phone number, rather than a free pick.

The one place real and constructed sit too close together

Case One, the Mars Climate Orbiter, and Case Three, COMPAS, are both real, documented, sourced incidents with names, dates, and numbers attached. Case Two — the anxious patient whose chest pain gets read through an anchored diagnosis — is an explicitly framed composite, correctly introduced with "picture a woman" rather than a fabricated specific date and location, which the notes file confirms was a deliberate fix, and the right one. But it sits under the same "Case Two" heading, in the same present-tense narrative voice, at the same paragraph length as the two real cases on either side of it. The signal that it's illustrative rather than documented is there, but it's one soft opening phrase against two hard, sourced neighbors. A chapter that's explicitly about the danger of pattern-matching probably wants its own case studies to be unmistakably distinguishable at a glance — a reader skimming for the shape of an argument, exactly the System 1 read this chapter warns about, could come away having pattern-matched all three cases as equally real.

Where System 1 and System 2 actually land

The framework that names what's actually happening in all three heuristics — Kahneman's fast system and slow system — doesn't show up until Section 4.5, after the reader has already been through the human examples, the AI mapping, and all three high-stakes cases. That's a legitimate choice: examples first, mechanism named after, so the naming lands as a synthesis rather than a lecture. But it means Sections 4.2 through 4.4 are read without the vocabulary that would let a reader explicitly frame each example as "System 1 doing this" while it's happening, rather than only in retrospect. Worth a deliberate choice rather than an accident: either keep it as the payoff it currently is, or move a short, one-paragraph version of System 1 and System 2 up into Section 4.1 or 4.2, so the AI mapping and the three cases can be read through that lens the first time instead of the second.

None of the above touches the chapter's actual content — that's a separate question, and worth checking on its own terms, especially in a book about not trusting things that sound right. Cross-referencing the chapter's specific claims against its own notes file turned up three things.

Checking the facts, not just the shape

The most significant one sits in the Eickhoff study anchoring Section 4.5's close. The chapter attributes it to "a seven-person research team led by Carsten Eickhoff at the University of Tübingen," and the notes file's own citation entry lists the lead author as "Eickhoff, C." But the actual paper behind that DOI — BMJ Open 2026, volume 16, article e112695 — is led by Nicholas Tiller, with co-authors including Marcon and Zenone, involving Timothy Caulfield's group at the University of Alberta, not Carsten Eickhoff at Tübingen at all. Eickhoff is a real person, a professor of medical data science at Tübingen who works in exactly this area, which may be how the two got crossed in the first place. The study's actual findings hold up: five chatbots tested, roughly half the answers rated problematic, the 32-versus-7-percent split between open-ended and closed questions, all checking out against the real paper. Only the byline is wrong — and it's wrong in both the chapter's prose and its citation entry, not just one or the other, so both need the fix. The "seven-person team" detail is worth re-verifying specifically, or dropping — nothing in the sources confirms a headcount, and it's exactly the kind of invented specificity earlier passes on this chapter already caught and removed elsewhere.

The second is smaller. The Linda Problem exhibit box in Section 4.2 is headed "Kahneman & Tversky, 1983." The chapter's own notes file cites the same paper correctly, as Tversky first — which is also the real published author order. The exhibit heading has it backwards. It's a plausible slip given the very next citation in that same section, the representativeness paper from 1973, genuinely does list Kahneman first — an easy pair to cross.

The third is softer, closer to a phrasing choice than an error. The opening section describes the wheel-of-fortune anchoring experiment as happening "in the early 1970s." The chapter's own citation for that exact experiment is dated 1974 — a real, specific, correctly dated source sitting one document away from where the chapter wrote a vaguer one.

Everything else checked out cleanly: the Mars Climate Orbiter's cost, date, and units-mismatch story; the ProPublica/COMPAS numbers and its "nearly twice as likely" framing, which turns out to be ProPublica's own phrase rather than a paraphrase; and the Linda problem's eighty-five percent figure, which matches the original study exactly.
