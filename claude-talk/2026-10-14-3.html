Your Family Tree: What We Know For Sure

Written October 14, 2026

Prepared October 2026 for April.

Trenton and I have spent the past few days going through the family tree one person at a time. We checked each link against a real record: censuses, death records, draft cards, Social Security files, and handwritten parish registers from Poland. Some names on the tree turned out to be strangers and have been removed. What's left is a smaller tree, but every person in it has a record behind them.

Here's your mother's side of the family. Your father's side, the Fipps family, comes next.

Your mother's family at a glance

Your mom, Catherine Ann Spaugh, was the daughter of Wilfred "Bill" Brien and Florence Rose Byszewski. Those two brought together two very different worlds.

Bill's side is Norwegian, German and Dutch farm families who settled in Minnesota and Wisconsin in the 1800s.

Florence's side is entirely Polish, from a small cluster of villages northeast of Warsaw. They came to Minneapolis in the 1910s and 1920s.

Bill Brien's family: Minnesota farm country

Bill's parents were George Brien and Myrtle Drake.

George's father, Eddie Brien (born 1871, Nicollet County, Minnesota), was Bill's grandfather. He was born a Larson, and the family took the name Brien later. Eddie's son Earl, Bill's uncle, wrote a handwritten family history saying Eddie ended up in "the TB hospital in Western N.D." That's confirmed: Eddie died in 1936 at San Haven, North Dakota's tuberculosis sanatorium.

George's mother, Mary Gunderson (born 1880, McLeod County, Minnesota), was the daughter of Ole Gunderson, born 1851 in Kneppe, Norway. Ole's parents, John Gunderson and Inger Olsdatter, appear in the 1870 census in Carver County, Minnesota. Mary's mother, Caroline Coenen, was born in 1855 in Holland. Her family were Dutch Catholics from the Limburg region.

Bill's mother, Myrtle Drake (born 1902, Mille Lacs County), was the daughter of Gustave Drache, born 7 February 1862 in Dodge County, Wisconsin, to German immigrants from Brandenburg, and Amanda Rusch, born 1868 in Wisconsin, who died in 1942 in Hubbard County, Minnesota. Five censuses, her death index entry and her grave all confirm Amanda.

Amanda's own mother, Wilhelmina Beilke, was a Pomeranian German immigrant. She died in April 1875 at about 27, when Amanda was only six or seven. Her grave is in Herman Center, Wisconsin. The 1880 census shows Amanda living with her father William Rusch, a dry goods merchant, along with her stepmother Carolina, her brother and sister, and her new half-siblings.

Florence Byszewski's family: Poland under the Tsar

This is the part of your tree that changed the most, and it's now the best-documented branch.

Florence was born in Minneapolis on 15 July 1926. Her parents were Adam Byszewski and Rose (Rozalia) Malinowska. For years the tree had the wrong Rose: a different woman with the same name. The mix-up came from two towns named Warren, one in Ohio and one in Michigan. That's been fixed, and the real Rose is confirmed by her baptism record, two censuses, and Florence's own Social Security application.

Rose was born on 10 April 1884 in the village of Budziszewo-Śledziony, in Andrzejewo parish. That part of Poland belonged to the Russian Empire then, so her baptism is written in Russian and gives two dates, because Russia used a different calendar. We have a photo of the original page.

Rose married Adam on 14 February 1904, at age 19, in the Andrzejewo church. Both families were living in the village of Łętownica. Adam was under 21, so his mother came to the church and gave her consent out loud. Only his mother is named. His father is never mentioned.

Then the parish records show something nobody in the family seems to have known. Rose and Adam had a whole family in Poland before Florence.

Stanisława, born 1904.

Aleksander, born 1906.

Zofia, born 1908, who died as a baby in 1909.

Stefan, born 1911.

Anna, born 1914.

Adam left for America around 1913. His 1917 World War I draft card says he was supporting "wife and two child in Russia." Rose didn't join him until 1921, after the war, and Florence was born five years later. Rose was 42. Florence was the youngest of at least six children, and the only one born in America.

Rose's parents and grandparents: the Malinowskis

We traced Rose's family back three more generations using the original parish books.

Paweł Malinowski, Rose's father, was born in 1852 in the village of Smolechy, in Jasienica parish.

Apolonia Kępista, Rose's mother, was born in 1853 in the next village, Chmielewo. She died in 1930.

They married in 1877. Over the next fourteen years they moved between villages around Andrzejewo as they had children.

Paweł's parents were Ludwik Malinowski (born 11 October 1829) and Marianna Bulak (born 1 April 1828). Their wedding record from 10 February 1850 survives, and it's wonderfully detailed. Ludwik was a 20-year-old hired servant. Marianna was a 22-year-old day laborer. Her father Wojciech had already died. Ludwik's parents, Jan Malinowski and Marianna Piotrak, were in the church that day. No one in the wedding party could write their name.

That takes your Polish line back to people born around 1800: about seven generations from you.

Adam's mother: a probable find

Adam's mother was Anna Byszewska. In the next parish over we found an Anna Biszewska. She was born in 1851 in the village of Ruskołęki and never married. She had a son, Józef, in 1877 and a daughter, Wanda, in 1880, with no father named. Wanda died at age three. Anna died in 1926.

It's very likely she's Adam's mother, which would make Józef his older half-brother. We've marked it "probable" until we find Adam's own baptism record. It's in a parish whose books haven't been indexed yet.

What makes your tree interesting

Five countries: Norway, Germany (Prussia), the Netherlands, Poland (under the Russian Empire), and the United States.

Real paper trails back to 1800, including photos of the original handwritten pages from the Polish parish books, now saved in the tree.

A family split by World War I: Rose raised their children in Poland while Adam worked in Minneapolis, for eight years, before they were reunited.

Family memory that holds up: Earl Brien's handwritten notes about Eddie and the TB hospital match the official death record exactly.

What we're working on next

One. Your father's side: the Fipps family. Nothing there has been checked yet. The tree traces it through Arkansas, Oklahoma, Tennessee and back to Pennsylvania around 1700, but it needs record-by-record checking the same way your mother's side got.

Two. Adam's own baptism record, to confirm Anna as his mother.

Three. Gustave Drache's parents in Wisconsin, and Eddie Brien's real father, the Larson name.

Four. Wilhelmina Beilke's and Caroline Coenen's families in Germany and Holland.

If you have photos, letters, funeral cards or family stories about any of these people, especially Florence, Rose, Adam or Bill, send them to Trenton. Family memories like Earl's notes are often what cracks a hard case.
