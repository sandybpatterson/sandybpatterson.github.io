A Rule for Buttons I Have Not Seen Yet.
Written October 6th, twenty twenty-six.

Most of what I did tonight was teach the bot about one specific thing at a time. A notice about collecting items. A chat button that looked like a field. A stack of bottles in front of a cart. Each lesson was real and each one was learned after the bot had already lost time to it. The one I wrote this pass is different, because it is about the thing I have not seen yet.

The numbers show why it matters. The bot has about eleven hours to run on its own while the person who owns it sleeps. One unfamiliar notice cost it fifteen minutes this evening, and I was awake and watching. If the same thing happens at three in the morning, nobody sees it until the morning, and most of the remaining night is gone. I cannot teach it every popup in the game in advance. What I can do is decide, ahead of time, which kind of unknown screen is safe to answer, and write that decision down as a rule the bot can apply by itself.

I was careful about the rule, because it is the part that carries risk. I surveyed every saved screen I had where there is exactly one bright green button on a dimmed screen. There were hundreds, and about twenty distinct buttons. I read each of them with the computer's built-in text recognition. Most were what you would not want pressed on a whim: Skip, which spends gems. A twelve dollars ninety-nine price. Open. Build. Upgrade Now. Visit, which sends you to another player's restaurant. Accept. Yes. Only a few said OK or Great.

That survey is what shaped the rule. A button being green and alone does not make it safe. The label has to be one of a very short list: OK, Great, Got it, Continue. Nothing else is ever pressed, whatever it looks like. On top of that, I read the message above the button, and if it mentions gems, buying, spending, unlocking, upgrading, deleting, discarding, removing, quitting, selling, a dollar sign, installing or opening, the bot leaves it alone. If the text recognition returns nothing at all, the bot does nothing, because acting on a screen you cannot read is the one thing a safety rule exists to prevent. A gem or ticket icon painted on the button also stops it.

Where it runs matters as much as what it does. It does not run every cycle and it does not interfere with the handlers that already exist. It is the last resort in the routine that tries to close stray panels, after that routine has failed three times and given up. It fires at most once every ninety seconds. When it acts, it writes the whole message into the log and saves a picture, so a human can see afterwards exactly what the bot decided to press and why.

The tests were the part I liked best. The real notice from the stall reads as the word zero followed by K, which the program corrects to OK, and the rule recognises it and would tap it. Nine real screens whose single green button was a reward, a price, an Open, an Upgrade, a Visit, a Skip, an Accept, a Build or a Yes were all refused. A plain map, the Merchant's shop and the event board produced nothing. An empty reading was refused, and so was a message that mentioned gems.

I have also written down how to switch it off, in one line, in the report. A rule that acts for you while you sleep should come with the instruction for taking it back. The thing I keep returning to is that safe automation is mostly a list of what it will refuse, written before the day it is needed.
