The Water That Refuses to Freeze.
Written September 12th, twenty twenty-six.

You were right, and I should have found this the first time. My last search stayed narrowly inside the Pollack-versus-diffusiophoresis argument, which meant I walked right past a different piece of water news that's actually a better fit for the book, and newer: a paper on water's glass transition under nanoscale confinement, covered on phys dot org this month, out of Nature Communications.

What They Actually Found

Here's the problem the paper solves. Cool ordinary water down and it crystallizes into ice almost immediately, which means nobody has ever been able to watch water do the other thing a liquid can do when it's cooled: turn into a glass, an amorphous solid with no crystal structure at all, the way window glass or hard candy locks in place without ever forming an ordered lattice. Water forms ice too fast to catch that other transition in the act, at least in bulk. The trick this team used was confinement. They trapped water in sub-nanometer layers sandwiched between self-assembling lipid membranes, using a fatty alcohol called phytantriol, thin enough and mobile enough on the walls that the water inside genuinely never freezes. And then threw an unusually complete toolkit at it: neutron scattering, synchrotron terahertz spectroscopy, solid-state NMR, X-ray scattering, and molecular dynamics simulations, to actually watch what happens instead. What happens is real and well resolved now: the water slows down continuously as it cools, across six full orders of magnitude in timescale, and settles into a genuine glassy state somewhere in the range of about minus 74 to minus 20 degrees Celsius depending on which measurement you're reading. No ice. Just water getting slower and slower until it stops being a liquid without ever becoming a crystal.

Why This One Actually Belongs Next to Your Book

This isn't the exclusion zone, and I want to be direct that it doesn't touch the Pollack or diffusiophoresis argument at all. It's a different confirmed effect in a different subfield, closer to cryophysics and soft matter than to Pollack's bioengineering lab. But it's making almost exactly the same argument your whole book is built on, from a completely independent direction: get water close enough to a surface, confine it tightly enough, and it stops behaving like the water in the glass on your kitchen table. Chapter 11 already makes the case that this matters inside living things, not just in a physics lab, and this is precisely the kind of confinement biology actually uses. Cells are full of water squeezed into gaps this small, and the paper's own authors point at cryopreservation and food freezing as the direct practical stakes, which means the same nanoscale trick that let physicists finally see water refuse to freeze is also a real description of what's happening inside a cell that survives being frozen, or doesn't.

One honest flag before I let this go: the phys dot org writeup is dated this month, but the underlying paper itself may have posted earlier in the year, so "brand new" applies more confidently to the news coverage than to the research itself. That's a small thing to verify before it goes anywhere near the book, not a reason to doubt the finding. But it's exactly the kind of adjacent, independently confirmed strangeness, water at an interface doing something bulk water can't, that a bonus chapter built around "what's changed since twenty twenty-six" would actually want to include alongside whatever you decide about the exclusion zone debate.
