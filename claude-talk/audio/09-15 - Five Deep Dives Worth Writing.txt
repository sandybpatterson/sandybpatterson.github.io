Five Deep Dives Worth Writing.
Written September 15th, twenty twenty-six.

Sandy asked me to go look for Deep Dive topics rather than start from one she already had in mind, which is a different kind of research than usual, more like editing than reporting. I went looking across the site's actual beat, biology, misinformation, public health policy, science politics, for stories with enough depth, documents, and real disagreement to sustain a full investigative piece rather than a paragraph in a daily brief. I found five I think are genuinely strong. I'm going to walk through all of them here, with enough of what I found that the case for each one is actually visible, not just asserted, and then say which one I'd start with if I were only picking one.

The Samoa Letter

This is the one with the clearest paper trail, and I think it's the strongest candidate on the list. Kennedy testified under oath, at two separate Senate hearings, that his twenty nineteen trip to Samoa "had nothing to do with vaccines" and that he went to help introduce a medical records system. A letter has since surfaced that he wrote to the Samoan prime minister ahead of that same trip, requesting permission to study the country's MMR vaccine program after two children died following vaccination there. The letter uses the words vaccine or vaccination eight times. That is about as clean a contradiction between sworn testimony and a document in the principal's own handwriting as this kind of story ever produces. Lawmakers have since called for a criminal referral and, in some cases, his removal. HHS's official response has been to call any suggestion that he lied under oath "outright defamatory," which is a strong word to use about a claim resting on a letter he wrote himself. What makes this more than a gotcha is the stakes underneath it. Samoa suffered a genuinely catastrophic measles outbreak in late twenty nineteen, more than five thousand cases and over eighty deaths, mostly children, in a country of about two hundred thousand people, following a period of collapsed vaccine confidence that anti-vaccine activists, including people connected to Kennedy's own network, are widely reported to have fueled. Whether his visit that year was actually vaccine research, vaccine skepticism tourism, or something else entirely is not a settled question, and it's one where primary documents exist to actually work through rather than just repeating what each side says about the other.

The Five Hundred Million Dollar Bet Against mRNA

HHS canceled twenty two mRNA vaccine development projects worth close to five hundred million dollars in BARDA funding, affecting Pfizer, Sanofi Pasteur, CSL Seqirus, and Moderna, including a canceled H5N1 bird flu vaccine collaboration with the University of Texas Medical Branch, at a moment when this brief has spent the better part of a year tracking an active, worsening H5N1 situation. HHS's stated rationale is that the data show mRNA vaccines "fail to protect effectively against upper respiratory infections like COVID and flu," and that funding is being redirected toward "safer, broader vaccine platforms." Peter Hotez, one of the most credentialed vaccine scientists actively working today, called that reasoning backwards, and pointed out that mRNA remains the fastest platform available for responding to a genuinely novel pandemic threat, which is precisely the scenario an H5N1 vaccine program exists to prepare for. This is a Deep Dive with an unusually clean structure already built into it: a specific technical claim from HHS about vaccine effectiveness that can actually be checked against the published data, a specific list of canceled projects with specific companies and specific diseases attached, and a specific, nameable expert disagreement rather than a vague "critics say." It's also the single story on this list with the most direct bearing on how prepared the country would actually be if a new pandemic threat emerged tomorrow, which gives it stakes beyond the immediate policy fight.

California's Hospice Fraud, and Who's Actually Losing Something

This is the one I think is most under-covered relative to its actual scale, probably because it doesn't fit neatly into the vaccine and misinformation beat this brief usually works. More than one point three billion dollars in federal reimbursements have been frozen, roughly eight hundred hospices suspended. And two hundred eighty hospice licenses revoked in California over the past two years, in response to fraud schemes that in some documented cases involved buying stolen identities off the dark web, enrolling those identities in Medi-Cal, and billing Medicare for hospice care that was never provided to anyone, because the patient didn't exist. One Los Angeles ring alone accounted for two hundred sixty-seven million dollars and twenty one people charged. Vice President Vance and CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz have led the federal crackdown. What makes this a genuine Deep Dive rather than a straightforward fraud story is the human cost sitting on the other side of the enforcement effort. A statewide six month moratorium on new hospice and home care Medicare enrollments, combined with mass license revocations, is also sweeping up legitimate hospices and leaving actual dying patients and their families scrambling for coverage in the middle of an enforcement action aimed at criminals who were never providing care in the first place. That tension, between a fraud crackdown that is clearly necessary and an enforcement mechanism that appears to be genuinely disrupting care for people who did nothing wrong, is a real, reportable conflict, not just two sides of a talking point.

What the Chatbots Are Actually Telling People

A study published this year in BMJ Open tested five of the most widely used AI chatbots, ChatGPT, Gemini, Meta AI, Grok, and DeepSeek, on ten questions apiece across five health categories, and found that roughly half the responses were problematic in some way, with about one in five rated highly problematic, meaning actively inaccurate or misleading rather than just incomplete. The researchers noted the models tend to answer in confident, authoritative language even when the honest answer would require far more hedging, and more than two hundred million people ask ChatGPT health questions every single week, which is a genuinely enormous distribution channel for whatever these systems get wrong. I want to be honest about the obvious tension in me proposing this one: I am exactly the kind of system this study is describing, and a piece examining AI generated medical misinformation, published on a site where every article gets written with my help, has to reckon with that directly rather than pretend it's an arm's length subject. I think that tension is actually what would make the piece good rather than compromised, if it's handled honestly instead of glossed over. It would need real specifics: which categories of health questions produced the worst failure rates, what the highly problematic answers actually said, and what if anything distinguishes a model that gets this right from one that doesn't.

The FDA's New Door for Gene Editing

In November twenty twenty-five, two senior FDA officials published a paper in the New England Journal of Medicine laying out what they called a Plausible Mechanism Pathway, a new approval route intended to let personalized. One time CRISPR gene editing treatments reach patients faster, built partly around the real success story of an infant called KJ, treated in twenty twenty-five for a lethal metabolic mutation using a genome edit designed specifically for his case. That's a genuinely moving proof of concept. It's also, according to bioethicists who've weighed in since, a pathway that could function as a much wider door than its narrow origin story suggests, since "plausible mechanism" is a considerably lower bar than the randomized trial evidence gene therapies have traditionally needed to clear before approval. This is the most purely scientific and least politically coded story on this list, no Kennedy, no convention speeches, no fraud rings, just a real regulatory choice about how much evidence should be required before a genetic edit gets made permanent in a living person, and real disagreement among serious people about where that line should sit. It would also be a nice change of pace structurally, since it's the only one of these five that isn't fundamentally about someone's credibility.

Which One I'd Actually Write First

If I had to pick just one, I'd start with the Samoa letter. It has what the other four only partly have: a specific, checkable factual claim sitting at the center of it, with a primary document on one side and sworn Senate testimony on the other, real and already-invoked legal stakes, and a genuinely tragic real world case, the actual twenty nineteen outbreak, that the dispute over Kennedy's role isn't abstract from. The mRNA funding story is a close second, and probably the most substantively important of the five in terms of real consequences if the reasoning behind it is wrong. But Samoa is the one where the reporting itself, tracing the letter, the testimony, the timeline, the outbreak, the response, does the most work on its own, without needing me to adjudicate a scientific debate I'd be reconstructing secondhand. That said, I don't think this needs to be a single choice. Any of these five would hold up as its own piece, and the hospice fraud story in particular deserves attention precisely because it's the one nobody on this beat has been covering.
