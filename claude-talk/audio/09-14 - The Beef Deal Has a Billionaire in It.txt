The Beef Deal Has a Billionaire in It.
Written September 14th, twenty twenty-six.

Sandy asked me to dig further into the beef import story before we turn it into a Deep Dive, and I want to lay out everything I found here first, in full, before it gets compressed down into something publishable. A Deep Dive has to hold a line and commit to a structure. This doesn't. This is me thinking out loud with all the receipts on the table, including the parts I'm not sure belong in the final piece, and including some real speculation about where this goes next, clearly marked as speculation rather than reporting.

The short version of where I landed: the "pink slime" framing that got us into this topic turns out to be more accurate than I expected going in, but it's also the least interesting thing about the story. The actually interesting thing is that a sitting president met privately with a Brazilian meatpacking billionaire the day before announcing a policy that benefits that billionaire's company, and almost nobody outside agricultural trade press has connected those two facts out loud yet.

How Beef Got This Expensive In the First Place

None of this happens without the underlying price problem, so it's worth sitting with the numbers. The U.S. cattle herd entered twenty twenty-six at eighty-six point two million head, the smallest national inventory in seventy five years. Beef cow inventory specifically, the animals that actually produce the next generation of calves, sat at twenty-eight point five million head as of July 1st, the lowest figure since USDA started keeping that particular series in nineteen seventy-one. The proximate cause is years of drought across the West and the Plains that depleted grassland faster than ranchers could adapt, forcing many of them to sell off breeding cows early just to survive, which is exactly the wrong animals to be selling if you ever want the herd to recover. A beef cow takes roughly two years from breeding decision to a calf reaching slaughter weight, so this is not a problem that responds to policy on any fast timeline. Every serious agricultural economist I found puts real herd rebuilding, meaning prices actually coming back down because there's more cattle rather than because demand collapsed, somewhere in twenty twenty-eight or twenty twenty-nine at the earliest. There was one mildly hopeful data point, heifer retention (young female cattle kept back for breeding rather than sold for meat) was up about three percent year over year, which is the first visible sign ranchers are starting to try to rebuild. But the Kansas City Fed's own research bluntly described the rebuilding effort as fragile at best. This is the actual, structural, multi year problem that the administration is trying to paper over with a ninety day trade policy, and I think that mismatch in timescale is worth keeping in view for everything that follows.

Against that backdrop, retail beef prices hit records in twenty twenty-six, up more than twelve percent year over year by some measures even after two previous rounds of import expansion, an October twenty twenty-five move that roughly quadrupled Argentine beef imports and a November twenty twenty-five removal of the 40 percent punitive tariff that had been sitting on Brazilian beef. Neither of those earlier moves meaningfully brought prices down, which the administration's own officials have more or less acknowledged, and which is the immediate context for why September's much larger action happened at all.

The Actual Order, or Orders

There isn't one single executive order here, there's a cluster of them across about a month, and it's worth being precise about which one does what, because they get conflated in casual coverage.

In August, Trump signed "Further Ensuring Affordable Beef for the American Consumer," which set the stage. Then on September 4th and 5th, a follow up package: one order pausing tariffs and expanding the quota to allow 300,000 metric tons, about six sixty-one million pounds, of tariff free ground beef and lean beef trimmings into the country from Brazil and Argentina over 90 days, capped at 100,000 tons a month, beginning September 1st. Trump personally promised the imported beef would sell at roughly 25 percent below prevailing market prices. A second, closely linked order the same week directs Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins to spend 90 days reviewing what legal authority the administration actually has to bring back mandatory country of origin labeling on beef, explicitly framed by administration officials as a response to the immediate backlash from ranchers. A third order that week, mostly separate in substance, focuses on domestic meat processing rather than imports at all, directing USDA to widen three existing programs, the State Meat and Poultry Inspection Program, Cooperative Interstate Shipment. And Talmadge Aiken, so more state inspected meat can cross state lines, and to streamline what the order calls overly prescriptive federal inspection paperwork. That third order is really a different story about domestic small processors, and I don't think it belongs folded into the imports piece even though the White House rolled them out in the same week for obvious messaging reasons.

Where "Pink Slime" Actually Fits

Here's the part that surprised me by holding up. The imported product at the center of the September order isn't whole cuts of steak, it's specifically lean beef trimmings, the same broad category of product that lean finely textured beef, LFTB, the thing the public nicknamed pink slime back in twenty twelve, belongs to. The mechanical reason is straightforward and actually makes sense on its own terms. American cattle are mostly grain finished, which produces fattier trim. To hit the fat ratio consumers expect in a package of ground beef, packers need to blend that fatty domestic trim against leaner trim from somewhere. Grass fed cattle, which describes most of the herds in Brazil and Argentina, naturally produce leaner trim. So importing lean trimmings to blend against fatty domestic trim isn't a scam or a trick, it's how ground beef has been made at industrial scale for decades. USDA's own research describes exactly this blending relationship.

The part that is genuinely a live controversy, not settled history, is disclosure. There is currently no mandatory country of origin labeling on beef in the United States at all, for imported whole cuts or for trimmings blended into ground beef, because the law that used to require it was repealed in twenty fifteen. The "Product of USA" claim that does exist is entirely voluntary. A packer blending imported Brazilian or Argentine trim into a package of ground beef simply does not use that claim, and nothing legally obligates them to say where the beef actually came from instead. So you end up with a real, non speculative fact: six sixty-one million pounds of imported lean trim is entering the American ground beef supply this fall, at a larger single volume than almost anything comparable in recent memory, and a shopper standing in front of the meat case has no reliable way to know it's there. That is structurally the same complaint that drove the original pink slime backlash, an invisible component of ground beef that consumers didn't know was there and would have wanted disclosed. The product category rhymes. The disclosure gap is identical. I think that's a legitimate, defensible frame for a Deep Dive rather than a cute rhetorical stretch, which is not what I expected to conclude when I started digging.

The Billionaire in the Room

This is the part I think is actually the spine of the story, and I want to lay it out carefully because the sourcing matters. The Wall Street Journal reported that the day before Trump announced the tariff pause, he met privately in the Oval Office with Joesley Batista, co-owner of JBS, the Brazilian meatpacking conglomerate and the single largest beneficiary of any policy that makes it cheaper to move Brazilian beef into the United States. According to that reporting, the two discussed lowering the roughly 26 percent import tax that had been sitting on Brazilian beef. The next day, Trump's public announcement proposed selling the newly tariff free imported beef at close to a 25 percent discount to market price, a suspiciously round number relative to the tariff figure that was reportedly discussed the day before.

Layered on top of the meeting itself, several other facts are independently documented and worth holding in mind together rather than separately. JBS is currently one of four major U.S. meatpackers under Justice Department investigation for possible anticompetitive conduct in the meat processing industry generally. Pilgrim's Pride, the poultry company JBS controls, contributed five million dollars to the Trump-Vance inaugural committee in January twenty twenty-five, the single largest reported inaugural donation from any one source. And Joesley Batista and his brother Wesley were fined by the Justice Department back in twenty twenty for violating the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, over a bribery scheme connected to expanding their U.S. operations. None of these facts individually proves that the tariff decision was corrupt or bought. People meet with presidents about policies that affect their businesses constantly, that's not illegal and it's barely even notable on its own. But the specific sequence, private meeting about a specific tariff number, followed within roughly twenty four hours by a public policy announcement matching that number almost exactly. Involving a company with an existing DOJ antitrust cloud over it and a documented history of using money to move U.S. policy, is the kind of pattern that agricultural trade press picked up immediately and that I think a wider audience genuinely hasn't seen laid out together yet. That gap, between how much attention this has gotten in outlets like Drovers and Pro Farmer versus how little it's gotten in general news coverage, is itself worth naming in the piece.

The Safety and Quality Complication

Running in parallel to all of this, and genuinely relevant to whether the imported beef is what Trump promised it would be when he called it "very clean" and "very good," is that the European Union suspended imports of Brazilian beef, poultry, and other animal products starting September 3rd, two days before the American tariff pause took effect. The EU's stated reason is specific and worth getting right rather than exaggerating: Brazil couldn't provide sufficient guarantees under the EU's antimicrobial use rules, specifically that its producers don't use antibiotics as growth promoters or reserve antibiotics that are needed for human medicine. Importantly, the regulation itself doesn't cite actual test results showing unsafe drug residue in Brazilian meat, this is a documentation and verification failure, not a confirmed contamination event. That distinction matters and I don't want to lose it in a more dramatic retelling. Still, the timing, an EU verification ban on the exact same country two days before the U.S. throws its own doors open wider to that country's beef, is the kind of coincidence that writes its own headline whether or not it deserves one.

There's a real underlying science question here too, separate from the Brazil specific dispute, that I think belongs in the piece as its own thread rather than folded into the political story. Recent twenty twenty-six research on antimicrobial resistance specifically flags imported retail beef and chicken as a documented pathway for introducing antibiotic resistant E. coli into new regions, essentially because meat production systems in different countries use antibiotics differently, and importing meat can mean importing whatever resistant bacteria came along with it. Separately, and more of a one off data point than a trend, Chinese customs rejected a 22 ton container of Argentine beef in March twenty twenty-six after lab tests found chloramphenicol, an antibiotic that's banned from food producing animals across most of the world because of documented human health risks, including a rare but serious bone marrow suppression. One rejected container from months ago and thousands of miles away doesn't tell you anything about the safety of this specific six sixty-one million pound program. But it does establish that residue testing on South American beef exports has caught real problems before, which is useful context for readers trying to calibrate how worried to actually be.

I also want to flag, because Wetwear exists partly to catch exactly this kind of thing, that there's a specific false claim already circulating that the piece should address head on rather than ignore. A 29,628 pound recall of Argentine beef, distributed in Texas and Florida by a Miami based importer, happened on August 7th, roughly three weeks before the tariff free program even started on September 1st. Online commentary has linked the two as though the new imports are already turning up unsafe, and Snopes and other fact checkers have traced the timeline and confirmed there's no connection, the recall predates the program by weeks and was issued for a routine reinspection failure, not a contamination finding. Repeating the correct timeline here does real work, both because it's true and because it's exactly the kind of thing that will keep circulating as a talking point on both sides of this fight if nobody nails it down clearly.

Why Industry Reaction Has Been Almost Uniformly Negative

What struck me researching the reaction is how little daylight there is between different cattle industry groups that don't normally agree with each other on much. The National Cattlemen's Beef Association, generally the more establishment, packer aligned of the major groups, condemned the move outright, saying flatly that no cow-calf producer in America asked for more imports. R-CALF USA, which represents independent ranchers and has historically been the more populist, anti consolidation voice in the industry, called it doubling down on a failed strategy and used the moment to push its long standing demand for exactly the mandatory labeling law the administration is now dangling as a 90 day review. The U.S. Cattlemen's Association president was the most quotable: "You don't put America first by putting U.S. cattle producers last... this move will weaken our markets and gamble with food safety. And multiple Republican senators from cattle states broke publicly with the administration, which is itself a data point, since intra party criticism this direct usually signals real constituent pressure rather than performative opposition.

The economic argument underneath all of this, made by several agricultural economists independently, is that the promised consumer savings are unlikely to fully reach shoppers even if the imports work exactly as designed. Large packers and retailers sit between the imported beef and the consumer, and margin capture at that level, rather than passthrough to the register, is the more likely outcome based on how similar import expansions have played out historically. If that turns out to be true, the policy would manage to simultaneously depress prices for domestic ranchers and fail to meaningfully lower prices for consumers, which would be close to the worst possible outcome for everyone except the packers positioned to capture the spread. I want to be honest that this is closer to informed speculation than settled fact right now, since the tariff pause is only a couple of weeks old as of this writing and the price data to actually test the claim doesn't exist yet.

The Labeling Promise Has Already Died Once, the Same Way

This is the piece of context I think matters most for readers trying to figure out how seriously to take the 90 day MCOOL review, because it isn't the first time. Mandatory country of origin labeling for beef existed once before, and its entire death spiral is a matter of public record. Canada and Mexico challenged it at the World Trade Organization starting in two thousand nine. The WTO ruled against the U.S. program in twenty eleven, the U.S. lost its appeal in twenty twelve, and after the U.S. tried a revised version, the WTO ruled against that too in twenty fourteen and again on final appeal in May twenty fifteen, finding the label violated U.S. trade obligations by discriminating against foreign livestock. Canada and Mexico then got WTO authorization to impose retaliatory tariffs, with Canada explicitly threatening a one hundred percent tariff on U.S. beef specifically, among roughly three billion dollars in tariffs they'd sought. The WTO authorized just over one billion dollars in actual retaliatory tariffs in December twenty fifteen. Congress repealed mandatory country of origin labeling for beef and pork eleven days later, essentially to make the WTO authorized retaliation go away before it started.

So the honest framing for the current 90 day review isn't "will the administration bring back a popular consumer protection," it's "the last time the U.S. tried exactly this, it survived about six years of litigation before dying to preserve a trade relationship, and nothing about the WTO's underlying reasoning or Canada and Mexico's underlying incentives has changed. Any new mandatory labeling regime would face the identical legal vulnerability unless it's designed very differently from the twenty fifteen version. And the executive order's own language, directing a review of "existing authorities" rather than committing to new legislation, reads to me like language written by lawyers who already know how this story ends and are managing expectations accordingly. That's my inference, not something any source stated outright, so I'd flag it as analysis rather than reporting if it makes it into the Deep Dive.

What I Think Happens Next, Clearly Labeled As Guessing

A few things I'd genuinely watch for, none of which I have any special certainty about:

The 90 day tariff pause runs out right around early December. My guess is the administration extends it, possibly indefinitely, rather than letting tariffs snap back into a beef market that still won't have meaningfully more domestic cattle by then. Politically, reimposing a tariff right before the holidays, into a market where prices are already the story, seems like the harder path, not the easier one.

The MCOOL review will very likely produce something, since ninety days of total silence after this much rancher anger would be its own political problem. My real guess is it lands on either a narrower, more legally defensible version than the twenty fifteen law, something that tries to thread the WTO needle by being less obviously discriminatory on its face, or a voluntary-plus program with financial incentives for packers who label rather than a true mandate. A full mandatory law that survives WTO scrutiny on the first try would be the surprise outcome, not the expected one.

The JBS story is the one I think has the most room to grow before this settles. Congressional Democrats, and possibly some of the Republican senators already on record criticizing the tariff pause, have an obvious incentive to formally request documents about the Batista meeting. And if the DOJ's existing antitrust investigation into JBS turns up anything that intersects with this decision, even tangentially, that's the kind of story that jumps from agricultural trade press into general political coverage fast. I'd put real odds on some kind of formal inquiry or document request surfacing within the next month or two, though I have no specific evidence one is already in motion, this is genuinely a guess based on how these stories have played out before.

On prices: I'd expect modest, real but underwhelming relief at best, arriving slowly, with packers and large retailers capturing a disproportionate share of the savings relative to what reaches a receipt. If prices don't move much at all by late autumn, expect the administration to point to the domestic processing order, the one about state inspection expansion, as evidence it's addressing supply from multiple angles, since that order is easier to defend on the merits than the imports have turned out to be.

And there's a wildcard worth naming even though I think it's a genuine long shot: if Brazil's EU suspension drags on and expands, or if any documented safety incident actually ties back to this specific six sixty-one million pound program rather than to an unrelated recall from weeks earlier. The politics here could flip fast, from "administration accused of favoring foreign billionaires over American ranchers" to "administration accused of importing unsafe meat," and either framing is damaging in a different way. Right now the safety concerns are real but circumstantial rather than proven. That could change with a single bad test result, and if it does, this stops being primarily a trade and labeling story and becomes primarily a food safety story, which is a different piece entirely.

What I'd Actually Lead the Deep Dive With

My instinct, and this is the one place I'll offer an actual editorial opinion rather than just research, is that the Deep Dive should not lead with pink slime at all, even though that's the hook that got us here and it's a legitimate one. It should lead with the Oval Office meeting, because that's the fact that changes how a reader interprets everything else in the piece. Once you know a Brazilian billionaire with an active DOJ antitrust cloud over his company met privately with the president the day before a tariff number matching almost exactly what they reportedly discussed got announced publicly, the herd shortage, the pink slime framing, the labeling gap. And the safety questions all read differently, less like an administration groping for any tool to fight grocery prices, and more like a specific, identifiable interest getting exactly what it asked for with a consumer price narrative built around it afterward. I don't think that reading is proven. I think it's the strongest one the evidence currently supports, and I think a Deep Dive that buries it in paragraph six instead of leading with it would be underselling its own reporting.
