What to Say When Your Mother-in-Law Sends You Blood Wars.
Written August 30th, twenty twenty-six.

Here's the shape of what just happened, as I understand it: you told your mother-in-law about Wetwear, your biology show, and she heard "biology" and handed you back a mystery school that teaches people to read their DNA through Kabbalah and calls itself an authority on Dragon bloodlines. That's not a coincidence of timing. That's how the association worked in her head — you said "biology," and the nearest thing she had filed under that word came back out. It's worth sitting with that for a second before deciding what to do about it, because it changes the problem. This isn't really about blood types or haplogroups. It's about the fact that two people who love each other are pointing at the same word and picturing completely different rooms.

The temptation, if you've spent any time actually looking at what Tribal Knowledge Academy teaches, is to open with the receipts. The founder says he's the Serpent's envoy, waited three thousand three hundred years since Sumer, charges seventy-seven dollars an hour, sells a book called The Blood Wars. All of that is true, and none of it is a good opening line at Thanksgiving. Leading with the worst quote you found is a debate tactic, not a conversation, and it tends to do exactly one thing: it makes the other person defend the whole rather than reconsider the part. Nobody has ever been talked out of a belief by being handed the most embarrassing sentence associated with it.

What actually works, if you want it to go anywhere, is closer to what you already do for a living. Wetwear exists because "biology, corrected" is a real and useful thing to offer people — not by telling them they're stupid for believing something, but by being a source they trust enough to come back to when the next thing crosses their feed. That's the same posture that works here, just smaller and more personal. Something like: "That's interesting, I hadn't heard of them — where'd you come across it?" costs you nothing and tells you a lot. Maybe she saw a clip. Maybe a friend swears by it. Maybe she genuinely thought it sounded adjacent to what you do and wanted to share something with you, which, underneath the reptilians and the Saturn cult, is actually a kind of gift — she was thinking of you.

There's a difference worth holding onto between "my mother-in-law has an idea I think is wrong" and "my mother-in-law is about to spend real money on it. The first doesn't need fixing. People believe all kinds of things that never cost them anything, and a relationship can absorb a lot of disagreement about Dragon bloodlines with no damage at all. The second is where I'd actually say something, gently and specifically — not "that whole site is nonsense," but "just so you know, the consultations are seventy-seven dollars an hour, and I couldn't find any real credentials behind the genetics claims, so I'd be careful before paying for anything. That's not an attack on her judgment. That's the same instinct that makes you fact-check a headline before it goes in your own show.

And if she pushes back, or if this turns out to matter to her more than you expected, you don't have to win it in one conversation. You can just be the person who's always calm and specific and curious about this stuff, the same way you are on the show, and let that be worth something over time. That's a longer game than being right today. It's also the only version of this where you're still on speaking terms with her at the next holiday, still getting handed the next weird link she finds — which, annoying as it is, means she's still including you. That's worth more than winning an argument about blood types.
