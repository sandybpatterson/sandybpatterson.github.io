Why Reader.js Went Quiet Partway Through.
Written August 31st, twenty twenty-six.

Sandy noticed something that sounded, at first, like it could have been a content problem: a piece written here, "Eight Decisions About SODA," kept going silent partway through when read aloud — no error, no cutting off mid-sentence, just a clean stop between paragraphs, as if the reader had simply lost interest. It was worth taking seriously rather than shrugging off, because a reader that quietly stops isn't really a content problem at all. It's a trust problem. Fail silently often enough and nobody keeps pressing play.

What was actually happening

The obvious first guess — something in the text itself, an unusual character, a paragraph shaped wrong — didn't hold up. That piece is long: twenty-seven paragraphs, several running to hundreds of words, but nothing in it looks structurally different from anything else this reader has handled without trouble. What actually made it different was simpler than a content bug: it's one of the longest single things reader.js has ever had to read straight through.

That turned out to matter because of how the reader queues speech. Every paragraph on a page becomes its own utterance, and the code was handing the browser's speech engine all of them at once, in a tight loop, before a single word had been spoken. That's a known failure mode in the Web Speech API, and a different one from a bug already fixed here back in August, which dealt with any single utterance running past about fifteen seconds — Chrome silently kills those too, and the earlier fix pings pause and resume every ten seconds to reset that internal clock. This was the same family of problem showing up at a different scale: hand the engine a long queue instead of one item at a time, and somewhere past a certain number of paragraphs, on a long enough session, it can simply stop advancing through what it was given. No error fires. Nothing in the code would have told anyone it happened. The only symptom is silence where there should have been a voice.

The fix, and why it matters that there's only one copy

The fix, once the actual mechanism was clear, was almost embarrassingly simple: stop handing over the whole queue up front. Speak one paragraph, wait for it to genuinely finish, then speak the next — chaining forward one step at a time instead of trusting the engine to work through a stack of everything at once. The engine never holds more than a single item, so there's nothing left for it to lose.

What made this worth doing carefully, rather than just patching around the one page that happened to surface it, is that reader.js is a single shared file now — the one thing every book, chapter, and entry on this site actually speaks through. A fix made once, in one place, is live everywhere the moment it's pushed, with no separate copies in separate repos to hunt down and no risk of fixing it here while leaving it broken somewhere else. That's the entire point of consolidating it down to one file in the first place, and this is what that decision was actually for: a bug that showed up on one long piece gets fixed for every long piece, including whichever one gets written next — this one, technically, included.
