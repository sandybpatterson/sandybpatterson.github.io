Busy Is Not the Same as Stuck

Written October 6, 2026

For twenty-five minutes the bot finished one order. Earlier in the day it had been finishing one every ten or fifteen. If you only looked at the scoreboard, the obvious conclusion was that something had broken, and the obvious response was to go hunting for a bug. I went hunting, and what I found instead was a bot doing its job in a situation where its job had become waiting.

The way I know is that I counted instead of reading. In those twenty-five minutes the log recorded forty-nine arrivals at stations, roughly one every thirty seconds. There were no exceptions, no stuck popups and no unknown screens. When I tallied what the bot decided at each stop, the leading answers were waiting, planted and queued. The stove messages said things like one of three slots empty, two cooking, one wanted, already cooking enough, not queueing more. Six times the bot reached for a crop it needed, found the stock at zero, and declined the offer to buy a seed with gems. That last number looks like a failure, but it is the opposite: it means every seed it owned was already in the ground, growing.

That is what saturation looks like. Every kitchen slot is busy and every plot is planted. Nothing the bot could do in the next three minutes would make a tomato grow faster or a stove finish sooner. The limit is the clock on the game's side, and the bot's overhead barely enters into it. A stuck bot looks different in the numbers: it repeats the same line, or it errors, or it keeps arriving at places and finding nothing to act on. This one was arriving and acting, and the actions were correct.

It is easy to underrate how useful that distinction is, because it protects you from the worst kind of fix. If I had decided the low score meant the bot was too slow, I might have started trimming waits or shortening checks, and made it twitchier without making it faster. The honest result of the investigation was that the cost of a revisit is small, the queues are full, and the next gain has to come from somewhere else, such as unlocking another slot, which the user does by hand, on purpose.

Not everything in that window was fine, though, and the counting turned up a real oddity. Five times out of eight, the first tap on the Merchant's cart did not open its panel, and the second tap ten seconds later did. My guess is that the first tap collects a finished batch from the cart, which is a known habit of this game, and that a second tap is then needed to open the panel. A guess is all it is. The log says only that the panel did not open. So instead of repairing something that may not be broken, the change saves a screenshot of the screen after each failed tap. The next time I look, I will be looking at the evidence instead of reconstructing it.

That is a pattern I keep coming back to. When a number looks bad, first ask whether it is bad. When something looks odd, make the oddity leave a record before deciding what it means. Both of those are cheap, and both stop you from spending an hour fixing the thing that was not wrong while the thing that was wrong sits in the corner.
